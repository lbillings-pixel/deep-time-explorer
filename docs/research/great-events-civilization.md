# Dossier: "The rise of human civilization" (nine vignettes)

Research notes behind the site's texts and pictures, not text written for visitors. Compiled 14 November 2026.

## How to read the verification marks

Every reference below was checked this session against Crossref, Open Library, Europe PMC / OpenAlex, or by fetching the publisher or institution page. Wikipedia was used for no citation.

- **[V]** The reference exists (record checked) **and** the specific claim was read this session in its abstract, open full text, or the institution's page.
- **[R]** The reference exists (record checked), but the full text is paywalled and was **not re-read this session**. The claim attached to it is what the work is generally cited for; the editor should confirm the page before relying on it.
- **UNVERIFIED** I could not confirm the reference or the claim. Do not use without checking.

Dates: "BC" throughout; "cal BP" = calibrated years before present. "Conventional" means the usual textbook date, which may be uncertain by decades.

A general rule for the illustrator that applies to all nine: the standing ruins and the museum objects are evidence; almost everything above wall height (roofs, upper storeys, paint, plantings), and almost everything about ordinary clothing and its colours, is reconstruction. Each section says where the line falls.

## 1. Paleolithic: cave painting by firelight

**Recommended site and date: Lascaux (Dordogne, France), about 21,000 years ago.** Represent the range c. 21,500–21,000 cal BP (the newest dating), acknowledging older published figures of c. 23,500–22,000 cal BP and the long-quoted "17,000 years" (an uncalibrated radiocarbon age).

Why Lascaux rather than Chauvet: the vignette's subject is painting *by firelight*, and Lascaux is where the lighting kit is actually attested: "over a hundred lamps", including the carved red sandstone lamp, plus hearths set up solely for light [V, French Ministry of Culture]. Chauvet is better dated (direct dates on the charcoal drawings; two phases of human use, 37,000–33,500 and 31,000–28,000 years ago [V, Quiles et al. 2016]) and is the fair alternative if the editor wants the oldest securely dated European painted cave. The choice changes the picture: Chauvet is mostly black charcoal and red drawings of lions, rhinoceroses, mammoths and bears, some 15,000 years older; Lascaux is polychrome aurochs, horses and deer. Do not blend them.

**Structures.** None. A natural limestone cave. The painters did not live in the dark zone: the Lascaux hearths have no associated flint or bone working debris and are interpreted as lighting [V]. Possible wooden scaffolding in the Axial Gallery was claimed by André Glory from sockets in the wall; the Ministry's own page says scaffolding "was indeed used to work on certain pieces" but that extending the idea is doubtful, and figures sit 2.5–3.5 m up [V]. A simple pole platform or a person standing on a ledge is defensible; an elaborate scaffold is not.

**Objects and tools (attested at Lascaux).**
- Stone lamps: mostly rough limestone slabs with a hollow for fuel; one "completely crafted and polished" red sandstone lamp [V]. Fuel: animal fat is established for Palaeolithic lamps generally (de Beaune 1987 [R]; Medina-Alcaide et al. 2021 [V] tested fat lamps, torches and hearths experimentally). The Ministry page states resinous torches are evidenced in some caves "although not at Lascaux" [V]: so lamps and small hearths, not flaming torches, for this site.
- Flint blades, bladelets and flakes (350+ pieces), some with wear from engraving [V].
- Long antler/bone spear points ("assegais"), decorated and plain, one nearly 45 cm [V].
- Pierced shells used as ornaments [V].
- Pigments: iron oxides (reds, yellows) and manganese oxides (blacks) (Chalmin, Menu & Vignaud 2003 [R]). Lascaux blacks are mineral, not charcoal, which is why the paintings themselves are not directly radiocarbon dated.

**Crops.** None. No cultivated plant of any kind may appear.

**Domestic animals.** None. **Trap: no dog.** The earliest widely accepted domestic dog is Bonn-Oberkassel, Germany, about 14,000 years ago (Janssens et al. 2018 [R]); genomes show dogs had a deep Palaeolithic history but do not date the origin (Bergström et al. 2020 [V]). A dog at 21,000 years ago in the Dordogne is not defensible. Wild animals of the art are fine on the wall (aurochs, horse, red deer, ibex). Note that reindeer dominate the food bones at Lascaux [V] yet are almost absent from the walls: the art is not a menu.

**People.** Almost nothing is directly known. Known: pierced-shell ornaments at the site [V]; eyed needles and therefore sewn clothing exist in the European Upper Palaeolithic (d'Errico et al. 2018 [R]); elaborate beaded garments are evidenced at Sunghir, Russia, c. 34,000 years ago (Trinkaus & Buzhilova 2018 [R]), but that is a different time and place and should not be copied. Ancient DNA suggests most Palaeolithic Europeans had dark or intermediate skin; lighter pigmentation spread slowly and late (Perretti et al. 2025 [V]). Hand-stencil measurements from French and Spanish caves suggest many makers were women (Snow 2013 [V]; sample of 32, contested; no hand stencils at Lascaux).
**Avoid:** "cavemen" (clubs, ragged pelts, hunched posture); an all-male group; people living in the deep cave; a shaman in costume presented as fact (the "ritual/shamanic" reading is one interpretation); pale northern-European skin as a default; any children's-book mammoth hunt.

**Cautions.**
- Lascaux dating is weak and contested: the Ministry's own pages give 21,500–21,000 (LAsCO project), 23,500–22,000 (1998/2002 AMS on antler) and older estimates, and its FAQ still attributes the art to the Solutrean [V]; Ducasse & Langlais 2019 [V] is the current reassessment. Dates come from objects on the floor, not the paintings.
- European cave art is not the oldest: narrative cave painting on Sulawesi is at least 51,200 years old (Oktaviana et al. 2024 [V]). A 2026 claim of a hand stencil of at least 67,800 years on Sulawesi exists in press coverage by the same team; I could not locate the primary paper's record: **UNVERIFIED**.
- "Paleolithic" covers 3 million years; this vignette is the last sliver of it.

## 2. Neolithic revolution: first fields and a village

**Recommended site and date: Çatalhöyük East Mound (Konya plain, Türkiye), painted at about 6700–6500 BC**, within an occupation of roughly 7100–5950 BC. UNESCO gives 7400–6200 BC for the East mound [V]; the Bayesian redating that moved the start to c. 7100 BC is Bayliss et al. 2015 [R].

Why: it is the most intensively excavated and published early farming settlement, with a 1,500-year crop sequence and a very large animal-bone dataset, and its houses are well enough preserved to draw. **But it is not "first fields."** Cultivation of wild cereals began in the Levant and Upper Euphrates more than 2,000 years earlier, and domesticated-type cereals appear there around 10,700–10,200 years ago (Arranz-Otaegui et al. 2016 [V]). If the caption says "first," the honest alternatives are Abu Hureyra or Jericho, which are far harder to draw defensibly. Suggest the label speak of "early farmers" rather than "the first."

**Structures.**
- Tightly packed rectangular mudbrick houses, back to back, **no streets, entry through the roof** by ladder; movement was across the roofs [V, UNESCO]. Flat roofs of timber, reeds and mud are a reconstruction from collapsed roof material and the absence of doors [R, Hodder 2014].
- Interiors: white-plastered walls and floors, re-plastered many times; raised platforms; an oven and hearth under the roof hatch; some walls with red-painted geometric and figurative panels, and plastered wild-bull horn installations [V for paintings/reliefs, UNESCO; details R, Hodder 2014]. Colour of exteriors: mud-brown; white plaster outside is not established.
- Houses are of similar size; no public buildings, temples or palaces have been identified [V, UNESCO criterion iv; R, Hodder 2014].
- Setting: a low mound in a seasonally wet alluvial plain; where the fields lay is debated.

**Objects and tools.** Obsidian blades, points and polished obsidian mirrors; bone tools (awls, needles, spatulas); baskets and wooden vessels; grinding stones; clay balls and small clay and stone figurines (Nakamura & Meskell 2009 [R]); simple pottery (rare and plain early in the sequence, commoner later) [R, Hodder 2014]. Woven textiles are attested and are made of local tree-bast fibre, **not wool and not linen** (Rast-Eicher et al. 2021 [R]). No metal tools (a few hammered copper beads only), no wheel, no plough.

**Crops.** Emmer wheat and "new type" glume wheat are illustrated as stored crops in the site report [V, figure captions of Bogaard et al. 2017]; the same paper reports a diverse initial crop spectrum that was repeatedly adjusted over 1,500 years [V, abstract]. The fuller list for which that paper is cited is: einkorn, emmer, "new type" glume wheat, free-threshing wheat, naked barley, pea, lentil, bitter vetch and chickpea [R: I could not open the full text; the editor should confirm the list in the paper's tables]. **Traps:** no rye, no oats, no millet, no rice, no maize; no olive, vine or fig orchards; fields were small hand-tilled plots, not ox-ploughed.

**Domestic animals.**
- Sheep and goat: domestic from the start of the site; this is the first appearance of domestic caprines in Türkiye outside the Fertile Crescent [V, Arbuckle et al. 2014].
- **Cattle: wild (aurochs) for most of the sequence.** Small domestic cattle appear only in the later levels, mid to late seventh millennium BC [V, Arbuckle et al. 2014; Russell et al. 2005 R]. At the recommended date, show aurochs only as hunted wild animals or as horn installations, not as herd or draught animals.
- **Pigs: not domestic** in Neolithic central Türkiye; wild boar are rare [V, Arbuckle et al. 2014].
- Dog: present [R, Russell et al. 2005].
- No horses, donkeys, chickens or cats.

**People.** Direct evidence is thin: figurines (many of ambiguous sex), wall paintings showing small human figures, some apparently in animal-skin garments, bast-fibre cloth, and beads of stone, bone and shell in burials. Skeletal and isotope studies are cited for broadly similar diet and workload in women and men [R, Hodder 2014]. The dead were buried beneath house platforms.
**Avoid:** a "Mother Goddess" cult, priestesses or shrines presented as fact (James Mellaart's 1960s reading is not accepted by the current project); a matriarchy or a patriarchy; showing burials or plastered skulls as spectacle; woolly sheep and wool clothing (woolly fleece is a later development); a man-with-plough, woman-with-baby division of labour.

**Cautions.** "Neolithic revolution" is a 1930s phrase for what is now seen as a slow, multi-regional process over millennia (Arranz-Otaegui et al. 2016 [V]; Zeder 2011 [R]). Whether Çatalhöyük is a "village", "town" or "city" is argued; UNESCO's own text calls it both a village and an urban centre and describes it as "largely egalitarian" [V], a claim that is itself debated. Monument building preceded farming at Göbekli Tepe, 9600–8200 BC, built by hunter-gatherers [V, UNESCO], which undercuts a simple "farming then civilization" story. Farming also began independently elsewhere (see final section).

## 3. Sumer

**Recommended site and date: Ur (Tell al-Muqayyar, southern Iraq), painted at about 2100–2000 BC** (Third Dynasty of Ur; conventional Middle Chronology 2112–2004 BC).

Why: the ziggurat that everyone expects in a Sumer picture is a real, dated building here, and its lowest stage survives. **The decision the editor must make** is between two Urs that are 400–500 years apart and must not be mixed: (a) Ur III, c. 2100 BC, with the ziggurat; or (b) the Royal Cemetery, c. 2600–2450 BC, source of the Standard of Ur, the lyres and Puabi's headdress, when the ziggurat in its known form did not yet exist. I recommend (a). Uruk, c. 3300–3000 BC, is the alternative if the theme is "the first city" and the first writing.

**Structures.**
- Ziggurat of the moon god Nanna, built under Ur-Nammu and Shulgi: solid mudbrick core, skin of baked brick set in bitumen, three converging stairways on one face. **Only the lowest stage and part of the second survive. The upper stages and the temple on top are Leonard Woolley's reconstruction** (Woolley 1939 [R]); their number, height and colour are conjecture, as are the trees Woolley imagined on the terraces. The crisp lower facade seen in modern photographs is a 1960s rebuild in baked brick and cement [V, UNESCO]. Draw the upper part soft, distant or under construction.
- City of mudbrick: flat-roofed courtyard houses and narrow lanes. The best-known excavated houses at Ur are slightly later (early second millennium BC), so house detail is by analogy [R, Crawford 2015].
- Canals, levees, date-palm gardens and a harbour; the city stood beside marshes [V, UNESCO]. Reed architecture is appropriate at the water's edge.

**Objects and tools.** Clay tablets with cuneiform and a reed stylus; cylinder seals; baked bricks stamped with the king's name; pottery; copper and bronze tools and weapons (**no iron**); reed-and-bitumen boats; the ox-drawn seeder plough [R, Postgate]. Solid-wheeled wagons are attested (four-wheeled, on the Standard of Ur, c. 2600 BC [V, Bennett et al. 2022]); **spoked-wheel chariots are not** for this date.

**Crops.** Barley above all (barley plots are the stuff of the tablets [V, example in Bennett et al. 2022]); emmer wheat; date palm; flax; onions, garlic, lentils, chickpeas [R, Postgate; Ellison et al. 1978 for food remains from Ur]. No rice, no cotton, no citrus, no maize.

**Domestic animals.** Sheep (the wool economy), goats, cattle (plough oxen), pigs, donkeys, dogs [R, Postgate]. Equids need care:
- **Kungas**, bred hybrids of female donkey and male Syrian wild ass, pulled the wagons of the mid third millennium and are probably the teams on the Standard of Ur [V, Bennett et al. 2022].
- **Horses:** first mentioned in cuneiform in exactly the Ur III period, as a rarity ("equids of the mountain"); domestic horses reached Mesopotamia only at the end of the third millennium [V, Bennett et al. 2022; Librado et al. 2021 V]. No ridden horses, no horse chariots. Safest: donkeys only.
- **No camels. No chickens** (not in Mesopotamia until the late second millennium BC [V, Peters et al. 2022]).

**People.** From the culture's own art: in the Ur III period, men are shown clean-shaven with shaved heads or a brimmed cap, in a long fringed robe leaving one shoulder bare; women in long draped robes with hair bound up by a band; labourers in short kilts [R, Crawford 2015; Zettler & Horne 1998 for the earlier material]. Earlier (Royal Cemetery) art shows tufted fleece-like skirts and bare chests; that is the other Ur. Textile workshops staffed largely by women and children, and labour gangs paid in barley rations, are documented in the tablets [R, Postgate].
**Avoid:** Assyrian or Babylonian imagery a thousand or more years later (long curled beards, winged bulls, the Ishtar Gate's blue glaze, "hanging gardens"); turbans and generic "Arabian Nights" dress; camels; slave-drivers with whips; any image of the god in the summit temple.

**Cautions.** "Sumer" is a language-and-region label, not an ethnicity; Sumerian and Akkadian speakers lived together, and Ur III kings ruled both. "Cradle of civilization" in the singular is a framing many archaeologists now avoid. Absolute dates before c. 1500 BC float by decades between competing chronologies. The ziggurat's popular image is mostly Woolley. The site is in a country whose heritage has suffered recent war damage [V, UNESCO]; treat it as a living heritage site.

## 4. Egypt

**Recommended site and date: Giza in the Fourth Dynasty, about 2600–2500 BC**, anchored on the excavated workers' town (Heit el-Ghurab) south of the Sphinx.

Why: the pyramids are the image, and Giza uniquely has the builders' town, bakeries and food remains to make the foreground defensible. Radiocarbon modelling puts the start of Djoser's reign at 2691–2625 BC, earlier than some historical estimates, which constrains the Fourth Dynasty that follows [V, Bronk Ramsey et al. 2010]. Khufu's reign is conventionally c. 2589–2566 BC.
**Trap in the date itself:** the three pyramids were built in sequence over about three generations. A scene of Khufu's pyramid under construction can have **no second or third pyramid and no Sphinx**. A scene with all three finished is c. 2500 BC or later, with no construction under way on them.

**Structures.**
- Pyramid: core of locally quarried limestone blocks; outer casing of fine white Tura limestone, dressed smooth [R, Lehner 1997]. Almost all casing is gone; some survives at Khufu's base and near the top of Khafre's. A smooth, pale, sharp-edged pyramid is well founded. **The capstone is unknown:** a gilded tip is conjecture.
- Lower courses of red granite on Menkaure's (and a band on Khafre's) [R, Lehner 1997].
- Construction ramps: their form is not known and is disputed; show little.
- Workers' town: long mudbrick-and-fieldstone galleries, bakeries (two found in 1991, then the oldest known), and bell-shaped bread moulds littering the site "in the hundreds of thousands" [V, AERA].
- Nile, harbour basins and cargo boats: the Wadi al-Jarf papyri (the "diary of Merer") record boat crews carrying Tura limestone to Giza under Khufu [R, Tallet & Lehner 2021].

**Objects and tools.** Copper chisels and saws (**no bronze to speak of, no iron**); hard stone pounders; wooden sledges, levers and rollers; rope; large pottery bread moulds and beer jars [V, AERA for moulds]; grinding stones; papyrus and reed pens. **No pulleys, no wheeled transport** on the building site: loads moved by sledge and boat [R, Lehner 1997].

**Crops.** "Emmer and barley were clearly the staple cereals"; bread wheat was not in general use until Greek times [V, AERA]. Also flax (linen), lentils, onions and garlic, figs and grapes [R, Murray 2005]. No rice, sugar cane, cotton, maize, citrus or bananas, which are all later arrivals.

**Domestic animals.** Cattle, sheep and goats supplied the meat for the builders, with pigs eaten in the less formal part of the town, and much fish [R, Redding 2024; Murray 2005; the published herd-size estimates were seen only in press reports]. Donkeys were the pack animal; dogs are present [R]. **Traps:**
- **No camels.** The dromedary was domesticated in south-east Arabia and has been a transport animal for about 3,000 years [V, Almathen et al. 2016]; in the southern Levant it appears in the late tenth century BC (Sapir-Hen & Ben-Yosef 2013 [R]). The tourist camel at the pyramids is 1,500 years too early.
- **No horses, no chariots:** domestic horses spread from the steppe only from about 2000 BC [V, Librado et al. 2021] and reached Egypt later still.
- **No chickens:** not identified in Egypt until the Persian period, c. 550–330 BC [V, Peters et al. 2022].
- Cats: taming is evidenced earlier (Van Neer et al. 2014 [R]) but the household cat of Egyptian art is later; leave out. No water buffalo.

**People.** Old Kingdom statues and tomb reliefs are a rich, direct source: men in short wrapped white linen kilts, usually bare-chested, hair cropped or under a short wig; women in a long close-fitting sheath dress with shoulder straps; labourers in a loincloth or belt; broad bead collars on the well-off [R, Lehner 1997]. The red-brown men and yellow women of Egyptian painting are an artistic convention, not a record of skin colour.
**Avoid:** slaves under the lash (the workforce was organised, housed and fed by the state; buried in their own cemetery beside the pyramids [R, Lehner 1997]); "Hebrew slaves" (a thousand-year anachronism); a pharaoh in striped headcloth supervising on site; New Kingdom regalia (blue war crown, Tutankhamun-style gold, Nefertiti); animal-headed gods walking about; any hint of lost technology.

**Cautions.** Absolute dates are uncertain by several decades. How the stones were raised is unresolved. Workforce size is an estimate. "Egypt" in this vignette is one moment of a 3,000-year history, and Old Kingdom Egypt had long-standing neighbours in Nubia who are absent from the nine.

## 5. The Indus cities

**Recommended site and date: Mohenjo-daro (Sindh, Pakistan), painted at about 2300 BC**, within the urban ("Mature Harappan") phase of c. 2600–1900 BC.

Why: the best-preserved Indus city, with standing baked-brick streets, drains and the Great Bath. A warning about the evidence: Mohenjo-daro was dug mostly in the 1920s–30s without modern recovery of seeds and bones, so its **plant and animal list leans on better-sampled Indus sites** (Harappa and others). I flag where that matters.

**Structures.**
- Streets on a rough grid, houses of reddish **baked brick** around courtyards, with brick-lined wells (some 700 estimated) and covered street drains [V, Jansen 1989; UNESCO]. Walls survive to well above head height; upper storeys, flat timber roofs, doors and any wall colour are reconstruction.
- The "Great Bath": a sunken tank about 12 × 7 m and 2.4 m deep, with stairs at each end, brick colonnades around it; "most scholars agree" it served ritual bathing, but that is inference [V, Kenoyer, harappa.com].
- The so-called "granary" beside it is Mortimer Wheeler's label; the building's function is not established and no grain was found [R, Kenoyer 1998; Possehl 2002]. Do not show it stuffed with grain.
- **Trap: the tall round tower that crowns the mound in every photograph is a Buddhist stupa of about AD 200**, built of unbaked brick on the ruins some 2,000 years later [V, harappa.com; UNESCO]. It must not appear.

**Objects and tools.** Square steatite stamp seals with an animal (most often a one-horned "unicorn" bull) and a line of script; cubical chert weights in a standard series; long carnelian and other stone beads; copper and bronze tools and vessels; red pottery painted in black; terracotta figurines; terracotta model carts with solid wheels, so **ox-carts are legitimate here** [R, Kenoyer 1998]. No iron.

**Crops.** In the Indus plains the staples were winter-sown wheat and barley, with pulses (peas, lentils, chickpea), sesame, dates and cotton; summer crops (millets, and rice in the north-east of the Indus world) were important in other regions and increasingly through time [V abstracts: Weber 1999; Petrie et al. 2017; Bates et al. 2017 for rice in northern India]. Cotton is very old in the region (Moulherat et al. 2002 [R]). For Mohenjo-daro specifically: wheat, barley and cotton are the safe trio; rice paddies are not.

**Domestic animals.** Humped zebu cattle (the main animal, and the commonest draught animal), water buffalo, sheep, goats, dogs [R, Kenoyer 1998; Possehl 2002]. **Traps:**
- **No chickens.** The old claim that the chicken was domesticated in the Indus valley rested on a handful of bones from Mohenjo-daro and Harappa that have been re-examined and rejected; poultry keeping in South Asia is "a post-Harappan development" [V, Peters et al. 2022].
- **No horses.** Claimed horse bones (Surkotada) are disputed (Meadow 1997 [R]), and domestic horses spread across Eurasia only from c. 2000 BC [V, Librado et al. 2021]. No horse appears on any Indus seal.
- No camels at this date; donkeys doubtful. Elephants are shown on seals, but whether they were tamed is unknown: keep any elephant wild and distant, or leave it out.

**People.** From the culture's own art: the small steatite bust nicknamed the "Priest-King" (17.5 cm high) shows a man with combed-back hair, a short beard with shaved upper lip, a headband with a circular ornament, an armband, and a cloak over the left shoulder patterned with trefoils once filled with red [V, Kenoyer, harappa.com]. The bronze "Dancing Girl" and many terracotta figurines show stacked bangles, necklaces and, on women, elaborate fan-shaped headdresses [R, Kenoyer 1998]. Cotton cloth is likely. Unknown: garment colours, everyday dress, and who held authority.
**Avoid:** treating the "Priest-King" as a priest or a king (the name is a modern invention; there is no evidence that priests or monarchs ruled); thrones, palaces, temples, armies; later Hindu, Buddhist or Vedic imagery read backwards (including the "proto-Shiva" reading of one seal); the swastika motif (it does occur on Indus seals, but will be misread); saris, turbans and bindis as costume shorthand; any "Aryan versus Dravidian" racial contrast.

**Cautions.** The script is undeciphered and the language unknown. How the cities were governed is openly disputed: no palaces, royal tombs or ruler portraits have been found, and one current view is that this was a complex society without a ruling class (Green 2021 [V]). "Indus", "Harappan" and "Indus–Sarasvati" are not neutral names in South Asian politics; "Indus civilization" is the usual scholarly term. The old "Aryan invasion" explanation of the end is abandoned; climate and river change are discussed (Petrie et al. 2017 [V]). UNESCO's own page carries dated language ("college of priests", "granary", "flourished 2500–1500 BC", and a short description saying "unbaked brick" where the synthesis says baked) [V]: do not copy it.

## 6. Early China

**Recommended site and date: Anyang (Yinxu, Henan), the last Shang capital, painted at about 1200 BC**, within c. 1250–1046 BC. UNESCO gives 1300–1046 BC [V]; the inscribed record begins around the reign of Wu Ding, conventionally c. 1250 BC.

Why Anyang over Erlitou: Anyang has writing (oracle-bone inscriptions), a securely identified dynasty, a World Heritage listing, and large modern studies of its bones and workshops. Erlitou (c. 1850–1600 BC [V, Campbell 2014 chapter title]) is earlier, but its identification with the "Xia dynasty" of later texts is contested, which would put a dispute at the centre of the vignette.

**Structures.**
- Palace and ancestral-shrine area: more than 80 foundations of pounded (rammed) earth carrying timber-post buildings [V, UNESCO]. Only platforms and post-holes survive. Everything above the platform, thatched roofs, wattle-and-daub or earth walls, is reconstruction (Childs-Johnson 2020 [V abstract]). **No ceramic roof tiles, no upturned eaves, no painted red columns and brackets**: that is the architecture of much later periods.
- Royal tombs: huge cross-shaped shaft pits with ramps, across the river; accompanied by sacrificial pits with chariots and human victims [V, UNESCO]. Below ground, and see "avoid".
- Workshop quarters for bronze casting, bone working and pottery, on an industrial scale (Campbell et al. 2011 [V]).

**Objects and tools.** Cast bronze ritual vessels and weapons. **Colour trap: new bronze is golden to coppery; the green of museum pieces is 3,000 years of corrosion.** Inscribed oracle bones (cattle shoulder blades and turtle shells) [V, UNESCO]; carved jade; bone hairpins, awls and arrowheads in vast numbers [V, Campbell et al. 2011]; two-horse chariots with spoked wheels [V, UNESCO; Shaughnessy 1988 R]. Farm tools were stone, bone, shell and wood, not bronze [R, Liu & Chen 2012]. No iron, no coins, no paper.

**Crops.** North China dry farming: **foxtail millet** was the principal crop for four millennia, broomcorn millet second; rice present but "apparently not an important local crop"; wheat became significant between 1600 and 1300 BC; legumes also grown [V, Lee et al. 2007: this is a survey of the Yiluo valley, a few hundred kilometres south-west of Anyang, running up to the early Shang (c. 1300 BC); it is the best open dataset but is neither Anyang itself nor quite the recommended date]. Hemp and silk (mulberry) are attested for cloth [R, Liu & Chen 2012; Campbell 2014]. **Trap: no flooded rice terraces, no tea, no cotton.**

**Domestic animals.** Pig, dog, cattle, sheep/goat and horse. Cattle and pig are named as the main bone supply of the Anyang workshops [V, Campbell et al. 2011]; chariot burials imply horses [V, UNESCO]; dog and sheep/goat, and the shift in which animals were sacrificed, rest on Yuan & Flad 2005 [R]. Details and traps:
- **Horses arrive with the chariot** around 1250–1200 BC; they are an elite novelty at Anyang, absent from earlier Shang sites [R, Shaughnessy 1988; Yuan & Flad 2005; consistent with Librado et al. 2021 V]. A chariot team is right; mounted riders are not.
- **Chickens are just defensible:** the earliest bones confidently attributed to domestic chickens in East Asia date to the late Shang; earlier claims of Neolithic Chinese chickens are rejected [V, Peters et al. 2022; Eda et al. 2016 R]. If shown, keep them few.
- **Water buffalo: wild, not domestic.** Buffalo bones at Shang sites belong to an extinct native wild species, not the ancestor of the domestic buffalo (Yang et al. 2008 [R]). No buffalo pulling a plough; indeed no animal-drawn plough at all at this date [R, Liu & Chen 2012].
- No camels, donkeys or cats.

**People.** Small jade and stone figures from the tomb of Fu Hao, a royal consort who also led armies, show kneeling people in patterned robes with sashes, with braided or coiled hair and headbands; bone hairpins are the commonest ornament [R, Campbell 2014; Li Feng 2013]. Elite silk, commoner hemp. Colours unknown.
**Avoid:** dress, hairstyles and buildings of imperial China (wide-sleeved silk robes, topknots under official caps, pagodas, tiled curved roofs, the Great Wall, paper lanterns, later-style dragons); conical hats and rice-paddy clichés; and **human sacrifice**, which is very well attested here but should not be depicted.

**Cautions.** The "Xia dynasty" and its link to Erlitou are disputed inside and outside China. The end date 1046 BC comes from the state-sponsored Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project and is not universally accepted. The Shang were one power among several Bronze Age societies in what is now China; "Early China" should not be read as an early version of the modern state. UNESCO's phrases "first site of a capital in Chinese history" and "earliest site to possess the elements of civilization" [V] are nomination language, and earlier walled centres (Zhengzhou, Erlitou) are well known.

## 7. Norte Chico (Caral-Supe), Peru

**Recommended site and date: Caral (Supe valley, 23 km inland), painted at about 2400–2200 BC.** Radiocarbon dates on short-lived reed fibre put monumental building and irrigation at Caral between about 2627 and 1977 BC [V, Shady, Haas & Creamer 2001]; the wider cultural period is 3000–1800 BC [V, Haas et al. 2004].

**This is the vignette with the most traps.** In short: no pottery, no metal, no loom-woven cloth, no herd animals, no llamas, and maize is disputed.

**Structures.**
- Six large stepped platform mounds ("pyramids") of stone and earth, the larger ones fronted by **sunken circular courts**; the site sits on a dry desert terrace above the green irrigated valley floor [V, UNESCO].
- Construction: stone retaining walls holding fill carried in *shicra*, open-mesh reed bags of stones; it is these reeds that were dated [V via reports of Shady et al. 2001; R for detail, Shady 2006].
- Finish: walls mud-plastered and painted. The excavating project reports coloured plaster; I could not open a primary source stating which colours, so pale earth tones are the safe choice and strong colour is **UNVERIFIED**.
- Housing of cane-and-mud (wattle) with some stone, in residential sectors [R, Shady 2006]. No defensive walls [V, Zona Arqueológica Caral].

**Objects and tools.** Cotton cord, fishing nets and twined textiles; bottle-gourd containers and net floats; reed baskets and *shicra*; stone tools; small unfired clay figurines [R, Shady 2006]. A set of 32 flutes made from pelican and condor wing bones, from the sunken court of the "Amphitheatre" building [reported consistently, including by a Metropolitan Museum essay seen in search results; I could not open a primary source: treat the count as **UNVERIFIED**]. A knotted-cord device identified as a quipu [V, UNESCO; its interpretation is the excavator's]. **No ceramics and no loom weaving** [V, Sandweiss et al. 2009]. No metal of any kind.

**Crops.** Cotton first: "fields of cotton with scant comestibles" [V, Sandweiss et al. 2009]. Food plants recovered at Caral: the tree fruits guava (*Psidium guajava*) and pacae (*Inga feuillei*), achira (a root crop), beans, squash, sweet potato, avocado and peanut [V, Sandweiss et al. 2009, co-authored by the site's director]. Bottle gourd and chili pepper are also reported [R, Shady 2006].
**Maize: present, importance disputed.** The excavator reports maize remains from Caral and neighbouring Miraya "in diverse contexts" but has not quantified them, and "it is not possible at this time to assess the importance of maize at these sites" [V, Haas et al. 2013, describing Shady's finds]. The strong case that maize was "a primary component of the diet" rests on pollen, starch and coprolites from **two other sites, Caballete and Huaricanga, in the Fortaleza valley, not from Caral** [V, Haas et al. 2013]. Recommendation: cotton, squash, beans and fruit trees in the foreground; maize, if included at all, small and marginal. No potatoes or quinoa shown as field crops (highland staples, not attested here), no tomatoes.

**Animals.** "Animal protein came entirely from the sea, not from domesticated or terrestrial animals" [V, Sandweiss et al. 2009]. The picture should show dried small fish (anchovies and sardines [V for anchovy, found in coprolites at the Fortaleza-valley sites, Haas et al. 2013; sardines and anchovies at Caral itself R, Shady et al. 2001, seen only in press summaries of that paper]) and shellfish (mussels and clams [V]) arriving from the coast, perhaps traded for cotton nets.
- **Llamas and alpacas: not attested as herd or pack animals at Caral.** I found no faunal study reporting them, and the statement above from the excavator's own co-authored paper excludes domestic animals as food. Popular accounts mention horn-like instruments of deer and llama bone, with inconsistent counts (37 or 38); I could not trace this to a primary source (**UNVERIFIED**), and worked bone would not show herding in any case. **No llamas in the picture.**
- Dog: domestic dog droppings are identified at Caballete and Huaricanga [V, Haas et al. 2013], so a dog is defensible for the region but is not site-attested at Caral.
- Guinea pigs: not confirmed; leave out. No horses, cattle, sheep, pigs or chickens (none in the Americas before 1492). No wheels in use, no iron.

**People.** Very little is established. The unfired clay figurines and a few burials are the evidence; cotton clothing is a fair inference; hairstyles and body paint should be kept generic. The project describes the society as trading widely and "without walls or weapons" [V, Zona Arqueológica Caral].
**Avoid:** anything Inca, which is some 4,000 years later (llama caravans, bright woven ponchos, knitted caps with earflaps, gold, fitted stone masonry, Machu Picchu silhouettes); pottery of any kind; feathered-headdress stereotypes; a priest-king with invented regalia; human sacrifice.

**Cautions.**
- **The name.** "Norte Chico" (a modern Peruvian regional nickname) is used by the US team of Haas and Creamer, who work in the valleys to the north; "Caral" or "Caral-Supe" is used by Ruth Shady, who has excavated Caral since the 1990s, by the Peruvian state, and by UNESCO ("Sacred City of Caral-Supe" [V]). The two teams fell out publicly over credit after their joint 2001 paper. Since the vignette is based on Caral, "Caral-Supe" is the more defensible label; if "Norte Chico" stays in the title it should be as the alternative, not the lead.
- "Oldest civilization in the Americas", "city" and especially "state" (UNESCO: "a fully developed socio-political state" [V]) are claims, not consensus.
- Fish or farming: whether the sea or irrigated crops underwrote these societies is a long-running argument [V, Haas et al. 2004 and 2013; Sandweiss et al. 2009].
- "Peaceful" is an inference from absence of evidence.
- Period names differ: "Late Archaic" or "Late Preceramic" in journals; "Formativo Inicial" on the Peruvian project's site [V].

## 8. The Olmec

**Recommended site and date: La Venta (Tabasco, Mexico), painted at about 600–500 BC.** La Venta grew into a major centre between 1150 and 800 BC and peaked between 800 and 500 BC [V, Rust & Sharer 1988]; the last building stage of its great mound is about 400 BC [V, INAH].

Why La Venta over San Lorenzo: San Lorenzo (flourishing roughly 1400–1000 BC) is the older capital and has more colossal heads, but it is essentially a modified plateau with little that reads as architecture. La Venta has a planned ceremonial core, the great earthen mound, and monuments found in place. If the editor prefers the earliest Olmec, San Lorenzo is the alternative, and the mound must then go.

**Structures.**
- The Great Pyramid (Building C-1): **an earthen mound, not a stone pyramid**; rammed clay and sand, 30 m high, on a platform [V, INAH]. Its original shape (a stepped, squared pyramid with a stair, or the rounded fluted cone it is today) is debated; a "possible central stairway" is all INAH claims [V]. Surface colour: bare clay. Draw it rounded and soft-edged.
- Complex A, north of the mound: low earthen platforms arranged symmetrically around two courts, the northern one fenced by a wall of upright natural basalt columns; a tomb built of the same columns; buried offerings of jade and of serpentine blocks [V, INAH]. The coloured clay floors and buried mosaic pavements described in the 1950s excavation reports are underground features [R, Pool 2007].
- All buildings at the site are rammed earth [V, INAH]. Ordinary houses were pole-and-thatch on low earth platforms [R, Pool 2007]: reconstruction.
- Monuments: colossal heads of basalt and andesite, three of them found in a row north of Complex A; flat-topped "altars" (now read as thrones) with a figure seated in a niche; carved stelae. The stone came from the Tuxtla mountains, jade from Guatemala, serpentine from Oaxaca [V, INAH].

**Objects and tools.** Polished jade and serpentine axes ("celts") and small standing figurines; pottery; obsidian blades; stone grinding slabs and handstones; polished iron-ore mirrors worn as pendants [R, Pool 2007]. Solid rubber balls, made from *Castilla* latex, are an Olmec-region invention [V, Hosler et al. 1999]. Dugout canoes are a fair inference for a site among rivers and swamps. **No metal tools, no wheel in use, no draught or pack animals, and no secure writing.**

**Crops.** Maize: cultivated on the Tabasco coast near La Venta from very early (Pope et al. 2001 [V]; Pohl et al. 2007 [V]), with cotton and (disputed) sunflower appearing around 2500 BC and a possible early manioc record [V, Pope et al. 2001]. Beans, squash and avocado are part of the regional farming set [R, VanDerwarker 2006]. Cacao was in use at San Lorenzo between 1800 and 1000 BC [V, Powis et al. 2011]. INAH describes alluvial soils giving up to three harvests a year [V]. How much the Olmec depended on maize, as against fish, turtles and garden and forest foods, varies by site and is debated (VanDerwarker & Kruger 2012 [R]). Show maize plots among much water and forest, not a monoculture. No wheat, rice, sugar cane, bananas, coffee or citrus.

**Animals.**
- **The dog is the only domestic animal.** "Domestic dogs probably lived on-site" at Olmec-area villages, and dog bones in household refuse suggest they were also eaten [R, VanDerwarker 2006: quotation seen in a secondary excerpt, pp. 125–131; Peres, VanDerwarker & Pool 2013 R].
- **Turkeys: no.** The earliest evidence for turkey rearing in Mesoamerica is from the Maya site of El Mirador, 300 BC–AD 100, and the wild range of the Mexican turkey did not reach south of central Mexico [V, Thornton et al. 2012]. A domestic turkey at La Venta in 600 BC is not defensible.
- Wild animals that were eaten, and may appear as catch: fish, turtles, deer, peccary, waterfowl [R, VanDerwarker 2006; Peres et al. 2013]. Jaguars, crocodilians, sharks and harpy eagles belong to Olmec art, not the farmyard.
- No horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, goats or chickens.

**People.** From Olmec sculpture: the colossal heads are individual portraits of men wearing close-fitting headgear with straps and personal emblems, and large ear ornaments; INAH calls them "male human beings carrying insignia of power" [V]. Small figurines and throne carvings show loincloths and belts, capes, tall headdresses, chest pendants, and deliberately shaped (elongated) heads [R, Pool 2007]. Women's dress and everyday clothing are poorly known.
**Avoid:** the long-debunked idea that the heads show Africans (or any overseas visitors): they depict Indigenous Mesoamericans, and the pseudo-history is offensive to their descendants; Aztec and Maya imagery (cut-stone stepped temples, feathered serpents, heart sacrifice, quetzal-plume crowns of the Aztec kind); the "were-jaguar baby" motif and anything suggesting infant sacrifice; invented ritual.

**Cautions.**
- **"Mother culture" or "sister culture"?** One side: chemical sourcing shows Olmec-style pottery was exported from San Lorenzo, supporting Olmec priority (Blomster, Neff & Glascock 2005 [V]). The other: the Olmec were one of several interacting early societies and the "mother culture" is a myth (Flannery & Marcus 2000 [R]; Stoltman et al. 2005 [R]). Unresolved; the caption should not take a side.
- "Olmec" is not their name: it is an Aztec-era Nahuatl term for much later inhabitants of the rubber country. Their language is unknown.
- Dates in older books are uncalibrated and run two or three centuries too young.
- La Venta was badly damaged by oil works and an airstrip between 1958 and 1984 [V, INAH]; many monuments are now in a park in Villahermosa, so photographs of them "in situ" mislead.

## 9. Classical Greece

**Recommended site and date: Athens, painted at about 430 BC**, with the vignette representing the Classical period, 479–323 BC.

Why: by 432 BC the Parthenon (447–432 BC) and the gateway (Propylaia, 437–432 BC) were complete; the small temple of Athena Nike (427–424 BC) and the Erechtheion (421–406 BC) were not yet built [V, UNESCO]. A date of c. 430 BC therefore fixes exactly which buildings may stand on the Acropolis. Choosing c. 400 BC instead allows all four.

**Structures.**
- Parthenon: white marble from Mount Pentelikon [R, Hurwit]. **Not plain white.** Traces of Egyptian blue and a purple colourant survive on the Parthenon sculptures, and the carved drapery was painted with elaborate figured patterns [V, Verri et al. 2023]. What is *known* is that sculpture and mouldings carried colour; how saturated it was, and whether the columns and walls were tinted, is reconstruction. Recommend warm white marble with colour confined to the sculpture band, pediment figures and ornament, not a fully painted temple.
- Below the Acropolis: the Agora with long colonnaded halls (stoas) and the temple of Hephaistos [R, Camp 2001]. Ordinary houses were plain: mudbrick on stone footings, tiled roofs, inward-facing courtyards, on narrow unpaved streets [R, Camp 2001].
- **Trap: no stone theatre.** The Theatre of Dionysos of Sophocles's day had largely wooden seating; the stone auditorium is a rebuilding of about a century later [R, Camp 2001]. Also no arches, domes or Corinthian colonnades (Roman-era look).

**Objects and tools.** Red-figure painted pottery (black figures on red is the older style, fading by 430 BC); two-handled transport jars; silver coins stamped with Athena's owl; bronze helmets and round shields; iron tools and blades (**iron is correct here**, the only vignette where it is); the upright warp-weighted loom with clay weights; pottery oil lamps; olive presses; wax tablets and stylus; lyres and double pipes [R, Camp 2001; Lee 2015 for the loom and cloth].

**Crops.** The Mediterranean set: barley and wheat, olives, grapevines, figs, and pulses (lentils, chickpeas, broad beans) [R, Halstead 2014; Foxhall 2007; Isager & Skydsgaard 1992]. Attica's thin soils favoured barley and the olive; much of Athens's wheat was imported. **No maize, tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, citrus groves, prickly pear or cotton fields.**

**Domestic animals.** Sheep, goats, cattle (plough oxen), pigs, donkeys, mules, dogs, bees, and horses for the wealthy [R, Halstead 2014; Isager & Skydsgaard 1992]. **Chickens are correct here:** Greek seafarers carried them across the Mediterranean, with directly dated bones in Greek colonies in Italy from 776–540 BC [V, Peters et al. 2022; Best et al. 2022 V]. Traps: horses ridden without saddles, stirrups or horseshoes; no camels; cats were uncommon, so leave them out.

**People.** The best-documented of the nine, from vase painting, sculpture and texts: the chiton (a light tunic, usually linen), the peplos (a heavier pinned woollen dress), and the himation (a large wrap); adult citizen men usually bearded; women's hair bound up; clothing often coloured and patterned, not uniformly white (Lee 2015 [R]; Verri et al. 2023 [V] for painted textile patterns). Who was in the street matters: citizens were a minority; resident foreigners and a large enslaved population did much of the work, and women's work (weaving, fetching water) was constant.
**Avoid:** a gleaming all-white marble city; Roman togas, laurel-wreathed senators, gladiators; men walking about naked (nudity belongs to the gymnasium and to artistic convention); a street of bearded philosophers only; Spartan-film armour; casting everyone as northern European; the lost gold-and-ivory statue of Athena shown in confident detail.

**Cautions.** "Birthplace of democracy" and "cradle of Western civilization" are heritage slogans; UNESCO's own text uses them ("On this hill were born Democracy, Philosophy, Theatre...") [V] and should not be echoed uncritically. Athenian democracy excluded women, the enslaved and foreigners, and the building programme was paid for in part by tribute from subject allies. "Classical Greece" was hundreds of city-states, not Athens alone. Colour reconstructions of Greek sculpture are hypotheses. The Parthenon sculptures in London are the subject of a live restitution dispute; the picture need not touch it, but source images should be credited with care.

## Across the whole picture

**Where to end it.** The latest things in the nine are the Parthenon (finished 432 BC), the final stage of La Venta's mound (c. 400 BC) and the Classical period's conventional close (323 BC). The fairest end is **about 400 BC, "roughly 2,400 years ago"**, with 323 BC as the outer limit. Nothing in the picture should imply Rome, the Han empire, the Maya, the Inca or anything later. Note also that the nine are not one moment and not a sequence of "stages": they span about 20,000 years, Caral was abandoned before Anyang was founded, and the Americas' vignettes are neither earlier nor later "steps" than the Old World's.

**What a careful reader will notice is missing.**
- **Africa beyond Egypt.** Nothing from Nubia (Kerma, and later Kush, on the Nile south of Egypt), from the West African Sahel and forest zone (early farming of African cereals; the Nok sculptures of Nigeria in the first millennium BC), or from the Horn. I did not source these this session; they are listed as omissions, not as vetted content.
- **Mesopotamia's and Egypt's neighbours:** Elam and the Iranian plateau, Anatolia (the Hittites), the Levant, Bronze Age Crete and Mycenae, Central Asia's oasis cities, Persia.
- **The Pacific and Australia.** New Guinea is an independent centre of agriculture: at Kuk swamp, wetland cultivation goes back about 7,000 years and possibly 10,000 [V, UNESCO; Denham et al. 2003 R]. In south-eastern Australia the Gunditjmara built one of the world's oldest and most extensive aquaculture systems [V, UNESCO]. The settlement of the Pacific islands is absent.
- **North America.** Poverty Point, Louisiana: monumental earthworks built by hunter-fisher-gatherers 3,700–3,100 years ago, older than La Venta and made without farming [V, UNESCO].
- **The rest of East, South-East and South Asia** beyond the Yellow River and the Indus; and the Amazon basin.
- **People who built without farming, cities or kings**, as at Göbekli Tepe and Poverty Point. The nine equate "civilization" with cities, monuments and fields, which is itself a choice. So is the title: many archaeologists now avoid "the rise of civilization" in the singular and "cradle" language altogether.
- Europe supplies both the first and the last vignette, although the oldest known cave art is in Indonesia [V, Oktaviana et al. 2024].

**What the caveat should probably say** (draft sense, for the editor to word):
"These nine scenes are examples, not a complete map or a ladder of progress: people built complex societies in many other places, including much of Africa, the Pacific and North America, and not all of them farmed or lived in cities. Each scene is anchored to one excavated site and date; buildings above their surviving walls, and most clothing and colour, are informed reconstruction."
