# Dossier: the other humans of the Late Pleistocene, and facts for a mammoth-hunt painting

Status: complete draft, compiled 14 November 2026. Companion to section 7 of `lesser-extinctions-mesozoic-cenozoic.md` (the megafauna research is not repeated here). Part A covers when and why Neanderthals, Denisovans, *Homo floresiensis*, late *Homo erectus* and *Homo luzonensis* disappeared. Part B covers facts for a painting of a mammoth hunt. A consolidated list of what could not be verified is at the end.

## How to read this dossier

- **Citation checks.** Every citation marked **[CR]** was looked up in the Crossref database during this session (14 Nov 2026); authors, year, title, journal, volume, pages and DOI are as Crossref returned them. For papers with long author lists I give the first three names and Crossref's author count; copy the full list from the publisher page. Where Crossref gives an online year different from the print year, both are given. Anything not confirmed in Crossref is marked **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Claim checks.** A real citation is not a checked claim. **[read]** = I read the statement in the paper's full text (open-access copy at Europe PMC) this session. **[abstract]** = from the paper's abstract (retrieved from Europe PMC, OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar). **[not checked]** = my background knowledge, not confirmed against a source this session. Many of the key papers are paywalled, so most claims here are [abstract]; that is a real limit and is flagged again at the end.
- Dates: "ka" = thousand years ago. "cal BP" = calibrated (calendar) years before 1950. Radiocarbon ("14C") ages are younger than calendar ages in this period; where I convert, I say so and the conversion is approximate.
- Wikipedia was used only to find leads and is not cited.

# PART A. When and why the other humans disappeared

## A0. The short version

| Group | Last secure evidence | *Homo sapiens* in the same region | Overlap | Can *H. sapiens* be called the likely cause? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Neanderthals (Europe, western Asia) | About 41–39 ka (Higham et al. 2014 [abstract]); disputed claims to about 37 ka in southern Iberia | A brief incursion about 54 ka; settled by 45 ka or earlier | At least 5,000 years across Europe; perhaps 1,400–2,900 years within one region | Likely a necessary and probably decisive factor, by inference from timing and models. Mechanism not agreed. No direct evidence of violence. |
| Denisovans (eastern Asia) | A rib from the Tibetan Plateau dated about 48–32 ka (Xia et al. 2024 [abstract]); DNA in living people implies contact later still | Mainland Southeast Asia by about 77–70 ka; Tibetan Plateau about 40–30 ka | Yes, certainly (they interbred), length unknown | Unknown. No extinction date, so not even the timing argument is available. |
| *Homo floresiensis* (Flores) | Bones about 100–60 ka, stone tools to about 50 ka (Sutikna et al. 2016 [abstract]) | Region by 65–50 ka; Flores itself perhaps by about 46 ka | Not demonstrated | Possible; timing is suggestive, but a 2025 study argues for drought, and the original authors call it "an open question". |

## A1–A2. Dates, arrival of *Homo sapiens*, and overlap, group by group

### Neanderthals

**Last dates.**
- Higham et al. 2014 remains the standard estimate. From improved radiocarbon dating at 40 sites from Russia to Spain: "the Mousterian ended by 41,030–39,260 calibrated years BP (at 95.4% probability) across Europe"; disappearance "occurred at different times in different regions"; overlap with incoming modern humans of "2,600–5,400 years (at 95.4% probability)" [abstract]. Note what this dates: the end of the Mousterian tool industry, used as a proxy for Neanderthals, not the last Neanderthal skeleton.
- Northwest Europe: redating of the Spy, Engis and Fonds-de-Forêt Neanderthals (Belgium) by a compound-specific method shows that earlier, younger dates were wrong "by up to 10,000 y" because of contamination; Neanderthals "disappeared from Northwest Europe at 44,200 to 40,600 cal B.P. (at 95.4% probability)" (Devièse et al. 2021 [abstract]).
- Slimak et al. 2022 summarize: "The latest Neanderthal remains in Europe are dated to 42 ka to 40 ka ago, while their Mousterian technologies ended by ~41 ka to 39 ka ago" [read].
- Claims of later survival, and their status:
  - Gibraltar (Gorham's Cave): survival "to 28 kyr BP" claimed by Finlayson et al. 2006 [abstract]. Wood et al. 2013 re-tested 11 southern Iberian sites, found that only two had reliably datable material, that those came out "at least 10 ka 14C years older", and concluded that "evidence for the late survival of Neanderthals in southern Iberia is limited to one possible site, Cueva Antón" [abstract].
  - Cueva Antón (Murcia, Spain): Zilhão et al. 2017 argue the Mousterian layer "can be no more than 37,100 years-old" and that Neanderthals persisted south of the Ebro about 3,000 years longer than elsewhere [abstract]. This is a live disagreement between the Zilhão group and the Oxford dating group (Higham, Wood); I did not find a paper that settles it.
  - Byzovaya (Polar Urals, Russia): Mousterian-type tools at "about 31,000 to 34,000 calendar years ago", which "may thus represent a late northern refuge for Neandertals" (Slimak et al. 2011 [abstract]). No Neanderthal bones were found there; the claim rests on tool type alone. [That the attribution is widely doubted: not checked.]
- A technical caution: the 2020 radiocarbon calibration curve (IntCal20) changed the conversion between 48 and 40 ka, exactly this window, and Bard et al. 2020 use the Neanderthal–*sapiens* overlap as their illustration [abstract; the size of the change is not stated in the abstract]. Dates published before 2020 shift somewhat when recalibrated.

**Arrival of *Homo sapiens*.**
- Much earlier, short-lived presences: a skull from Apidima Cave, Greece, dated to "more than 210 thousand years ago" and described as early *Homo sapiens* (Harvati et al. 2019 [abstract]) [that the identification is disputed: not checked].
- Grotte Mandrin, Rhône Valley, France: modern human presence "between 56,800 and 51,700 years ago", in a layer lying between Neanderthal layers (Slimak et al. 2022 [abstract]). The full text describes "a modern human incursion at around 54 ka cal. B.P. ... followed by Neanderthal reoccupations" and a second modern human phase "from ~44.1 ka to 41.5 ka cal. B.P." [read]. [That the identification rests on a single child's tooth and a distinctive tool industry, and that some specialists question it: not checked; from memory.] Note the implication: here Neanderthals outlasted the first arrivals of our species by some ten thousand years [my arithmetic from the dates above].
- Bacho Kiro Cave, Bulgaria: directly dated *Homo sapiens* with Initial Upper Paleolithic tools, "before 45 thousand years ago" (Hublin et al. 2020 [abstract]); three individuals genetically dated "between 45,930 and 42,580 years ago" (Hajdinjak et al. 2021 [abstract]).
- Ranis (Ilsenhöhle), Germany: *Homo sapiens* remains that are "among the earliest directly dated Upper Palaeolithic *H. sapiens* remains in Eurasia"; they "were present in central and northwestern Europe long before the extinction of late Neanderthals in southwestern Europe" (Mylopotamitaki et al. 2024 [abstract]; the title gives "by 45,000 years ago").

**Length of overlap.**
- Continent-wide: "Modern humans arrived in Europe more than 45,000 years ago, overlapping at least 5,000 years with Neanderthals" (Sümer et al. 2025 [abstract]). If Mandrin is accepted, sporadic presence goes back about 14,000 years before the end [my arithmetic].
- Within one region: in France and northern Spain, statistical modeling of 66 dates suggests *Homo sapiens* arrived "up to 1400–2900 years" before the Neanderthals disappeared there; the authors add that whether this "featured some form of direct interaction ... remains to be resolved" (Djakovic et al. 2022 [abstract]).
- Outside Europe the two "both occupied the Levant for tens of thousands of years" before the final replacement (Greenbaum et al. 2019 [abstract]). So contact did not mean rapid disappearance.

**Evidence that they met and interbred** (this is the one part of the story that rests on direct evidence).
- Neanderthals "shared more genetic variants with present-day humans in Eurasia than with present-day humans in sub-Saharan Africa" (Green et al. 2010 [abstract]). Neanderthals "have contributed 1–3% of the DNA of present-day people in Eurasia" (Fu et al. 2015 [abstract]). [Prüfer et al. 2014's figure of about 1.5–2.1% for non-Africans: not checked this session; the full text could not be retrieved.] Chen et al. 2020 **[CR]** reported that African populations also carry some Neanderthal ancestry, less than non-Africans [title only; not checked].
- When: "a single, shared extended period of gene flow that occurred between 50,500 to 43,500 years ago" (Iasi et al. 2024 [abstract]); "approximately 45,000–49,000 years ago" (Sümer et al. 2025 [abstract]).
- Individuals with recent Neanderthal ancestors: the Oase 1 man from Romania (37,000–42,000 years old) had "a Neanderthal ancestor as recently as four to six generations back", though his population "did not contribute substantially to later humans in Europe" (Fu et al. 2015 [abstract]); all three Bacho Kiro individuals "had Neanderthal ancestors a few generations back", "suggesting that such mixing could have been common" (Hajdinjak et al. 2021 [abstract]).
- The reverse direction is not seen late on: in five late Neanderthals (about 47–39 ka), four of whom postdate the arrival of modern humans in Europe, "we do not detect any recent gene flow from early modern humans" (Hajdinjak et al. 2018 [abstract]). Li et al. 2024 find older modern-human DNA in Neanderthals (2.5–3.7%), from much earlier contacts [abstract].

### Denisovans

There is no extinction date for Denisovans. They are known from a handful of fragments identified by DNA or proteins ("only ten fragmentary remains from two sites" as of the review by Peyrégne et al. 2024 [abstract]; more have been reported since), so their record is far too thin to date a disappearance.

- Denisova Cave, Altai, Siberia: Denisovans present "as early as 195,000 years ago"; "the youngest Denisovan dates to 52,000–76,000 years ago" (Douka et al. 2019 [abstract]). The same paper says that artifacts dated 49,000–43,000 cal BP "may be assumed" to be associated with Denisovans, since no modern human remains that old are known from the Altai, an assumption, not a finding [abstract].
- Baishiya Karst Cave, Tibetan Plateau: Denisovan mitochondrial DNA in sediments "deposited ~100 thousand and ~60 thousand years ago (ka) and possibly as recently as ~45 ka" (Zhang et al. 2020 [abstract]); a rib assigned by proteins to the Denisovan lineage "dates to approximately 48–32 thousand years ago" (Xia et al. 2024 [abstract]). This is the youngest physical evidence I found. The authors write that the results "raise questions as to the cause and timing of Denisovan extinction on the Tibetan Plateau" [read]: they offer no cause.
- Other fossils now assigned to Denisovans are older or loosely dated: the Xiahe jaw (Chen et al. 2019 **[CR]**; "late Middle Pleistocene" in the title); a molar from Laos (Demeter et al. 2022 **[CR]**; "Middle Pleistocene" in the title); a jaw dredged off Taiwan (Tsutaya et al. 2025 **[CR]**; its age range is wide [not checked]).
- Evidence from DNA in living people. Modern humans interbred with several distinct Denisovan populations: Papuans carry DNA "from two deeply divergent Denisovan lineages", and introgression from one of them "predominantly took place east of the Wallace line and continued until near the end of the Pleistocene" (Jacobs et al. 2019 [abstract]). [Jacobs et al.'s specific estimates of about 46 ka and about 30 ka for the two pulses, with a possibility as late as about 15 ka: not checked; from memory of the paper.] Ayta Magbukon people of the Philippines have "the highest level of Denisovan ancestry in the world", about 30–40% more than Papuans and Australians (Larena et al. 2021 [abstract]). A review counts "at least three introgression events from distinct Denisovan populations" (Ongaro & Huerta-Sanchez 2024 [abstract]). [Proportion of Denisovan ancestry in Papuans and Aboriginal Australians, usually given as roughly 3–5%: not checked.]
- *Homo sapiens* in the region: at Tam Pà Ling, Laos, "by 70 ± 3 kyr", and possibly 77 ± 9 kyr (Freidline et al. 2023 [abstract]); Sumatra "between 73 and 63 ka" (Westaway et al. 2017 [abstract]); on the high Tibetan Plateau at Nwya Devu, "dating from 40,000 to 30,000 years ago" (Zhang et al. 2018 [abstract]; attributed to modern humans from the tools, with no human fossils [not checked]).
- Overlap: certain, because they interbred, and on the Tibetan Plateau the Denisovan rib (48–32 ka) and the first *sapiens* site (40–30 ka) have overlapping age ranges. How long, and where the last Denisovans lived, is unknown.

### *Homo floresiensis*

- Original reports (Brown et al. 2004 **[CR]**; Morwood et al. 2004 **[CR]**) put the last *floresiensis* as recently as about 12,000 years ago. That is obsolete.
- Sutikna et al. 2016 redated the cave: the skeletal remains "are dated to between about 100 and 60 kyr ago, whereas stone artefacts attributable to this species range from about 190 to 50 kyr in age. Whether *H. floresiensis* survived after 50 kyr ago—potentially encountering modern humans on Flores or other hominins dispersing through southeast Asia, such as Denisovans—is an open question" [abstract].
- Sutikna et al. 2018: *H. floresiensis*, the dwarf elephant relative *Stegodon*, a giant stork and a vulture "were likely extinct by ∼50 ka ago"; a sharp change in stone raw material (more chert) at "∼46 thousand calibrated radiocarbon years before present" may mark the arrival of modern humans, "if an increased preference for chert does, in fact, characterize *Homo sapiens* assemblages" [abstract]. So the arrival date on Flores itself is an inference from tool stone, not from human bones.
- *Homo sapiens* in the wider region before that: Sumatra 73–63 ka (Westaway et al. 2017 [abstract]); cave art in Sulawesi "by 51,200 years ago" (Oktaviana et al. 2024 **[CR]**, title); northern Australia by about 65 ka according to Clarkson et al. 2017 (cited in the megafauna dossier; date challenged).
- Overlap on Flores: not demonstrated. The dates allow a brief overlap or none.

### Late *Homo erectus* (Ngandong, Java)

- Rizal et al. 2020: Bayesian modeling of 52 radiometric dates gives "modelled ages of 117 to 108 thousand years (kyr) for the *H. erectus* bone bed"; the results "negate the extreme ages that have been proposed for the site and solidify Ngandong as the last known occurrence of this long-lived species" [abstract].
- The "extreme ages" were on both sides: Swisher et al. 1996 **[CR]** argued for about 53–27 ka and "potential contemporaneity with *Homo sapiens*" (the phrase is in their title) [ages not checked]; Indriati et al. 2011 **[CR]** argued for much older ages [not checked]. A journalist will still find the young dates repeated in older books.
- A 2025 find of *erectus* skull fragments dredged from the Madura Strait is assigned to "an MIS6 population" [that is, older than Ngandong; roughly 190–130 ka, my gloss] (Berghuis et al. 2025 [abstract]). It does not change the last-appearance date.
- *Homo sapiens* in the region: Sumatra 73–63 ka (Westaway et al. 2017 [abstract]); on Java, the Wajak remains have "a minimum age of between 37.4 and 28.5 ka" (Storm et al. 2013 [abstract]). [A possible modern human tooth in the older Punung fauna of Java (Westaway et al. 2007 **[CR]**) is not accepted as secure, as far as I know: not checked.]
- Overlap: none demonstrated. Between the Ngandong bone bed (117–108 ka) and the earliest secure *sapiens* in island Southeast Asia (73–63 ka) lies a gap of at least 35,000 years [my arithmetic].

### *Homo luzonensis*

- Known only from Callao Cave, northern Luzon: a foot bone "dated to 67 thousand years ago" plus twelve further bones and teeth from at least three individuals, named as a new species in Détroit et al. 2019 [abstract]. [That the dates are minimum ages by uranium-series, and that a second specimen gave a minimum of about 50 ka: not checked.] Hominins were on Luzon far earlier ("by 709 thousand years ago", Ingicco et al. 2018 **[CR]**, title).
- *Homo sapiens* in the Philippines: Tabon Cave, Palawan, with a frontal bone of about 16,500 BP and "older ages" for other fossils (Détroit et al. 2004 [abstract]) [an age near 47 ka for one Tabon bone: not checked].
- There is no last-appearance date worth the name, so no overlap and no cause can be argued. One genetic search found no trace of interbreeding between living people and "super-archaic" humans such as *luzonensis* or *floresiensis*, while confirming widespread Denisovan ancestry (Teixeira et al. 2021 [abstract]).

## A3. What has been proposed for the part *Homo sapiens* played (Neanderthals)

Each line gives the idea, who argues it, and what kind of support it has.

**Explanations in which *Homo sapiens* is the cause**

1. *Competition for food and territory (competitive exclusion).* Banks et al. 2008: niche modeling shows the two species "exploited similar niches"; the Neanderthal range contraction "was not due to climate change or a change in adaptation, but rather concurrent AMH geographic expansion appears to have produced competition that led to Neanderthal extinction" [abstract]. Timmermann 2020: in a numerical dispersal model, abrupt climate shifts "were not major drivers of global Neanderthal extinction ... but played important roles regionally, in particular over northern Europe"; "a realistic extinction of the Neanderthal population can only be simulated when *Homo sapiens* is chosen to be considerably more effective in exploiting scarce glacial food resources" [abstract]. Gilpin et al. 2016: a mathematical model in which a cultural advantage lets a small modern human population exclude a larger Neanderthal one [abstract]. Support: models, plus the timing. None is a direct observation of competition.
   - Proposed advantages: projectile weapons (Sano et al. 2019: mechanically delivered projectiles in Italy 45–40 ka gave "a potential subsistence advantage over other populations and species" [abstract]); a broader diet including small game (Fa et al. 2013, on rabbits in Iberia [abstract]).
   - The counter-case: Villa & Roebroeks 2014, a systematic review of the archaeology, "finds no support" for explaining the demise by Neanderthal "inferiority in archaeologically visible domains" [abstract]. Roebroeks & Soressi 2016 likewise conclude that "the biological and cultural gaps between these populations were probably smaller than previously thought" [abstract].
2. *Numbers (demographic swamping).* Mellars & French 2011: in southwestern France "human populations increased by one order of magnitude" across the transition, "suggesting that numerical supremacy alone may have been a critical factor" [abstract]. [That their method was criticized in a published comment: not checked.] Kolodny & Feldman 2017: a model with no advantage on either side still "predicts the Neanderthals' replacement", because "the eventual replacement of the Neanderthals was determined by the repeated migration of modern humans from Africa into Eurasia" [abstract]. Important for wording: this is a "neutral" model, yet in it *Homo sapiens* is still the cause, simply by continuing to arrive.
3. *Absorption through interbreeding (assimilation).* Villa & Roebroeks 2014: "complex processes of interbreeding and assimilation may have been responsible for the disappearance of the specific Neandertal morphology from the fossil record" [abstract]. Li et al. 2024: Neanderthal effective population size was about 20% lower than previously estimated, and the pattern of gene flow is "consistent with a Neanderthal population that was decreasing in size over time and was ultimately being absorbed into the modern human gene pool"; assimilation "would have effectively increased the size of modern human populations while simultaneously decreasing the size of an already at-risk Neanderthal population" [read]. Hublin et al. 2020 state the mainstream framing in their first sentence: the transition "witnessed the replacement and partial absorption of local Neanderthal populations by *Homo sapiens* populations of African origin" [abstract]. Limits: the 1–3% figure shows that absorption happened, not that it was the main route to disappearance; and late Neanderthal genomes show no incoming *sapiens* DNA (Hajdinjak et al. 2018 [abstract]).
4. *Disease.* Houldcroft & Underdown 2016: "the transfer of pathogens between hominin populations, including the expansion of pathogens from Africa, may also have played a role in the extinction of the Neanderthals" [abstract]. Greenbaum et al. 2019: a model in which disease burdens kept the two apart in the Levant until modern humans overcame them first [abstract]. Support: hypothesis and modeling only; no pathogen has been recovered from a Neanderthal as evidence of an epidemic [not checked]. In the expert survey below, "epidemics" drew the most "don't know" answers (30%).
5. *Violence.* I found no published case for systematic killing, and no site that shows it. The two usual items: Shanidar 3 (Iraq), whose rib wound is "most consistent with injury by a low-mass, low-kinetic energy projectile weapon", which the authors link cautiously to "the possibility" that early modern humans had such weapons, while stating that accident or a Neanderthal attacker "cannot absolutely be ruled out" (Churchill et al. 2009 [abstract]); and St. Césaire 1 (France), a healed skull wound from "a sharp implement" (Zollikofer et al. 2002 [abstract]), with nothing to show who struck the blow. A cut-marked child's jaw from Les Rois, France, once suggested to be Neanderthal-like, has been reassessed in 2026 as "more consistent with *H. sapiens*" (Isle de Beauchaine et al. 2026 [abstract]), which removes it as a possible case of modern humans butchering a Neanderthal. Violence is therefore conjecture.

**Explanations that do not need *Homo sapiens***

6. *Small population size, inbreeding and bad luck.* Vaesen et al. 2019: models "suggest that the disappearance of Neanderthals might have resided in the smallness of their population(s) alone ... even in the absence of inter-specific competition" [abstract]. But note their own qualification: "if modern humans contributed to the demise of Neanderthals, that contribution might have had nothing to do with resource competition, but rather with how the incoming populations geographically restructured the resident populations" [abstract]. Degioanni et al. 2019: a fall of under 4% in the fertility of young women, sustained, would be enough over 10,000 years [abstract]. Evidence of inbreeding and isolation: the Altai Neanderthal woman's "parents were related at the level of half-siblings" (Prüfer et al. 2014 [abstract]); 17 congenital anomalies in the El Sidrón family group (Ríos et al. 2019 [abstract]); the Mandrin Neanderthal "Thorin" belonged to a small group genetically isolated for "some 50 ka" from neighbors (Slimak et al. 2024 [abstract]).
7. *Climate swings.* Staubwasser et al. 2018: cold, dry phases at 44.3–43.3 and 40.8–40.2 ka match archaeologically empty layers; "ecologic stress ... caused a diachronous pattern of depopulation of Neanderthals, which facilitated repopulation by modern humans who appear to have been better adapted to this environment" [abstract]. Vidal-Cordasco et al. 2022: in northern Iberia the Neanderthal disappearance "was contemporaneous with a significant decrease in the available biomass" [abstract]. Against: Banks et al. 2008 and Timmermann 2020 above; and the general point that Neanderthals had "thrived in Eurasia for more than 300,000 years" (Villa & Roebroeks 2014 [abstract]), which includes earlier cold phases [the inference that earlier swings were as severe is mine; not checked].
8. *The Campanian Ignimbrite eruption.* Dated at 39.85 ± 0.14 ka (Giaccio et al. 2017 [abstract]), which is very close to the end. Giaccio et al. see "compelling chronological evidence for the significance of the combined influence of the CI eruption and Heinrich Event 4 on European climate and potentially evolutionary processes" [abstract]. Lowe et al. 2012, tracing the ash through cave sites, conclude the opposite: "the combined effects of a major volcanic eruption and severe climatic cooling failed to have lasting impacts on Neanderthals or early modern humans in Europe. We infer that modern humans proved a greater competitive threat to indigenous populations than natural disasters" [abstract]. Black et al. 2015 **[CR]** modeled the eruption's climate effect [conclusion that it was insufficient alone: not checked]. The decline was under way in several regions before the eruption (Higham et al. 2014; Devièse et al. 2021 [abstracts]).

## A4. The state of the debate

**Is there a consensus statement?** The nearest thing is a survey of specialists, and it does not settle the question.

- Vaesen, Dusseldorp & Brandt 2021 surveyed 216 practicing paleoanthropologists. Abstract: "It appears that received wisdom is that demography was the principal cause of the demise of Neanderthals. In contrast, there is no received wisdom about the role that environmental factors and competition with modern humans played in the extinction process; the research community is deeply divided about these issues."
- Numbers, from Table 1 and the text [read]. Respondents scored each factor from 0 to 6. Composite scores: demography 3.41, environment 3.08, competition with modern humans 2.40; "the ranking of the scores was COMP < ENV < DEM". Single factors: population size 4.44 (the only one above 4); climatic factors 3.20; social advantages of modern humans 3.19; inbreeding 3.12; stochasticity 3.03; epidemics 2.76; technological advantages 2.60; cognitive advantages 2.19; morphological advantages 1.51.
- How to read that fairly. The survey measured belief in *competitive advantages* of modern humans, and found specialists lukewarm and split. It did not ask whether the arrival of modern humans mattered at all. "Small population size" is a condition, not a trigger, and several of the demographic explanations still run through *Homo sapiens*: repeated immigration (Kolodny & Feldman 2017), breaking up and isolating Neanderthal groups (Vaesen et al. 2019), absorbing them (Li et al. 2024). Assimilation was not offered as a choice; "only two respondents" wrote it in [read]. So the survey rules out saying "experts agree we out-competed them". It does not rule out saying that our arrival was probably decisive.

**Published statements that do make *Homo sapiens* the main factor:** Banks et al. 2008; Lowe et al. 2012; Timmermann 2020; Mellars & French 2011; and the framing sentences of the recent genetic and fossil papers ("replacement and partial absorption", Hublin et al. 2020) [all abstract]. I did not find a formal consensus statement or a recent broad review that states it as settled. Two reviews I could not verify or read: Stringer & Crété 2022 (UNVERIFIED) and Hublin's 2017 commentary "The last Neanderthal" (**[CR]**, not read).

**What is evidence and what is inference.**
- Evidence (direct): the dates of the last Neanderthals and first modern humans in Europe; the fact and rough timing of interbreeding; small, inbred, isolated Neanderthal groups (genomes); the timing of cold phases and of the eruption.
- Inference from timing: that the arrival caused the disappearance. The argument is that Neanderthals persisted for hundreds of thousands of years through earlier climate swings and vanished within a few thousand years of sustained modern human presence. It is the same form of argument as for the megafauna, and it has the same weakness: coincidence in time is not mechanism. Mandrin cuts the other way, since there Neanderthals returned after the first modern humans left.
- Inference from models: that competition, numbers, or absorption would suffice. Models show what is possible given their assumptions; rival models (small populations alone) also "work".
- No evidence: violence between the two; a specific disease.

**How settled, group by group.**
- Neanderthals: that modern humans were involved is the common framing and well supported by timing; that they were the *main* cause is the leading inference but not a consensus; *how* is openly disputed.
- Denisovans: much weaker. No extinction date, no published causal analysis that I found. Xia et al. 2024 say only that their results "raise questions as to the cause and timing" [read]. Contact with modern humans is proved by DNA; a causal role is conjecture by analogy with Neanderthals.
- *Homo floresiensis*: an open question in the discoverers' own words (Sutikna et al. 2016 [abstract]). The case for *Homo sapiens* is timing only, and the arrival date on Flores is itself inferred. Its disappearance coincides with the loss of *Stegodon* and two giant birds (Sutikna et al. 2018 [abstract]), which looks like the megafauna pattern, but a 2025 study argues for a non-human cause: summer rainfall fell "to a record low ... at 61,000–55,000 years ago, alongside a marked decline in both the abundance and relative proportion of *Stegodon* remains", and "progressive landscape aridification, and intensified human-faunal competition for dwindling resources, culminated in abandonment of Liang Bua" (Gagan et al. 2025 [abstract]; "human" there appears to mean *floresiensis* [my reading]). That drying starts before any evidence of modern humans on the island. Note also that leaving one cave is not the same as extinction.
- *Homo erectus*: on current dates, no. Ngandong at 117–108 ka is "the last known occurrence" (Rizal et al. 2020 [abstract]), tens of thousands of years before any secure *Homo sapiens* in the region. The published explanation is environmental: Southeast Asian savannas "retreated by the Late Pleistocene" and were replaced by rainforest, "at the expense of savannah and woodland specialists, including *Homo erectus*" (Louys & Roberts 2020 [abstract]). Strictly, a last *known* occurrence is not an extinction date, so a later survival cannot be excluded, but there is no evidence for one, and the older claim of contemporaneity with *Homo sapiens* (Swisher et al. 1996) is what Rizal et al. set out to refute. A text that blamed our species for the end of *Homo erectus* would be contradicted by the best current dating.
- *Homo luzonensis*: no basis for any statement.

## A5. My judgment (labeled as mine)

**Neanderthals.** Yes, with care. A plain-language text can fairly say that the arrival of *Homo sapiens* was *probably* the decisive factor, directly or indirectly. I would not write "the most likely cause" without the qualifier that the mechanism is unknown, and I would not write that scientists agree, because the one survey of specialists shows they do not agree on competition and rank small population size first. The honest formulation makes our arrival the trigger and Neanderthal fragility the condition.

Suggested text:

> Neanderthals had lived in Europe and western Asia for more than 300,000 years, through several ice ages. They vanish from the record about 40,000 years ago, a few thousand years after our own species settled in Europe. That timing makes our arrival the most likely trigger, though nobody has shown how it worked. There is no sign of a war. Neanderthal groups were already few, small and inbred, the climate was swinging sharply, and some of them joined us: people of non-African descent today carry about 1 to 3 percent Neanderthal DNA.

Caveats that must travel with it:
1. "Most likely trigger" is an inference from timing and from models, not something observed.
2. Specialists are divided on whether competition from modern humans mattered; many put small population size first (Vaesen et al. 2021).
3. Do not write or imply violence, extermination or "wiped out".
4. The two overlapped for at least 5,000 years in Europe and far longer in the Levant; at Mandrin Neanderthals outlasted the first arrivals. "Soon after we arrived" needs "a few thousand years".
5. "About 40,000 years ago" is right for the card; do not use the old figures of 30,000 or 28,000.
6. "1 to 3 percent" is from Fu et al. 2015's abstract; "about 2 percent" is the common figure [not checked].

**The others.** No single sentence covers them. My advice is not to write that "other ancient humans" in general disappeared because of us.
- Denisovans: say they met and interbred with our ancestors and then disappear from a very thin record; nobody knows when or why.
- *Homo floresiensis*: its last traces (about 50,000 years ago) are close to the time modern humans reached the region; a link is suspected, not shown, and drought is a published alternative.
- *Homo erectus*: leave it out of any sentence about human causes, or state plainly that its last known fossils (about 110,000 years ago) predate our arrival in Java.
- *Homo luzonensis*: omit, or mention only as one more human species alive in the Late Pleistocene.

A possible second paragraph:

> Neanderthals were not the only other humans. Denisovans in Asia and the small-bodied *Homo floresiensis* in Indonesia were also alive when our species spread across the world, and both drop out of the record around that time. We know our ancestors met Denisovans, because many living people in Asia and the Pacific carry their DNA. Whether we had a hand in their disappearance is suspected but unproven.

Caveat for that paragraph: "around that time" is loose for Denisovans (the youngest fossil is dated only to between 48,000 and 32,000 years ago), and for *floresiensis* the evidence is of last traces at one cave.

# PART B. Facts for the painting: a mammoth hunt on the cold steppe

## B0. Recommendation in brief

The best-evidenced setting for the proposed cast is **the Yana River, Arctic Siberia (about 71° N), roughly 32,000–31,000 years ago**, or, as the European alternative, **Kraków Spadzista, southern Poland, roughly 29,000–28,000 years ago**. Both have stone points lodged in mammoth bone. Show hand-held spears with stone tips. Do not show bows, dogs or mammoth-bone huts. Leave out the giant deer or keep it marginal. Treat a fire drive as the painter's device and say so in the caption.

## B1. Direct evidence that people hunted woolly mammoths

Embedded weapon points are the standard of proof; piles of mammoth bone are not, because bones can be collected from natural deaths.

1. **Kraków Spadzista, Poland (Gravettian).** "The oldest unequivocal evidence of mammoth hunting in prehistoric Central Europe": remains of more than 100 woolly mammoths, "with radiocarbon dates clustering ∼25–24 ka uncal BP"; "a fragment of a flint shouldered point is embedded in a mammoth rib, and more than 50% of the site's flint shouldered points and backed blades bear diagnostic traces of hafting and impact damage from use as spear tips"; the age profile of 112 mammoths suggests "recurring heavy hunting" (Wojtal et al. 2019 [abstract]). In calendar years that is about 29,000–28,000 years ago [my conversion, approximate].
2. **Yana, Arctic Siberia.** The site "dates to 27,000 radiocarbon years before present" and yielded "a rare rhinoceros foreshaft, other mammoth foreshafts" (Pitulko et al. 2004 [abstract]), that is, detachable spear foreshafts made of woolly rhinoceros horn and mammoth ivory. Human genomes from the site are about 31,000 years old (Sikora et al. 2019 [abstract]: "between 31,000 and 600 years ago"). Nikolskiy & Pitulko 2013 report the hunting evidence [not checked; the abstract is not openly available: from memory, mammoth shoulder blades and other bones with embedded fragments of stone points and ivory shafts, and an argument that mammoths were hunted selectively and in small numbers, largely for ivory]. A large mammoth bone accumulation lies beside the site (Basilyan et al. 2011 **[CR]**, title).
3. **Lugovskoe, western Siberia.** The site "produced ... unique direct evidence of mammoth hunting"; mammoth dates there run "from about 18,250 BP to about 10,210 BP" in radiocarbon years (Orlova et al. 2004 [abstract]). [The evidence is a mammoth vertebra pierced by a point with stone inserts, dated about 13,500 radiocarbon years, roughly 16,000 calendar years ago: not checked; described in Zenin et al. 2006 **[CR]**, whose abstract I could retrieve only in part.]
4. **Sopochnaya Karga, central Siberian Arctic.** "A mammoth kill site ... dated to 45,000 years before the present", near 72° N (Pitulko et al. 2016 [abstract]). The claim rests on injuries to the bones of one carcass, with no artifacts [not checked]; less secure than an embedded point.
5. **Kostenki, Russia.** Sinitsyn et al. 2019 **[CR]** is titled "New direct evidence of mammoth hunting from Kostenki" [not read; from memory, a mammoth rib with an embedded point]. **Yudinovo**, Russia: "possible evidence" in the authors' own title (Germonpré et al. 2008 **[CR]**).
6. **Mammoth-bone structures (Mezhyrich, Ukraine; Kostenki 11, Russia) are not direct evidence of hunting.** Pryor et al. 2020 date a new circular structure at Kostenki 11 and call it "one of the oldest such features on the Russian Plain", from "the onset of the Last Glacial Maximum" [abstract]. Whether the bones came from kills or from collecting is debated [not checked]. Mezhyrich is younger [about 18,000–17,000 calendar years ago: not checked].

**Most secure:** Kraków Spadzista and Yana (points in bone, many animals, well dated), then Lugovskoe (one bone, later). For this dossier Kraków Spadzista is the only one whose key claim I confirmed from an abstract.

**A place and time for the whole cast.** Yana, about 32,000–31,000 years ago, has mammoth hunters together with woolly rhinoceros (a horn foreshaft, Pitulko et al. 2004 [abstract]) and, from memory of the site's faunal lists, steppe bison, horse, reindeer, cave lion and wolf, with hare the most numerous animal [not checked]. The giant deer is the one that does not fit: it had a "relatively narrow overall distribution" and required "a mixed, partially open habitat providing both graze and browse" (Lister & Stuart 2019 [abstract]); it is not a far-northern open-steppe animal. Either omit it, or set the scene in central or eastern Europe before the glacial maximum and place it at a shrubby valley edge [its presence at Kraków Spadzista itself: not checked].

## B2. Weapons

Evidenced:
- **Spears with stone tips, hand-held.** Kraków Spadzista: flint shouldered points and backed blades hafted as "spear tips" (Wojtal et al. 2019 [abstract]).
- **Composite spears with detachable foreshafts** of mammoth ivory and rhinoceros horn at Yana (Pitulko et al. 2004 [abstract]).
- **Long spears of straightened mammoth ivory** in the Sungir burials, Russia, about 34,000 years ago [not checked; date from Sikora et al. 2017 [abstract]].
- **Bone or ivory points with small stone blades set into grooves** in Siberia later on (Lugovskoe) [not checked].
- Whether spears were thrust or thrown cannot be told from most finds; both are defensible.

Not evidenced there and then:
- **Spear-thrower: do not show, or treat as conjecture.** The oldest surviving spear-throwers are from southwestern Europe. Cattelain & Pétillon 2015 define the earliest type (15 objects from France and Spain) and date it to "19 000–18 000 cal BP", calling it "probably the first known Paleolithic spear-thrower model" [abstract, in French; my translation]. [The often-quoted older Solutrean specimen from Combe-Saunière is included in that group; its exact age: not checked.] None is known from the eastern European or Siberian mammoth steppe of 32,000–28,000 years ago [not checked]. Earlier use has been inferred from the wear on stone tips in Italy at 45–40 ka (Sano et al. 2019 [abstract]), which is indirect.
- **Bows: do not show.** The only claim for bows this early is an inference from the small size of points at Mandrin, France, 54 ka (Metz et al. 2023 [abstract]); it is not from the mammoth steppe and no bow or arrow shaft survives from the Paleolithic of northern Eurasia before the very end of the ice age [not checked].
- **Dogs: do not show in a scene of 32,000–28,000 years ago.** Genetic work argues that "dogs were domesticated in Siberia by ∼23,000 y ago" (Perri et al. 2021 [abstract], a hypothesis from genetics); ancient dog genomes show five lineages already distinct "by 11,000 years ago" (Bergström et al. 2020 [abstract]). The oldest generally accepted dog skeleton is Bonn-Oberkassel, Germany, about 14,000 years old (Janssens et al. 2018 **[CR]**; age not checked). Claims of "Paleolithic dogs" over 30,000 years old (Germonpré et al. 2009 **[CR]**) are disputed [not checked]. Wolves, as wild animals at a distance, are fine.
- Also keep out: mammoth-bone huts at Yana (they belong to the Russian and Ukrainian plains, later); Neanderthals (gone some 8,000 years before the Yana occupation).

## B3. Clothing

- **Eyed needles** are the main evidence for sewn, fitted clothing. Bone needles are "an original cultural innovation that emerged in Eurasia between 45–40 ka BP", earliest in Siberia and China; in western Europe they appear only "during the Solutrean" (d'Errico et al. 2018 [abstract]). Gilligan et al. 2024 argue that eyed needles signal finer sewing, perhaps layered clothing with underwear and decoration sewn onto garments [abstract]. [Eyed needles at Yana itself: not checked.]
- **Sungir, Russia, about 34,000 years ago** (Sikora et al. 2017 [abstract]; burials described in Trinkaus & Buzhilova 2018 **[CR]**). [The standard reading, from the placement of thousands of mammoth-ivory beads on the skeletons, is tailored clothing: a pullover shirt or parka, trousers with attached footwear, and a cap or hood, all sewn with beads: not checked this session; the 2018 abstract does not describe the clothing.]
- **Mal'ta and Buret', near Lake Baikal, about 24,000 years ago** (date of the Mal'ta child: Raghavan et al. 2014 [abstract]). The ivory figurines include a "rendition of clothing and accessories" (Lbova et al. 2017 [abstract]). [That some figurines show a hooded one-piece fur suit: not checked; widely illustrated.]
- For the painter: fitted fur or hide clothing, with hood, sleeves, trousers and boots, is well founded for 34,000–24,000 years ago. Rows of ivory beads and pendants are evidenced at Sungir. Bare limbs and draped skins are not appropriate for an Arctic setting.

## B4. Fire in hunting

- I found **no direct archaeological evidence** of fire used to drive or trap mammoths, or any other game, on the mammoth steppe.
- What exists is fire at camps: charcoal and burnt bone at Kostenki 11 (Pryor et al. 2020 [abstract]); bone burned as fuel where wood was scarce (Marquer et al. 2012 **[CR]**, on Mezhyrich; title only).
- And an argument by analogy: Kaplan et al. 2016 "hypothesize", "based on historical analogues", that ice-age hunter-gatherers "had mastered the use of fire as a tool for landscape management, for improving hunting and foraging opportunities" [read]. That is a modeling study of European forest cover; it presents no evidence of fire drives.
- Advice: a fire drive may be painted only as the painter's device and should be labeled as such ("there is no direct evidence that mammoth hunters used fire in the hunt"). Torches or a camp fire are safer. Dry, cured grass in late summer or autumn would carry fire [my inference].

## B5. The look of the mammoth steppe, and what replaced it

- The classic account (Guthrie 2001 **[CR]**; Zimov et al. 2012 **[CR]**) [abstracts not openly available; the following is not checked]: a cold, dry, largely treeless grassland under clear skies, with grasses, sedges, herbs and sagebrush (*Artemisia*) on firm, well-drained soil, stretching from western Europe across Siberia to Yukon; more productive than today's tundra, and partly kept open by the grazers themselves (Zimov's argument).
- A correction from ancient DNA: "for much of the period investigated, Arctic vegetation consisted of dry steppe-tundra dominated by forbs" (non-grass flowering herbs); the authors say their findings "question the predominance of a Late Quaternary graminoid-dominated Arctic mammoth steppe" (Willerslev et al. 2014 [abstract]). So paint flowering herbs through the grass, not a uniform sward. A later, larger study describes "a relatively homogeneous steppe–tundra flora" across the Arctic during the glacial maximum (Wang et al. 2021 [abstract]).
- What replaced it: "after 10 kyr BP ... moist tundra dominated by woody plants and graminoids" (Willerslev et al. 2014 [abstract]); "extensive peatlands, wet tundra, birch shrubland and coniferous forest" (MacDonald et al. 2012 [abstract]); extinction "came when the last pockets of the steppe–tundra vegetation finally disappeared, when the Arctic-wide paludification was brought on by warmer and wetter climates" (Wang et al. 2021 [read]).
- At 32,000–28,000 years ago the scene is before the glacial maximum ("MIS 3", when mammoths "were abundant in the open-habitat", MacDonald et al. 2012 [abstract]): cold but not the extreme of the ice age. No ice sheet need be in view at Yana [not checked].

## B6. Humans versus climate for the woolly mammoth and woolly rhinoceros

The species-level studies do not support a simple "hunters killed them off" for these two animals. They divide between climate as the main driver and climate plus sustained low-level hunting. None that I checked argues for hunting alone.

**Woolly mammoth**
- Stuart et al. 2004: before extinction, mammoths "underwent dramatic shifts in distribution, driven largely by climatic/vegetational changes" [abstract].
- Nogués-Bravo et al. 2008: "90% of its geographical range disappeared between 42 ky BP and 6 ky BP" for climatic reasons, which made mammoths "more vulnerable to the increasing hunting pressure"; humans were "the coup de grâce" [abstract].
- Lorenzen et al. 2011: "climate has been a major driver of population change over the past 50,000 years"; each species responded differently [abstract]. [Their specific verdict on the mammoth, which I recall as unresolved: not checked; the abstract does not name it.]
- MacDonald et al. 2012: "Long sympatry in Siberia suggests that humans may be best seen as a synergistic cofactor"; "Mammoth extinction was not due to a single cause" [abstract].
- Wang et al. 2021 (environmental DNA): "humans appear to have been a minor factor in driving animal distributions" [abstract]; "the notion of human overkill as the cause of Arctic megafaunal extinction is highly improbable"; mammoths and people co-existed "over at least a 20-kyr interval"; "human hunting was not an important factor in their extinction" [read]. They report mainland mammoth DNA until 3.9 ± 0.2 ka [abstract].
- Challenge to that late date: Miller & Simpson 2022 argue that "the slow decomposition of mammoth tissues on cold Arctic landscapes is responsible for the release of DNA into sediments for thousands of years after mammoths went extinct" [read]. This questions the dating, not the conclusion about hunting.
- Fordham et al. 2022 (simulation models tested against fossils and DNA): the data require "a constrained synergy of climatic change and human impacts"; "validated models needed humans to hasten climate-driven population declines by many millennia"; "the role of humans in the extinction dynamics of woolly mammoth began well before the Holocene" [abstract].
- Wojtal et al. 2019: the heavy hunting at Kraków Spadzista "could portend" the later extinction, when "climate-caused habitat changes ... in combination with opportunistic human hunting, may have led to woolly mammoth extinction" [abstract].
- The last mammoths, on Wrangel Island, were cut off "around 10,000 years ago" and died out "around 4,000 years ago"; the population was "demographically stable" until near the end (Dehasque et al. 2024 [abstract]). [Cause of that final extinction: unknown; whether people had reached the island by then is debated: not checked.]

**Woolly rhinoceros**
- Lorenzen et al. 2011: "climate change alone can explain the extinction of some species, such as Eurasian musk ox and woolly rhinoceros" [abstract].
- Stuart & Lister 2012 **[CR]**: the dating study [not checked; abstract not openly available; from memory, extinction about 14,000 years ago, linked to warming and the spread of shrubs and trees, with deep snow a likely factor].
- Lord et al. 2020 (genomes): population size "remained steady for more than 13 ka following the arrival of humans"; the final contraction "may have thus been sudden and mostly driven by rapid warming in the Bølling-Allerød interstadial" [abstract].
- Wang et al. 2021: rhinoceros DNA in sediments until 9.8 ± 0.2 ka [abstract] (the Miller & Simpson caution applies).
- Fordham et al. 2024 (simulation models): "the combination of cooling temperatures and low but sustained hunting by humans trapped woolly rhinoceroses in suboptimal habitats along the southern edge of their range"; "climate-driven habitat fragmentation and low but persistent levels of hunting by humans are likely to have synergistically weakened metapopulation processes"; matching the fossil and DNA record "requires low and sustained levels of hunting" [abstract and read].

**For the caption (my wording, as a suggestion).** "People did hunt mammoths; spear points have been found lodged in their bones. But on the northern steppe the animals and the hunters lived side by side for more than 20,000 years, and the mammoth and woolly rhinoceros dwindled as a warmer, wetter climate turned their dry grassland into bog and forest. Studies disagree on whether hunting merely added to that pressure or was needed to finish them."

Two cautions on that caption: the genetic studies (Lord, Wang) find little or no human signal, while the two simulation studies (Fordham 2022, 2024) find that humans were necessary, and both simulation studies come from the same research group. And the global analyses in the megafauna dossier (Svenning et al. 2024 and others) argue for a human cause overall; the northern steppe is the region where that case is weakest.

**Limits of the whole dossier**
1. **Most claims are from abstracts, not full texts.** Full text was read (in part, by searching open-access copies) only for Vaesen et al. 2021, Li et al. 2024, Slimak et al. 2022, Xia et al. 2024, Wang et al. 2021, Miller & Simpson 2022, Fordham et al. 2024 and Kaplan et al. 2016. The four *Nature* papers that anchor Part A (Higham 2014, Sutikna 2016, Rizal 2020, Détroit 2019) are paywalled and were checked at abstract level only.
2. **Abstracts for Wojtal et al. 2019 and Sano et al. 2019 came from Semantic Scholar**, and those for several others from OpenAlex, not from the publisher's page. I have no reason to doubt them, but the wording should be checked against the journal before it is quoted in print.

**Part A**
3. **Stringer & Crété 2022** (*PaleoAnthropology*): not found in Crossref under the DOI I had; UNVERIFIED and not read. It is probably the most relevant recent review of Neanderthal–*sapiens* contact, so this is a real gap.
4. **Whether any recent broad review states that *Homo sapiens* was the main cause.** I found none and rely on the Vaesen et al. 2021 survey plus the framing sentences of primary papers. Hublin 2017 (PNAS commentary) and Timmermann 2020 beyond its abstract were not read.
5. **Neanderthal ancestry "about 2 percent" (Prüfer et al. 2014: 1.5–2.1%)**: not checked; the dossier's "1–3%" is from the abstract of Fu et al. 2015. Li et al. 2024 revise such estimates; I did not extract their figure.
6. **The published criticism of Mellars & French 2011**, of the Mandrin tooth identification, and of the Apidima 1 identification: remembered, not checked.
7. **Jacobs et al. 2019's dates for Denisovan interbreeding** (about 46 ka and about 30 ka, possibly later): from memory; only "until near the end of the Pleistocene" is confirmed from the abstract. **Denisovan ancestry of roughly 3–5% in Papuans and Australians**: not checked.
8. **Age ranges of the Xiahe, Laos and Penghu Denisovan fossils**: titles only. Recent work assigning the Harbin skull to Denisovans (2025) was not looked up.
9. **Homo luzonensis dating details** (minimum ages; a second specimen at about 50 ka) and **the age of the oldest Tabon Cave human bone**: not checked.
10. **Ngandong**: the specific ages argued by Swisher et al. 1996 and Indriati et al. 2011, and whether Rizal et al. 2020 discuss *Homo sapiens* at all: not checked (full text not available to me). The statement that no secure *Homo sapiens* is known from Java before the Wajak minimum age rests on my knowledge, not on a review.
11. **Byzovaya**: that the Neanderthal attribution is widely doubted is from memory.
12. **Gagan et al. 2025**: my reading that "human-faunal competition" refers to *Homo floresiensis* and its prey is from the abstract alone. A 2026 preprint by the same group (DOI 10.31223/x5013w, found in Crossref) was not read.
13. **Size of the IntCal20 effect** on the Higham et al. 2014 dates: not quantified here.

**Part B**
14. **Nikolskiy & Pitulko 2013 (Yana mammoth hunting)**: citation verified, content not checked; no abstract was retrievable. The description of embedded points, selective hunting for ivory, and the Yana faunal list (bison, horse, reindeer, lion, wolf, hare) are from memory. This matters because Yana is my recommended setting.
15. **Lugovskoe vertebra** (nature of the point, the date of about 13,500 radiocarbon years): from memory; only "unique direct evidence of mammoth hunting" is confirmed (Orlova et al. 2004 abstract).
16. **Sinitsyn et al. 2019 (Kostenki)**, **Germonpré et al. 2008 (Yudinovo)**, **Wojtal & Wilczyński 2015**, **Basilyan et al. 2011**: titles only.
17. **Sopochnaya Karga**: that the kill interpretation rests on bone injuries without artifacts is from memory.
18. **Calendar-year conversions** of the Kraków Spadzista (25–24 ka uncalibrated) and Yana (27 ka uncalibrated) radiocarbon ages are my approximations, not calibrated values from the papers.
19. **Spear-throwers**: that none is known from the eastern European or Siberian Paleolithic, and the age of the Combe-Saunière specimen: not checked. **Bows**: the absence of any surviving bow or arrow before the end of the ice age in northern Eurasia: not checked.
20. **Dogs**: the age of the Bonn-Oberkassel dog and the disputed status of the Goyet "Paleolithic dog": from memory.
21. **Clothing**: the Sungir reconstruction (parka, trousers, footwear, cap) and the hooded Mal'ta and Buret' figurines: from memory; no source read this session describes them. Eyed needles at Yana: not checked. Sungir direct dates (Marom et al. 2012): figures not in the abstract.
22. **Mammoth steppe**: Guthrie 2001 and Zimov et al. 2012 are verified citations whose content I could not open; the "classic account" paragraph is from memory.
23. **Mezhyrich date**, and whether mammoth-bone structures were built from hunted or collected bones: not checked.
24. **Giant deer**: whether it occurs at Kraków Spadzista or any mammoth-hunting site of 32,000–28,000 years ago: not checked.
25. **Stuart & Lister 2012 (woolly rhinoceros)** and **Stuart & Lister 2011 (cave lion)**: content from memory. **Lorenzen et al. 2011's verdict on the mammoth specifically**: not in the abstract; not checked.
26. **Wrangel Island**: whether people were present before the last mammoths died: not checked.
