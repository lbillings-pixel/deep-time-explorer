## The Lapita expansion: the settling of the remote Pacific

**Recommended site and date (my view): Nukuleka, on the lagoon of Tongatapu, Kingdom of Tonga, painted at about 900–850 BC, "nearly 2,900 years ago".**

The date: uranium-thorium dating of coral files (abraders) puts the founding of Nukuleka "by 2838±8 BP" [V, Burley, Weisler & Zhao 2012]; the excavator rounds this to "900 BC" in his own book [V, Burley 2023, p. 97]. **A wording point for the editor:** the paper does not say what its "BP" counts back from. If it is 1950, as usual, 2838 BP is about 888 BC, which is about 2,910 years before today, not 2,840. The additions dossier's "by about 2,840 years ago" reads BP as "years ago". "About 900 BC" or "nearly 2,900 years ago" is safer.

Why Nukuleka, weighed against the others:

| Candidate | What it offers a painter | Why not |
|---|---|---|
| **Nukuleka, Tonga** (about 900–850 BC) | The best date in the Lapita world; the far eastern end of the expansion, the first settlement of Polynesia [V, Burley et al. 2012]; a described setting, "no more than a small sandy islet at the entrance into two expansive bays", with rich shellfish flats [V, Burley 2023, p. 97]; a clean animal list (see below). It grew to about 20 hectares over 200–250 years [V, Burley et al. 2012]. | No house has been excavated. Stilt houses are an inference (see Structures). Much of the plant and animal evidence comes from other Lapita sites on the same lagoon, a few generations later. Pottery survives only as sherds. |
| **Teouma, Efate, Vanuatu** (about 3,000–2,800 years ago) | The richest single site: complete pots; pig, chicken and two rat species; an extinct giant tortoise, megapode and land crocodile; banana in the tartar on the teeth; ancient DNA from the people themselves [V, Kinaston et al. 2014; White et al. 2010; Worthy et al. 2015; Tromp et al. 2020; Skoglund et al. 2016]. | It is a cemetery: 68 graves, about 100 individuals; its complete pots were grave furniture, one holding a skull under an upturned dish [V, Kinaston et al. 2014; Burley 2023, pp. 31–32]. The site's own rule (no burials) removes its main content. Kinaston et al. describe no houses [V]. |
| **Talepakemalai, Mussau, Bismarck Archipelago** (begun about 3,300 years ago) | **The best evidence anywhere for what a Lapita settlement looked like:** waterlogged house posts still in place, and preserved coconut, *Canarium* nut and other tree-crop remains [V, Kirch 2019; Kirch 2021 supplement]. | It is the homeland end. The Bismarcks had been inhabited for tens of thousands of years [R], so the scene would not show people reaching empty islands, which is the picture's subject. |
| **Bourewa, Fiji** | Taro and lesser yam starch in Lapita-era deposits [R, Horrocks & Nunn 2007, title]; argued to be Fiji's earliest site, 2950–3050 cal BP [V, cited in Burley et al. 2010]. | I read nothing of its settlement evidence this session. |
| **Lapita itself (Xapeta'a), New Caledonia** | The type site; postholes and hearths with large dentate-stamped sherds, 3000–2700 cal BP [V, Sand et al. 2019]. | Also the site of a pit of whole pots and a pit with human skeletons [V, same]; the 2019 report stresses how little of the site is understood. |

So the honest answer to "which has the best evidence for a settlement" is Talepakemalai, and the best site for *this picture* is, in my view, Nukuleka, with the form of its houses borrowed, openly, from Talepakemalai. **The fair alternative is Efate about 2,950 years ago**, if pigs and giant tortoises are wanted in the scene; that would have to be a generic beach settlement near Teouma, not the excavated cemetery. Do not blend the two: the animals differ (see below).

**Structures.**
- **At Talepakemalai (not the recommended site):** in a 12-square-meter excavation, "the anaerobically preserved post along with several others ... were the remains of stilts that had once supported a house standing over the shallow reef flat" [V, Kirch 2019, p. 488]; the excavator's later account describes "two alignments of anaerobically preserved house posts meeting at a reinforced corner", says a whole house was never fully exposed, and calls it "one of a complex of pole-supported structures" [V, Kirch 2021 online supplement, chapter 3, through the fetch tool]. A second stilt structure is reported from the Arawe Islands, New Britain, where a post is ironwood (*Intsia bijuga*) [V abstract, Hogg et al. 2022]. So: post bases and part of a ground plan are evidence; **floor height, walls, roof shape and thatch are all reconstruction.**
- **At Nukuleka: inferred, not found.** No posts survive. The argument is that decorated sherds lie "within a Lapita-age tidal range", which "implies the presence of stilt-house occupation, as documented ... in Near Oceania" [V abstract, Burley 2016]; Burley now writes flatly that "the first Lapita houses were not on the back beach above high tide, but on stilts within tidal range or in the offshore shallows of the lagoon" [V, Burley 2023, p. 63]. The editors of *Debating Lapita* are cooler: intertidal settlement "appears to be largely restricted to Near Oceania", with Burley 2016 and Nunn & Heorake 2009 noted as "suggested Tongan and Fijian examples" [V, Bedford et al. 2019, p. 6].
- **What to draw:** one or two small rectangular pole-built houses standing on posts over the tidal sand flat at the islet's edge, kept modest, partly hidden or at middle distance. Setting: a low sandy islet at a lagoon mouth, sand bars and shellfish flats, mangrove edge [V, Burley 2023, pp. 88, 97]. **Trap: no mountains.** Tongatapu is a low raised-coral limestone island [V, Burley 2023]; a volcanic peak behind the village would be wrong.
- Vanuatu, for comparison: Lapita sites are on back beaches, with hearths and fire-scoops [V, Bedford et al. 2019, table; Ono et al. 2019]. If the Efate alternative is chosen, houses on dry sand, not stilts.

**Objects and tools.**
- **Pottery is the firm evidence.** Decoration is impressed with a "dentate (comb-like) stamp" [V, Burley 2023, chapter 2]: rows of small tooth marks (their square shape is the usual description [R]), building bands (friezes) of interlocking and infilled triangles, labyrinth patterns, and stylized faces with "almond eyes framed by eyebrows, a projecting nose" [V, Burley 2023, pp. 29–30; Burley et al. 2010, figure 8]. Fine incised lines are also used [V, same figure]. That the stamping was done on unfired, leather-hard clay is the standard account [R].
- **Color:** red-slipped surfaces and white lime pressed into the stamped lines are both recorded on sherds from Nukuleka itself ("slipped red paste"; "dentate stamp infilled triangles and lime infill") [V, Burley et al. 2010, figure 8 caption]. Lapita pottery was also "painted with lime and clay, to give a red and white finish" [V abstract, Bedford 2006, on a pot from Vanuatu]. So: earthy red with crisp white patterning, not bare brown clay.
- **Forms:** flat-bottomed dishes and bowls; jars with a sharp shoulder (carinated) and out-turned rim; cylinder stands; open bowls on pedestal feet (the most ornate faces are on these last two) [V, Burley 2023, pp. 29–30, 87–89]. "Not all pots were decorated" [V, Burley 2023]: plain cooking pots should outnumber decorated ones.
- **At Nukuleka only sherds survive**, so a whole decorated pot in the scene is a reconstruction of form. Two flakes of volcanic glass at Nukuleka proved to be from northern Tonga, **not New Britain** [V, Burley et al. 2010, pp. 133–135: exact page not recorded]; some early sherds were themselves carried in from an unidentified island to the west [V, Burley et al. 2012].
- **May the designs simply be drawn? My view: yes.** They are archaeological finds 2,800–3,000 years old, published in drawings and photographs, and the site's rule (only what published archaeology supports) is met by redrawing motifs from excavated sherds, preferably ones from Nukuleka (Burley et al. 2010, figure 8). Two limits. First, particular published drawings and photographs have their own copyright and need crediting like any source image. Second, **context:** Kirch asks whether the face-bearing vessels were "ancestor pots", and whole decorated pots were used in burial at Teouma and buried in a pit at Lapita [V, Burley 2023, pp. 29–32, 275]. Show pots standing, carried or being made; no pot in ceremonial use, and I would keep face-motif vessels out of the foreground. The motifs are also not dead: Burley and Green trace them into present-day Tongan barkcloth (*ngatu*) and Polynesian tattoo [V, Burley 2023, p. 277].
- **Other objects at Nukuleka:** shell rings, bracelets and beads; shell adzes or chisels; stone adzes; coral files in hundreds; *Anadara* shell net weights [V, Burley et al. 2010, table 2 and text; the counts were garbled in extraction and are not quoted]. One-piece shell fishhooks and trolling lures are documented in Lapita Vanuatu [V, Ono et al. 2019]. No metal, no loom.
- **Obsidian from Talasea, New Britain,** reached the Reef/Santa Cruz Islands, Vanuatu, New Caledonia and Fiji, in the last case over more than 3,000 km [V, Burley 2023, pp. 43–44, citing Ross-Sheppard et al. 2013, which I did not read]. Not attested at Nukuleka (above).

**The canoe.** No Lapita canoe, canoe part or picture of a canoe was reported in anything I read [R: an absence I could not check directly]. What exists:
1. **The voyages themselves:** a 350-km open-sea gap beyond the main Solomon Islands [V, Hawkins & Worthy 2019]; obsidian carried over 3,000 km (above); and, if Tonga was settled directly from central Melanesia as the foreign sherds hint, a passage of 1,750–2,300 km, "a difficult feat" [V, Burley et al. 2010, p. 140]. People, pots, plants and live animals made these crossings.
2. **Words.** Comparing canoe terms in descendant languages, Pawley & Pawley conclude that speakers of the ancestral languages "were familiar with outrigger sailing canoes", with terms for outrigger float, outrigger boom, sail, steering paddle, planks built up on the hull, bailer, and (for Proto-Oceanic, the language associated with Lapita) a platform over the booms [V]. Their limits are stated plainly: the reconstructions "do not indicate whether the sails ... were triangular, crab claw or rectangular", nor whether masts were fixed; and "a term for double canoe can be attributed to" Proto-Polynesian "but not to" Proto-Oceanic, which does not prove such craft were unknown [V].
3. **Later canoes.** The Anaweka canoe from New Zealand "dates to approximately A.D. 1400" [V abstract, Johns, Irwin & Sung 2014]: about 2,300 years too late and from the far end of Polynesia. Sailing trials of model hulls offer only "tentative suggestions" about Lapita-period canoes [V abstract, Irwin & Flay 2015].
4. **Scholars differ on the hull.** Roger Green (reported in Pawley & Pawley) held that large double canoes were the key to settling Remote Oceania; Burley writes that Proto-Oceanic speakers "had large double-hulled vessels with a central platform, a standard mast/boom rig with woven mat sails, and a steering oar", citing Pawley & Pawley [V, Burley 2023, p. 20], which is more than that chapter's own conclusions claim. That early sailing rigs were simpler and poorer to windward is argued by Atholl Anderson [**UNVERIFIED**: I could not confirm the citation].

**What a painter may show without inventing: one outrigger sailing canoe, small in the frame, either far out on the lagoon or drawn up on the sand.** A single slender hull, one outrigger float on two or three booms, a steering paddle, and the **sail furled or lowered**, which avoids choosing a sail shape nobody knows. Everything about it is reconstruction, and the caption must say so, in the sense of: "No Lapita canoe has ever been found. The boat is reconstructed from words for canoe parts shared by the languages descended from that of the Lapita people." A beach with no canoe at all is defensible but would undersell the one thing the scene is about. A double-hulled vessel is not disproved, but every double canoe a painter could copy is a much later Polynesian or Fijian one.

**Crops. The "transported landscape".** Lapita voyagers carried their food plants with them, but the direct evidence is microscopic (starch grains, pollen, plant silica), thin, and often from deposits a little later than first landfall.
- **For Tonga:** taro (*Colocasia esculenta*), giant swamp taro (*Cyrtosperma*), banana, candlenut, pandanus and coconut are identified from a Lapita planting pit at Hopoate, part of the Nukuleka project [V, Burley 2023, pp. 86–87, reporting an analysis by M. Horrocks; the primary paper, "Burley et al. 2018", was not identified or read]. From Talasiu, on the same lagoon and later (about 700 BC, as I understand it [R]): three yams, taro, swamp taro, banana, Tahitian chestnut [V, Burley 2023, p. 142]. Burley's own reconstruction of Nukuleka is taro in a planting pit "with bananas, coconuts, and pandanus planted around the perimeter" [V, p. 98].
- **Elsewhere:** aroids (the taro family) at a Lapita site on Uripiv, Vanuatu, about 2700 BP, "the most compelling evidence to date of Lapita horticulture in Remote Oceania" in 2005 [V abstract, Horrocks & Bedford 2005]; banana in dental calculus at Teouma, about 2,900 cal BP, with most plant particles from trees and shrubs [V abstract, Tromp et al. 2020]; yet "no direct evidence thus far regarding horticulture at Teouma" as of 2014, and its authors think gardens were only "being established" [V, Kinaston et al. 2014]. Taro and lesser yam at Bourewa, Fiji [R, Horrocks & Nunn 2007]. Coconut, *Canarium* and *Terminalia* at Talepakemalai [V, Kirch 2019].
- **Coconut is not simply an introduction:** coconut pollen in a Tongan core dates to 4578–4344 BC, long before people [V, Burley 2023, p. 197]. Palms on the shore are right either way.
- **Breadfruit: no direct evidence found for the Lapita period at any of these sites** in what I read. It is on the linguistic list of early crops [V, Burley 2023, pp. 17–18, citing Blust]. Leave it out or keep it unrecognizable.
- **How to draw it:** a small garden plot with broad-leaved taro and a few banana plants behind the houses, coconut and pandanus on the sand. Foraging mattered as much: the Nukuleka deposits are shell midden up to 2 m thick [V, Burley et al. 2010].
- **Traps: no sweet potato** (an American plant: Roullier et al. 2013, marked [V abstract] in the additions dossier, not re-read). In a pollen core described in Burley's Ha'apai chapter, sweet potato "occurs in the upper part of Zone 5", a zone whose beginning is dated AD 689–991 [V, Burley 2023, p. 197]: at least 1,500 years after the scene. No cassava, papaya, pineapple, mango, citrus, frangipani, hibiscus hedges or bougainvillea, all later arrivals [R: standard crop history, not sourced this session]. Bananas small and upright-growing rather than supermarket fruit [R].

**Domestic animals. This is where the site choice matters most.**
- **Chicken: yes.** At Ha'ateiho, a Lapita site on the Tongatapu lagoon, 56 chicken bones come from the Lapita occupation [V, Burley 2023, p. 133]; chickens are in 19 of the Lapita and post-Lapita sites surveyed by Hawkins & Worthy 2019 [V] and in every layer at Teouma [V abstract, Worthy et al. 2015]. On Lifuka (Ha'apai), dated chicken bones do not come from the very lowest layer [V, Steadman et al. 2002, through the fetch tool]: a few birds, not a flock.
- **Pig: not in Lapita Tonga.** The excavator: "Pigs and dogs are absent from all other Lapita faunal assemblages in Tonga"; the two pig and four dog bones from Lolokoka (Niuatoputapu) "can only be viewed with a high degree of suspicion"; "pig, at least in Fiji and Tonga, is absent during the Lapita phase" [V, Burley 2023, pp. 252–253, 327]. **This is disputed:** Kirch, who dug Lolokoka, still calls "the recovery of pig bone at Lolokoka ... significant" [V, Kirch 2019], and Roger Green identified pig at two Tongatapu Lapita sites, which Burley rejects on context [V, Burley 2023, p. 327 note 27]. **At Teouma, by contrast, pigs are present** ("the domestic pigs and chickens at Teouma") [V, Kinaston et al. 2014], and a drilled pig tusk is reported at Talepakemalai [V, Kirch 2019].
- **Dog: no, at either site.** A study of ancient dog DNA found a dog lineage in late and post-Lapita Near Oceania but "did not find any evidence that these dogs were successfully transported beyond New Guinea"; the dogs of Polynesia look like a later introduction [V abstract, Greig et al. 2018]. Kinaston et al. list "Chickens, pigs and two rat species" at Teouma and no dog [V]. Burley calls the absence "enigmatic", since words for pig and dog exist in the reconstructed languages [V, Burley 2023, p. 285, where he is writing of the phase just after Lapita; p. 240 for no pigs or dogs in Lapita Vava'u].
- **Pacific rat (*Rattus exulans*):** present at Teouma with a second, larger rat [V, Kinaston et al. 2014]; "Polynesian rat" bones are in a Tongatapu lagoon assemblage [V, Burley 2023, p. 141; I did not confirm which site or its date]. Nothing a painter needs to show.
- **So: for Nukuleka, chickens only. For the Efate alternative, pigs and chickens. No dogs in either. No cats, cattle, goats or horses, ever.**
- **The animals that were there when people landed, and soon were not.** Tonga: a large iguana, *Brachylophus gibbonsi*, estimated at 1.9 times the size of the living Fijian banded iguana (which reaches 80 cm), 55 bones at Ha'ateiho; two extinct megapodes (mound-building fowl) larger than Tonga's surviving one; extinct pigeons, rails and a parrot; sea turtles in quantity [V, Burley 2023, pp. 132–133, 184–185; V abstract, Worthy & Burley 2019]. On Lifuka the iguana and the megapode *Megapodius alimentum* vanished "within a time interval too short (a century or less)" to measure [V abstract, Steadman et al. 2002]. Vanuatu: a large hornless land tortoise (?*Meiolania damelipi*), hunted from Vanuatu to Fiji [V abstract, Hawkins et al. 2016]; the megapode *Mwalau walterlinii*, which could fly [V abstract, Worthy et al. 2015]; the small land crocodile *Mekosuchus kalpokasi* [V abstract, Mead et al. 2002]. Fiji and New Caledonia: the giant fowl *Megavitiornis* and *Sylviornis*, for which "direct evidence for humans hunting [them] ... is generally tenuous" [V, Hawkins & Worthy 2019]. **Do not move animals between archipelagos:** no tortoise or crocodile in a Tonga scene (Burley notes Tonga's islands were too young for true giants [V abstract, Worthy & Burley 2019]); no giant iguana in Vanuatu. An iguana on a branch and a megapode at the forest edge would be well founded at Nukuleka; their size and color are reconstruction from bones.

**People.**
- **Who they were.** Genome-wide ancient DNA from three people buried at Teouma and one from Tonga shows that the first Lapita settlers "had little to no Papuan ancestry" and descended from an East Asian-related population [V abstract, Skoglund et al. 2016], traced to the spread of Austronesian languages out of Taiwan from about 5,000 years ago [V abstract, Lipson et al. 2018]. **That changed quickly:** people of almost entirely Papuan ancestry reached Vanuatu "by around 2300 BP" (Lipson) or "starting ~2,500 yr BP" (Posth), from the Bismarck Archipelago, and largely replaced the first population there while the languages stayed Austronesian [V abstracts, Lipson et al. 2018; Posth et al. 2018]. All Pacific Islanders today carry at least 25 percent Papuan ancestry [V abstract, Skoglund et al. 2016]. The Tongan individual in the 2016 study is later (about 2,700–2,300 years ago) than the scene.
- **For the painter:** brown-skinned, dark-haired people, drawn without attempting a specific physical type. DNA "cannot predict the facial appearance" [V abstract, Hayes et al. 2024]. The first settlers of Tonga should not be modeled closely on any one present-day population.
- **Tattooing: the practice is well founded; the designs are not known.** Obsidian flakes with tiny retouched points from seven Lapita sites in Papua New Guinea, the Solomons and Vanuatu carry wear and pigment showing they "had been used for tattooing" [V abstract, Torrence et al. 2017]. Four bone tattooing combs from a Tongatapu site are about 2,700 years old, "the oldest multi-toothed tattooing implements in the Pacific", though such combs are conspicuously absent "from nearly all early sites" [V abstract, Clark & Langley 2019]. **The link between the pottery stamp and tattoo is an interpretation:** Green argued that the same motifs ran across pottery, barkcloth and tattoo, and Kirch that the pots represent "tattooed ancestors" [V, Kirch 2019, describing his own and Green's arguments; Green 1979 itself UNVERIFIED as a record]; Burley lists it as one of three open questions [V, Burley 2023, p. 275], though he also writes that the Tongatapu combs "conclusively associate Polynesian tattooing with the arrival of Lapita canoes in Tonga" [V, Burley 2023, near p. 335: page not recorded]. No tattooed Lapita skin survives. A hint of dark patterning on an arm or leg is defensible at about 900–700 BC; any specific design or placement is invention.
- **Ornaments:** shell rings, bracelets and beads are attested at Nukuleka (above).
- **Dress: unknown.** No textile, barkcloth or garment survives. Faces on the pots may show skullcaps and feathered headdresses, but the author of that study links them to masks and ceremony [V, Spriggs 2019]: not everyday wear, and outside what this site shows. Simple wraps or plaited skirts of unspecified material, drawn plainly, are the least assertive choice; say in the caption that clothing is conjecture.
- Men, women and children together: these were colonizing families, and the dental and isotope work at Teouma covers both sexes [V, Kinaston et al. 2014].

**Avoid.**
- **Later Polynesia, 1,800 years or more after the scene:** the big double-hulled voyaging canoes of museum models and of the modern revival; tall crab-claw and lateen sails shown in confident detail; carved prows; moai, tiki figures and stone platforms; Hawaiian feather cloaks and helmets; Māori facial tattoo, carved meeting houses and greenstone; Samoan and Tongan full-body tattoo patterns; kava ceremonies; fire dancing; ukuleles.
- **Tourist Pacific:** grass skirts, flower garlands, hibiscus behind the ear, coconut-shell tops, outrigger racing canoes.
- **From the wrong site:** pigs or dogs at Nukuleka; tortoises and crocodiles in Tonga; New Britain obsidian at Nukuleka; a high volcanic island backdrop; the Teouma pots shown as they were found.
- **Under the site's own standards:** no burial, no skull, no pot in mortuary or ceremonial use, no masked or headdressed figure in a rite. **No likeness of a real person:** two facial approximations of Teouma individuals have been published [V abstract, Hayes et al. 2024]; do not copy them.
- **Wrong story:** people drifting or blown off course (the voyages carried plants and animals, so they were planned); a "lost" or "mysterious" people (their descendants live across the Pacific); an untouched paradise with no cost (the extinctions began at once); a conquest of other people (Remote Oceania was empty).
- Iron tools, woven cloth sails, pottery made on a wheel.

**Cautions.**
- **Where Lapita came from.** One pole: migrants from Taiwan through island Southeast Asia, moving fast (the "fast train"). The other: a culture that arose among the long-established peoples of the Bismarcks. Kirch says the big 1980s project led to "a rejection of both", in favor of intruders and locals forming something new there (Green's "Triple I": intrusion, innovation, integration) [V, Kirch 2019, p. 489]. Ancient DNA has since swung opinion toward migration for the *people* who first reached Remote Oceania [V, above], which does not settle where the pottery style and the rest of the culture took shape. A further argument, that the pottery came by way of the Mariana Islands, "remains unresolved" [V, Bedford et al. 2019, p. 5]. Whether "Lapita" was ever one bundled culture is itself questioned (Specht et al. 2014 [R]).
- **When, and how fast.** Start in the Bismarcks: 3470–3250 cal BP on Mussau by one model [V abstract, Denham et al. 2012], or about 3250–3150 cal BP by a stricter pruning of the dates, with which Kirch's own redating agrees [V, Bedford et al. 2019, p. 6]. Arrival in Remote Oceania: Vanuatu by 3250–3100 and Fiji by 3130–3010 cal BP (Denham et al.), against "not much more than 3000 BP" [V abstract, Sheppard et al. 2015] and "no earlier than 3000 cal. BP" [V, Sand et al. 2019]. On the short chronology, from the first move beyond the Solomons at about 3000 cal BP to Nukuleka at 2838 BP is roughly 150–200 years (my arithmetic, not a published figure): people "exploded out of the Bismarcks, and in a radiocarbon instant, occupied most of its ultimate range" [V, Sheppard 2010, quoted in Bedford et al. 2019, p. 6]. The old idea of a 400-year pause in the Bismarcks is, according to the same editors, "long gone".
- **Which way into Tonga.** By stages through Fiji is the expected route; the foreign sherds at Nukuleka suggest a direct founding voyage from farther west, which Burley et al. call unlikely on distance but not ruled out [V, Burley et al. 2010, p. 140]. Nukuleka's status as *the* founder colony is Burley's claim; I did not read his critics.
- **The long pause.** After Tonga and Samoa, no island farther east was settled for a very long time. East Polynesia was colonized from about AD 1025–1120 (Society Islands) and then AD 1190–1290 on the short chronology [V abstract, Wilmshurst et al. 2011], or from about AD 900 in the southern Cook Islands on another reading [V abstract, Sear et al. 2020]. Either way the gap is some 1,700–1,900 years, and its cause (canoe technology, winds, sea level, choice) is not agreed. The picture, ending at 400 BC, falls inside it: **Hawaii, Tahiti, Easter Island and New Zealand were all still uninhabited**, and the caption should not say "Polynesians settled the Pacific" as if it were done.
- **Dentate-stamped pottery was short-lived**, gone by about 2700–2500 cal BP everywhere [V, Bedford et al. 2019], and in Tonga the Lapita phase itself was "exceptionally short" [V abstract, Burley et al. 2015].
- Stilt houses in Tonga are an inference; the canoe is a reconstruction; see above.

**Wording.**
- **"Lapita"** is an archaeologists' label, taken from a site on New Caledonia (local name Xapeta'a [V, Sand et al. 2019]) and applied to a pottery style. It is not what anyone called themselves, and it is not an ethnic group. Plain forms: "the people who made Lapita pottery", "Lapita voyagers", "the Lapita culture". "The Lapita" as a tribal name is best avoided.
- **Places:** "Tonga" (formally the Kingdom of Tonga), island "Tongatapu"; "Vanuatu" (not New Hebrides), island "Efate"; "Bismarck Archipelago, Papua New Guinea". Burley writes "Sāmoa" with the macron. People of Vanuatu are "ni-Vanuatu" [V, used in Posth et al. 2018].
- **"Remote Oceania"** (the islands beyond the main Solomons, empty before Lapita) and "Near Oceania" are the neutral scholarly terms; "the remote Pacific" is a fair plain version. "Melanesia" and "Polynesia" are useful as place names; "Melanesians" and "Polynesians" as two contrasting races is a nineteenth-century scheme [R] that the DNA results above cut across. For 900 BC, say "the first settlers of Tonga" or "ancestors of the Polynesians", not "Polynesians".
- "Settled", "reached", "sailed to": not "discovered" where it implies accident, and never "primitive" or "Stone Age people". "Uninhabited" is right for Remote Oceania only: the Bismarcks and Solomons had been lived in for tens of thousands of years [R].
- Draft caption sense, for the editor to word: "Nearly 2,900 years ago, people who made a distinctive stamped pottery sailed into the uninhabited islands of the remote Pacific, carrying taro, bananas and chickens. This scene is set at Nukuleka in Tonga, the oldest known settlement in Polynesia. The pottery and shell tools are drawn from finds; the houses on stilts, the clothing and the canoe are reconstruction, and no canoe of this age has ever been found."

## Things I could not verify, gathered in one place

1. **Whether the 2838±8 "BP" for Nukuleka counts from 1950.** The paper (as the fetch tool read it) does not say. The calendar date, and "years ago" in any caption, depend on it. Burley's "900 BC" is the safe rounding.
2. **Nukuleka's own animal bones.** Chicken, iguana, megapodes and the absence of pig and dog are documented for Lapita Tonga generally and for Ha'ateiho on the same lagoon; I did not find or read a faunal list for Nukuleka itself. Burley's book mentions "an abundance of faunal bone" there without the list in the passages I read.
3. **The Hopoate plant identifications** rest on Burley's book reporting a specialist's analysis; the primary paper ("Burley et al. 2018") was not identified. Whether those plants date to first landfall or some generations later was not established. **Breadfruit and yam at about 900 BC in Tonga: no direct evidence found** (yam is at Talasiu, later).
4. **When sweet potato reached Tonga.** The pollen record quoted above shows only that it is later than a zone boundary dated AD 689–991; the date and route of its arrival in Polynesia rest on Roullier et al. 2013, read in abstract for the additions dossier and not re-read.
5. **Stilt houses at Nukuleka:** no structural remains; the inference is the excavator's, and the 2016 paper was read in abstract only. Nunn & Heorake 2009 (the Fijian case) was not located.
6. **Talepakemalai:** Kirch 2021 (the full monograph) and Kirch 2001 were not read; the house evidence is from his 2019 retrospective and one online supplement page read through the fetch tool. Whether Mussau itself was inhabited before Lapita was not checked.
7. **Canoe:** that no Lapita vessel or depiction exists is an absence I could not check directly. Anderson's argument for simpler early rigs: citation not confirmed. Horridge's views are known to me only as quoted by Pawley & Pawley. The print pagination of Pawley & Pawley 1998 was not checked.
8. **Pottery technique** (stamping leather-hard clay before firing; open firing without a kiln): standard, not read this session. The Academia Sinica online Lapita pottery database appeared in search results and was not opened.
9. **Green 1979** (pottery, barkcloth and tattoo): no Crossref record found; known only through Kirch and Burley. **Kirch 1997, pp. 132–144** on face motifs and tattoo: not read.
10. **Pigs in Lapita Fiji:** Burley's statement of absence is quoted; a faunal table for his own Fijian site (Kavewa) has a pig column with three bones in the surface layer, which I did not read around. Bourewa's fauna was not read.
11. **Dress, barkcloth, hair, body form:** nothing direct. Ambrose's chapter on plaited-textile patterns in the pottery (*Debating Lapita*, ch. 12) was not read.
12. **Which present-day populations the first settlers most resemble genetically** (Indigenous Taiwanese and northern Philippine groups are usually named): in the body of Skoglund et al. 2016, which I read only in abstract.
13. **Distances** not taken from a source read this session: the roughly 850 km gap between Vanuatu and Fiji; the height of Tongatapu.
14. **Later arrival dates of cassava, papaya, mango and other "wrong" plants; the nineteenth-century origin of "Melanesia/Polynesia" as racial terms; the 40,000-year occupation of the Bismarcks:** standard, not sourced this session.
15. **Critics of Nukuleka as the single founder colony,** and the dating of the earliest Samoan Lapita site: not read.
16. All items marked **[R]**. The highest-stakes ones: the Bourewa crops (Horrocks & Nunn 2007), and anything attributed to Kirch 1997.

One correction made on checking: the monograph on Talepakemalai is Kirch 2021 (Cotsen Institute), not to be confused with the 2001 volume of the same project (*Lapita and Its Transformations in Near Oceania*, Berkeley), which has no Crossref record that I found.
