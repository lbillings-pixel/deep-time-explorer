# Dossier: second-order extinction events, Mesozoic and Cenozoic

Status: complete draft, compiled 14 November 2026. Seven events. A consolidated list of what could not be verified is at the end.

## How to read this dossier

- **Citation checks.** Every citation marked **[CR]** was looked up in the Crossref database during this session (14 Nov 2026) and the authors, year, title, journal, volume, pages and DOI below are as Crossref returned them. For a few older Geological Society of America papers Crossref stores only the first page; in those cases the page range is flagged. Citations I could not confirm in Crossref or on a publisher page are marked **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Claim checks.** A citation being real does not mean I read the whole paper. Where a number or statement was read directly from the paper's full text (open-access copy) it is marked **[read]**. Where it comes from the paper's abstract, or from a later paper quoting it, that is said. Anything from my background knowledge that I did not confirm against a source this session is marked **[not checked]**.
- Wikipedia was used only to find leads and is not cited.

## 1. Carnian Pluvial Episode (CPE), Late Triassic

**Date.** About 234 to 232 Ma, inside the Carnian Stage (straddling the Julian–Tuvalian substage boundary). Both Bernardi et al. 2018 and Dal Corso et al. 2020 give "around 234 to 232 million years ago" [read]. Duration is not settled: about 1.2 Myr from cyclostratigraphy, versus about 1.6–1.7 Myr from integrated biostratigraphy (onset at the *Aonoides/Austriacum* ammonoid-zone boundary interval, end at the base of the *Subbullatus* Zone) — both figures as reported in Dal Corso et al. 2020 and Bernardi et al. 2018 [read]. Your recollection (~234–232 Ma) matches the literature. There is no single radiometric date pinned on the event itself; the ages come from correlation to dated ash beds elsewhere in the Carnian.

**Key sources.**
1. Simms, M.J. & Ruffell, A.H. (1989). Synchroneity of climatic change and extinctions in the Late Triassic. *Geology* 17(3): 265–268. DOI 10.1130/0091-7613(1989)017<0265:SOCCAE>2.3.CO;2 **[CR]** (Crossref stores first page 265 only; end page 268 not confirmed.) — The paper that proposed the event.

Extinction and dawn of the modern world in the Carnian (Late Triassic). *Science Advances* 6(38): eaba0099. DOI 10.1126/sciadv.aba0099 **[CR]** — Best recent review; open access. Also verified and useful: Ruffell, A., Simms, M.J. & Wignall, P.B. (2016). The Carnian Humid Episode of the late Triassic: a review. *Geological Magazine* 153(2): 271–284. DOI 10.1016/j.gr.2020.05.009 **[CR]** (found in Crossref; not read).

**What died.** All from Dal Corso et al. 2020 [read] unless stated:
- "Extinction of ~33% of … marine genera" across the Julian–Tuvalian boundary, calculated from Sepkoski's compendium.
- Marine invertebrate genus counts fall from 1129 (early Carnian) to 775 (late Carnian). The authors themselves warn that the drop may be exaggerated for some groups by uneven sampling.
- Conodonts: 40 species in the early Julian, 13 in the late Julian, 9 at the start of the Tuvalian.
- Ammonoids: high extinction rates in the late Julian. Crinoids (Encrinidae and others) "either went extinct or severely declined". Bryozoans, and benthic suspension feeders in general, declined.
- Bony fishes: a 51–62% decline.
- On land: high extinction rates in several herbivorous tetrapod groups, including rhynchosaurs and dicynodonts.
- Simms & Ruffell 1989 originally pointed to losses among ammonoids, crinoids and bryozoans, and a turnover in land plants and tetrapods (from the abstract and later reviews; original not read in full).

Numbers do not obviously disagree between sources, but there are few independent counts: the 33% figure is the only global genus-level number I found.

**What rose afterwards** (Dal Corso et al. 2020 [read]; Bernardi et al. 2018 [read]). Dinosaurs diversified; in the Dolomites track record, crurotarsan (crocodile-line) tracks make up 80–100% before the CPE, dinosaurs roughly 40% just after (early Tuvalian), and more than 90% by the late Tuvalian–Norian (Bernardi et al. 2018). Also first appearing or expanding around this time: reefs with scleractinian (modern-type) corals, early calcareous plankton (calcispheres), modern conifer families and bennettitaleans, the first large amber deposits, and early crocodylomorphs, turtles, lepidosaurs and mammals. Note for the writers: the claim is that dinosaurs *diversified* at the CPE, not that they originated then, and they did not dominate until after the end-Triassic extinction about 30 Myr later [the last clause is general knowledge, not checked this session].

**Proposed causes.** Leading hypothesis: eruption of the Wrangellia large igneous province (now in Alaska, Yukon and British Columbia), releasing CO2, causing warming, an intensified water cycle (hence "pluvial"), repeated negative carbon-isotope excursions, and local marine anoxia and a shutdown of carbonate platforms. This is the majority view but it is not proven: Dal Corso et al. 2020 write that "we can only speculate on the possible volcanic triggers" because the dating link between the basalts and the event is weak [read]. Other Tethyan volcanism is also mentioned. Well supported: the climate change itself (a switch to humid conditions at low latitudes, shown by river sandstones, palaeosols, pollen and amber) and the carbon-isotope excursions. Debated: the trigger, and whether the humidity was global.

**How disputed is it as an extinction?** Moderately. It was rejected early on by Visscher et al. 1994, who argued the German "pluvial" sandstones record a river system crossing a dry landscape rather than a climate change, and the event was largely ignored for twenty years. Since about 2012 it has been widely accepted as a real climatic and biotic turnover, but it is not one of the "Big Five", it is missed by most database analyses because of coarse time bins, its extinction numbers are few and sampling-sensitive, and the dating of the land record (including the dinosaur link) is admitted to be loose (Dal Corso et al. 2020 [read]). The 2016 review by Ruffell et al. says the evidence for humidity is "not always so clear" in South America, Antarctica and Australia (from a search summary of that review; not read in full).
- Dissent: Visscher, H., Van Houte, M., Brugman, W.A. & Poort, R.J. (1994). Rejection of a Carnian (Late Triassic) "pluvial event" in Europe. *Review of Palaeobotany and Palynology* 83(1–3): 217–226. DOI 10.1016/0034-6667(94)90070-1 **[CR]**.

**Key localities.**
- Dolomites, northern Italy (Southern Alps): Heiligkreuz Formation (the CPE interval, with amber and dinosaur-bearing track sites) overlain by the Travenanzes Formation and then the Dolomia Principale (Bernardi et al. 2018 [read]). Lead for a locality description, not verified in Crossref: Breda et al. 2009, *Geo.Alp* 6: 80–115 (UNVERIFIED).
- Northern Calcareous Alps, Austria (Lunz and Raibl beds); Lagonegro Basin, southern Italy (deep-water record).
- Germanic Basin: Schilfsandstein (Stuttgart Formation), a river sandstone inside the otherwise evaporitic Keuper.
- Wessex Basin, SW England (Mercia Mudstone Group; Miller et al. 2017).
- Nanpanjiang Basin, South China.
- Ischigualasto Formation, Argentina (tuff near base 231.4 ± 0.3 Ma) and Santa Maria Formation, Brazil (233.23 ± 0.73 Ma) for the earliest dinosaurs (ages as quoted by Bernardi et al. 2018 [read]).
- Wrangellia basalts: Vancouver Island, Yukon, Alaska.

**Facts a painter could use.**
- In the Dolomites the event is a visible change in the cliffs: pale reef and platform limestones and dolomites give way to a darker band of mudrock, marl and sandstone (the basal Heiligkreuz Formation is described as "dark mudrock and marlstone" laid down in an oxygen-poor basin; from a search summary of a 2022 core study, not read in full), with a "sudden increase of coarse and immature" sand and mud washed off the land (Bernardi et al. 2018 [read]). Above come the Travenanzes beds — widely described as red, green and violet mudstones of a dry coastal plain [colours not checked] — and then the huge pale tidal-flat dolomite of the Dolomia Principale.
- On land: a wet, forested lowland with conifers and bennettitaleans dripping resin (the Dolomites amber occurs as millimetre-sized droplets in ancient soils [droplet size not checked]); rivers carrying sand across what had been salt flats. Before: crocodile-line archosaurs, rhynchosaurs and dicynodonts leaving the tracks. After: small bipedal dinosaurs increasingly common in the trackways.
- In the sea: before, shallow tropical platforms built largely by microbes and sponges, with crinoid meadows and abundant ammonoids; during, muddy water and a pause in limestone building; after, the first small patch reefs with modern-type (scleractinian) corals (Dal Corso et al. 2020 [read]).

## 2. Early Toarcian extinction and Toarcian Oceanic Anoxic Event (T-OAE, "Jenkyns Event"), Early Jurassic

**Date.** Two linked pulses: one at the Pliensbachian–Toarcian stage boundary and a larger one about a million years later at the onset of the T-OAE.
- Pliensbachian–Toarcian boundary: 184.2 Ma in the Geologic Time Scale 2020 (as quoted in Al-Suwaidi et al. 2022 [read]); revised to 183.73 +0.35/−0.50 Ma by Al-Suwaidi et al. 2022 from U–Pb dated ash beds in Argentina [read]. So the sources differ by about half a million years.
- T-OAE onset: close to the *tenuicostatum*–*serpentinum* ammonite-zone boundary, dated at about 182.77 Ma by Al-Suwaidi et al. 2022 [read]. Most papers simply say "~183 Ma".
- Duration of the T-OAE carbon-isotope excursion: disputed, from about 300–500 kyr to about 900 kyr depending on the section and how the sedimentary cycles are counted (search summaries of Suan et al. 2008, Boulila et al. 2014 and 2019; papers not read). Overall published range roughly 0.2 to 1 Myr.
- Volcanism: Ferrar magmatism (Antarctica) lasted 349 ± 49 kyr, starting by 182.779 ± 0.033 Ma; Karoo magmatism (southern Africa) began as early as 183.246 ± 0.045 Ma (abstract of Burgess et al. 2015 as returned by a search; not read in full).
The commonly quoted figure of about 183 Ma is right.

**What died.** Mostly sea-floor invertebrates (bivalves, brachiopods, gastropods, ostracods, foraminifera), plus ammonites and belemnites; Al-Suwaidi et al. 2022 call it "a second-order extinction event affecting ammonites, belemnites, gastropods, and many other benthic and pelagic groups" [read].
- About 26% of marine genera globally — figure as given by Dunhill et al. 2024 [read], who attribute it to Danise et al. 2013 (*PLoS ONE* 8: e56255). I did not trace the number to its original calculation.
- About 60% of marine species, and 87% of benthic species, within the Cleveland Basin (Yorkshire) — Dunhill et al. 2024 [read], attributed to Little & Benton 1995 and Caswell et al. 2009. This is a regional figure, not a global one.
- Ammonites: 40–65% of species disappear in an average subzone through the interval, rising to 70–90% in the worst pulses, across a dataset of 772 species from NW Tethys and the Arctic (abstract of Dera et al. 2010, via search; not read in full).
- Family level: elevated family extinction "spanned five ammonite zones … an interval of ~7.5 m.y.", with most species extinctions in the early Toarcian (abstract of Little & Benton 1995, via search). An unverified secondary figure of "about 5% of families" also circulates.
- Tethyan reef corals: 49% of genera and 90.9% of species (a single search-result summary; source paper not identified — treat as UNVERIFIED).
- Recovery of the Yorkshire sea-floor community took about 7 Myr (Dunhill et al. 2024 [read]).
The global percentage is low compared with the Big Five; the striking numbers are regional (NW Europe).

**Proposed causes.** Consensus: eruption of the Karoo–Ferrar large igneous province (South Africa, Lesotho, Antarctica) released carbon, producing a large negative carbon-isotope excursion, rapid warming, an intensified water cycle, and widespread loss of oxygen in shelf seas, with black organic-rich muds as the result. Dunhill et al. 2024 summarise warming of "up to 13 °C in the mid-latitudes" (the figure comes from their cited sources; other estimates are lower) and add ocean acidification [read]. Debated: (a) how much of the carbon came from volcanic gas versus methane hydrates or magma baking coal and organic shale in the Karoo Basin; (b) whether the anoxia was truly global or concentrated in restricted European basins; (c) the exact timing between Karoo and Ferrar pulses and the two extinction steps.

**How disputed is it as an extinction?** The extinction is accepted as real, but two qualifications are standard. First, it is multi-phase and drawn out (a step at the stage boundary, a bigger step at the T-OAE, and elevated losses over several ammonite zones), so "event" compresses perhaps a million years or more (Little & Benton 1995; Dera et al. 2010). Second, the best record and the largest percentages come from NW Europe, where the sea floor was unusually stagnant; Hallam (1986) originally treated the extinction as a regional European event, and McArthur et al. (2008; and McArthur 2019) argued that the classic black shales reflect local basin restriction rather than a global anoxic ocean (both papers confirmed to exist — see list below — but I characterise them from their titles and from how later papers cite them, not from reading them).

**Key localities.**

**Facts a painter could use.**
- The rock itself: thin-bedded, finely laminated black shale, rich enough in organic matter to smell of bitumen and, in Germany, to have been burned as oil shale; lines of pale limestone nodules; brassy pyrite. In Yorkshire it weathers into dark grey cliffs and platforms, and holds jet — the black gemstone, which is fossil driftwood from monkey-puzzle-like conifers [jet origin not checked this session].
- The sea: a warm, shallow, greenish, plankton-rich surface over a dark, still, oxygen-starved floor. Life hung in the upper water: ichthyosaurs, squid-like belemnites, ammonites, and at Holzmaden great tangled colonies of sea lilies (crinoids) hanging beneath floating logs [Holzmaden details are well documented but not checked this session]. Carcasses sank into black mud undisturbed, which is why skeletons are complete.
- Before and after on the sea floor (Dunhill et al. 2024 [read]): before, a varied community of burrowing and surface-living bivalves, brachiopods, snails and others; after, a monotonous pavement of small, thin-shelled, surface-living bivalves (*Bositra buchii*, *Pseudomytiloides dubius*) — "low-diversity, high-abundance" opportunists tolerant of low oxygen.

## 3. Aptian events (Early Cretaceous): OAE 1a "Selli Event", and the supposed "Aptian extinction"

**Verdict up front.** I could not find any peer-reviewed paper that establishes a discrete "Aptian extinction event" at 116–117 Ma. The idea appears to trace back to one sentence in Raup & Sepkoski (1986): their genus-level data suggested "additional apparent extinction events in the Aptian (Cretaceous) and Pliocene stages" beyond the eight main peaks (abstract, read via the NASA technical reports copy). That is a statistical bump in a stage-level compilation, not a described event with a dated horizon, a victim list or a locality. What the literature *does* document in the Aptian are three separate things, at three different times, none of which is conventionally called a mass extinction: (a) OAE 1a at about 119.5 Ma, a carbon-cycle and ocean-oxygen crisis with a plankton abundance crash; (b) a mid-Aptian crisis of rudist–coral carbonate platforms; (c) a large turnover of planktonic foraminifera at the Aptian–Albian boundary, about 113 Ma. My recommendation: if the site includes this at all, present it as "OAE 1a, an ocean crisis without a clear mass extinction", not as an extinction event.

**Date.**
- OAE 1a onset: 119.55 +0.072/−0.079 Ma; duration 1116 +87/−93 kyr, from U–Pb dated ash beds in Hokkaido, Japan (Li et al. 2024, abstract read via Europe PMC). This is the best current figure.
- "~120 Ma" is the round number used in many papers (e.g. the title of Li et al. 2008).
- Caution on older literature: the age of the whole Aptian Stage has been revised downward by several million years in the last decade. Leandro et al. 2022 put the Barremian–Aptian boundary at about 120.2 Ma and the Aptian at about 7.2 Myr long (abstract read); the Geologic Time Scale 2020 (Gale et al. 2020) uses about 121.4 Ma for the base [GTS2020 figure from memory, not checked]. Papers written against older time scales place the base of the Aptian near 125–126 Ma and OAE 1a near 124–125 Ma [not checked]. So "OAE 1a at ~120 Ma" and "OAE 1a at ~125 Ma" are the same event on different time scales.
- "Aptian extinction, 117 Ma": no dated event found in the primary literature.

**What died.** No published percentage of genera or species lost that is specific to an "Aptian extinction" was found.
- OAE 1a: the "nannoconid crisis" is "a sharp decline in abundance of the nannoconids" (heavily calcified nannoplankton), i.e. a population crash and a temporary disappearance from sediments, not the extinction of the group (Matsumoto et al. 2025 [read]; Erba 1994, title). Some Pacific cores are barren of all calcareous microfossils through the worst interval (Matsumoto et al. 2025 [read]).
- Molluscs: Freymueller et al. 2019 found raised genus-level turnover "associated with several Cretaceous OAEs" but single out only OAE 2 as significantly worse than background (abstract, via search). So OAE 1a does not register as a clear extinction in that analysis.
- Mid-Aptian: collapse of rudist- and coral-built carbonate platforms around the Tethys, with losses among the rudist bivalves (the subject of Skelton & Gili 2012; I have not read it and give no numbers).
- Aptian–Albian boundary (~113 Ma): planktonic foraminifera suffered a major turnover; conference abstracts by Huber, Leckie and colleagues give "at least 70%" of late Aptian species lost, and "about 80%" at one drill site (ODP Site 1049). These figures are from 2008–2009 meeting abstracts (via search), not confirmed in the final 2011 paper.
- The Wikipedia article's statements about particular land animals disappearing are flagged there as lacking citations; I found nothing to support them and suggest not using them.

**Proposed causes.** For OAE 1a: massive submarine volcanism of the Ontong Java Plateau (with Manihiki and Hikurangi: "Ontong Java Nui") in the Pacific, releasing CO2, acidifying surface waters and fertilising the ocean. The evidence is a sharp shift in osmium isotopes in sediments at the start of the event (Tejada et al. 2009). This was the consensus until 2023, when Davidson et al. redated the plateau basalts as "as much as 10 million years younger" and concluded it was too young to have caused OAE 1a (abstract read). Li et al. 2024 and Matsumoto et al. 2025 argue back that sediment evidence still ties the volcanism to OAE 1a; Matsumoto et al. say the discrepancy with the basalt ages is unresolved [read]. So the cause is currently *actively disputed*. The Wikipedia link of a "117 Ma" extinction to the Rajmahal Traps / Kerguelen hotspot: I found no primary paper making that case for an extinction; a GSA Special Paper chapter by Erba et al. (2015) on the environmental consequences of Ontong Java and Kerguelen volcanism is cited by Matsumoto et al. 2025 but I could not check it (UNVERIFIED).

**How disputed is it as an extinction?** It is not established as an extinction event at all; it is a list entry descended from a 1986 statistical analysis. OAE 1a itself is undisputed as an environmental event and is one of the best-studied in the Cretaceous, but specialists describe its biology in terms of crises, turnovers and platform drownings, not mass extinction. Marshall's 2023 review mentions only an unspecified "mid-Cretaceous peak" among lesser extinction intervals [read].

**Key localities.**
- Umbria–Marche Basin, central Italy: the Selli Level in the Gorgo a Cerbara section and the Poggio le Guaine (PLG) core; Cismon core, Venetian Alps (Malinverno et al. 2010; Matsumoto et al. 2025 [read]).
- Cau core, SE Spain (Castro et al. 2021, *Paleoceanography and Paleoclimatology* 36: e2020PA004004, DOI 10.1029/2020PA004004 **[CR]**).
- Yezo Group, Hokkaido, Japan (dated ash beds; Li et al. 2024).

- Vocontian Basin, SE France ("Niveau Goguel") [not checked].

**Facts a painter could use.** (Weaker support than for other events; most of this needs checking.)
- The Selli Level in Italy is a band a metre or two thick of black shale and dark greenish radiolarian-rich beds, interrupting pale grey-white limestone and marl [thickness and colours not checked; Matsumoto et al. 2025 do describe a "greenish-gray interval" just below].
- Open ocean: nothing dramatic at the surface — a hot greenhouse sea, possibly discoloured by blooms; the drama is chemical. In the central Pacific, ash from submarine eruptions settled into the mud (Matsumoto et al. 2025 [read]).
- Shallow tropics before and after: broad white-sand carbonate platforms with thickets of rudists (large horn- and cup-shaped bivalves) and corals, which in many places drowned or were replaced by microbial crusts and orbitolinid foraminifera in the early–mid Aptian [not checked; see Skelton & Gili 2012].

## 4. Cenomanian–Turonian boundary event and Oceanic Anoxic Event 2 (OAE 2, "Bonarelli Event"), Late Cretaceous

**Date.** Cenomanian–Turonian boundary: 93.90 ± 0.15 Ma (Meyers et al. 2012; figure as quoted in later papers found by search — I did not open the paper itself). An earlier time scale (GTS2004) used 93.55 Ma, so older papers differ by about 0.35 Myr. OAE 2 began in the latest Cenomanian and ended in the earliest Turonian: the carbon-isotope excursion lasted roughly 500–600 kyr (563–601 kyr from the orbital time scale of Sageman et al. 2006, as quoted in a 2009 conference abstract), though longer estimates of up to about 0.9 Myr exist depending on how the end is defined [upper figure from memory, not checked]. Most of the extinction therefore falls *before* the stage boundary, in the last half-million years of the Cenomanian. Your recollection (~94 Ma) is right.

**What died.** Numbers vary a great deal with region and method, and I could trace none of them back to the original page; each is given with the route by which I found it.
- About 26–27% of marine animal genera globally — attributed to Sepkoski and Raup's compilations (as quoted in a 2008 conference abstract and a 2004 journal paper found by search).
- About 25% of genera and 33–53% of species — attributed to Bambach 2006 (as quoted in a 2023 paper found by search).
- 79% of macro-invertebrate species in the US Western Interior seaway — attributed to Harries & Little 1999 (as quoted by Monnet & Bucher 2007, via search summary).
- 45–75% of species in non-tropical faunas, depending on group — Kauffman, in a 1995 US National Academies volume (via search).
- "53% of marine species" is described by Keller et al. 2008 as the original invertebrate-based claim, which they say the plankton record does not support (via search).
Group by group:
- **Ichthyosaurs** — the one major group that ended for good. Fischer et al. 2016 [read]: the extinction was "abrupt yet staggered", in two phases — a major loss in the earliest Cenomanian and a final disappearance by the end of the Cenomanian ("in the earliest Turonian at the latest"), after a history of about 157 Myr. The last were large platypterygiine apex predators; late specimens come from the USA, Russia and Germany. The share of marine-reptile-bearing formations containing ichthyosaurs falls from 84% (Albian) to 19% (Cenomanian) to 0% (Turonian).
- **Pliosaurs** — did *not* die at the boundary. The last known pliosaurs (brachauchenines such as *Megacephalosaurus* and *Brachauchenius*) are from the middle Turonian of Kansas, a few million years later (Schumacher et al. 2013; Madzia et al. 2019 — from titles and search summaries). They were already low in diversity. Writers should say pliosaurs "faded out shortly afterwards", not "were killed by OAE 2".
- **Rudists** — the reef-building bivalves suffered a platform collapse: tropical rudist extinctions began in the Cenomanian and peaked 0.5–1 Myr before the boundary, and the major Early Cretaceous reef-building lineages were largely gone by the middle–late Cenomanian (Kauffman 1995, via search; Philip & Airaud-Crumiere 1991, from title). Rudists as a group survived, re-radiated, and lasted to the end of the Cretaceous [not checked].
- **Inoceramids** — the big flat clams of the chalk seas. In the Western Interior, "ammonites and inoceramids accounted for most of" the species loss (attributed to early work by Koch, via the 1995 National Academies volume). The group survived, with a turnover to new forms (*Mytiloides*) in the earliest Turonian [not checked], and went extinct later in the Cretaceous [not checked].
- **Ammonites** — heavy species turnover, but the decline began around the middle–late Cenomanian boundary, about 0.75 Myr before the anoxic deposits, and partly reflects fewer new species arising rather than more dying (Monnet & Bucher 2007 and Monnet et al. 2003, via search summary).
- Also: benthic and some planktonic foraminifera, and belemnites and other groups declining stepwise through the Cenomanian (Fischer et al. 2016 [read]).

**Proposed causes.** Broad agreement on the chain: a burst of submarine volcanism from a large igneous province (the Caribbean plateau and/or the High Arctic province) added CO2 and nutrients; the already very warm, high-sea-level greenhouse ocean became more productive and lost oxygen at depth over wide areas; black organic-rich muds accumulated. The volcanic trigger rests on osmium-isotope evidence (Turgeon & Creaser 2008, title; later work via search). Debated: which volcanic province; and whether anoxia was the actual killer. Monnet & Bucher 2007 argue from ammonites that the decline started before the anoxia, so something else (sea-level or temperature change) began it. Fischer et al. 2016 attribute the ichthyosaur extinction to long-term "climatic volatility" acting on a group with low evolutionary rates, and reject older ideas of competition from mosasaurs (the first large mosasaurs appear in the middle Turonian, about 3 Myr too late) or a collapse of belemnite prey [read].

**How disputed is it as an extinction?** Seriously disputed, more than any other event in this dossier except the Aptian. Gale et al. 2000 studied the chalk seas of England and France bed by bed and concluded the faunal change was "largely a pattern of immigration–emigration rather than true extinction", and that the event "is most probably an artifact" of a change in the kind of sediment preserved as sea level rose (abstract, via search). Smith et al. 2001 generalised the point: the western European record has "all the predicted signatures" of a rock-record bias. Against that, Freymueller et al. 2019, using a global mollusc database, found the diversity drop and extinction rate at OAE 2 "significantly more severe than Cretaceous background" (abstract, via search), and the ichthyosaur extinction is real. A fair summary: a real but modest and regionally uneven extinction, stretched over the Cenomanian rather than concentrated at the boundary, whose size in the classic sections is inflated by sampling effects.

- Rock Canyon, near Pueblo, Colorado, USA: the official boundary reference section (GSSP), in the Bridge Creek Limestone Member of the Greenhorn Formation (Kennedy et al. 2005; Sageman et al. 2006).

- Gubbio and the Umbria–Marche Apennines, Italy: the "Bonarelli Level" (Livello Bonarelli), the type black shale of OAE 2 [not checked this session].

**Facts a painter could use.**
- The Bonarelli Level at Gubbio is the textbook image: a band about a metre thick of black, paper-thin shale and dark chert slicing through white and pale pink pelagic limestone [thickness and colours not checked]. In England the equivalent is a grey-green marly band within brilliant white chalk; at Pueblo, a rhythmic staircase of pale limestone ledges and dark shale [not checked].
- A hothouse world with no polar ice and sea level so high that shallow seas covered much of Europe and split North America in two. One 2024 study of rudist shells reports early Turonian sea-surface temperatures of 41–45 °C (Křížová et al. 2024, via search summary only — treat as a single unverified estimate).
- Before: dolphin-shaped ichthyosaurs up to several metres long and huge short-necked pliosaurs as top predators, over sea floors with rudist thickets (tropics) or inoceramid clams the size of dinner plates. After: no ichthyosaurs; the first small mosasaurs appearing within about 3 Myr (Fischer et al. 2016 [read]); sharks, fast bony fishes and short-necked polycotylid plesiosaurs in the predator roles.

## 5. Eocene–Oligocene extinction / turnover and the "Grande Coupure"

**Date.** Eocene–Oligocene boundary: 33.9 Ma, defined at Massignano, Italy, by the extinction of the hantkeninid planktonic foraminifera (Hutchinson et al. 2021 [read], using the GTS2012 time scale). The climatic "Eocene–Oligocene transition" (EOT) is a longer interval of about 790 kyr, from 34.44 Ma to 33.65 Ma; the main step of Antarctic ice growth (the "earliest Oligocene oxygen isotope step", formerly "Oi-1") peaks at about 33.65 Ma, i.e. roughly 250 kyr *after* the boundary (Hutchinson et al. 2021 [read]). The Grande Coupure in Europe is placed in the earliest Oligocene, coincident with that glaciation step (Hooker et al. 2004, abstract via search). Weppe et al. 2023 give the EOT as about 34.1–33.55 Ma (abstract read). In Afro-Arabia the mammal losses come later still, with diversity lowest by about 30 Ma (de Vries et al. 2021 [read]). Your figure (~33.9 Ma) is the boundary; the biological events cluster in the few hundred thousand years after it.

**What died.**
- Marine plankton: the hantkeninids — spiny planktonic foraminifera — went extinct, "all five morphospecies and two genera" apparently together, about two-thirds of the way through the transition; the cause is unknown. Larger benthic foraminifera also suffered extinctions within the transition (Hutchinson et al. 2021 [read]; Pearson et al. 2008).
- Marine molluscs: turnover of mollusc species on the US Gulf coastal plain "was over 90%" (Ivany et al. 2000, abstract via search; a university press release says "almost 90 percent"). This is turnover in one region, not a global extinction percentage.
- European mammals (Grande Coupure): among the endemic European even-toed hoofed mammals (artiodactyls), "77% of species" went extinct, from a dataset of 90 species and more than 2,100 occurrences in the Quercy region of SW France (Weppe et al. 2023, abstract read). Many endemic European families were replaced by immigrants from Asia (Hutchinson et al. 2021 [read]). Hooker et al. 2004 found that in southern England mammal diversity "show[s] a sharp reduction … only after the Grande Coupure" (abstract via search).
- Afro-Arabian mammals: about 63% of lineages of primates, rodents and hyaenodont carnivores lost between the late Eocene and about 30 Ma ("almost two-thirds") (de Vries et al. 2021 [read]).
- Asia: the "Mongolian Remodelling", a shift from faunas dominated by large odd-toed browsers to ones dominated by rodents and rabbits (Meng & McKenna 1998; described as synchronous with the Grande Coupure in Hutchinson et al. 2021 [read]; the faunal description itself not checked).
- North American mammals: "no clear response" at the transition (Hutchinson et al. 2021 [read]).
- No single global percentage of genera lost at the boundary was found in the sources I read.

**Proposed causes.** Consensus: global cooling and the first continent-wide Antarctic ice sheet. Figures from Hutchinson et al. 2021 [read]: deep-sea cooling of about 2 °C at the first step; sea-surface cooling of 1–5 °C in many records; land cooling estimates of about 3 to 8 °C in North America; sea-level fall of about 70 m; an Antarctic ice sheet 70–110% of its modern volume. The best-supported driver is a fall in atmospheric CO2 (models fit best with a drop of about 40%, which the authors call an upper estimate), following DeConto & Pollard 2003. The older idea that the opening of ocean gateways around Antarctica (Tasman Gateway, Drake Passage) thermally isolated the continent (Kennett 1977) is now regarded as secondary: the Tasman Gateway deepened too early and deep circumpolar flow through Drake Passage came too late [read]. For Europe specifically, two things combined: climate (cooling and, especially, stronger seasonality — Ivany et al. 2000 found Gulf Coast winters became about 4 °C colder; Weppe et al. 2023 point to increased seasonality) and the arrival of Asian mammals once the Turgai seaway between Europe and Asia closed, itself helped by the sea-level fall. Debated: how much of the European turnover was competition from immigrants versus climate; Weppe et al. 2023 find no support for active competition and describe "passive or opportunistic replacement".

**How disputed is it as an extinction?** The climate event is undisputed; calling it a single "extinction event" is not standard. It is usually called a turnover, and it is strongly regional: severe in Europe and Afro-Arabia, marked in Asia, weak in North America. Prothero's 1994 review is generally cited for the view that the extinctions were stepwise and spread over millions of years from the middle Eocene to the early Oligocene rather than concentrated at the boundary [characterisation from memory of how the paper is cited; not checked this session]. An older objection specific to Europe: Sigé & Vianey-Liaud (1979) argued the Grande Coupure was unsuitable as a marker for the Eocene–Oligocene boundary (*Newsletters on Stratigraphy* 8(1): 79–82, DOI 10.1127/nos/8/1979/79 **[CR]**, title only), and indeed it falls slightly after the boundary as now defined.

**Key localities.**
- Massignano quarry, near Ancona, Italy: boundary reference section (GSSP) (Premoli Silva & Jenkins 1993; Hutchinson et al. 2021 [read]).
- Isle of Wight / Hampshire Basin, England: Solent Group (Bembridge Marls, Hamstead beds) — the Grande Coupure in a continuous coastal-plain record (Hooker et al. 2004).
- Quercy phosphorites, SW France: fissure fillings with thousands of mammal fossils (Weppe et al. 2023). Paris Basin (Stehlin's original area). Eastern Ebro Basin, NE Spain (Costa et al. 2011).
- Tanzania (coastal drill cores with exceptionally preserved plankton; Pearson et al. 2008). Deep-sea cores, e.g. DSDP Site 522, South Atlantic (Hutchinson et al. 2021 [read]).
- US Gulf Coast, Mississippi–Alabama (molluscs and fish ear-stones; Ivany et al. 2000).
- Fayum Depression, Egypt, for Afro-Arabian mammals [locality not confirmed in the part of de Vries et al. 2021 that I read].
- Mongolian Plateau (Meng & McKenna 1998).

**Facts a painter could use.**
- Europe before: an archipelago of warm, wet, forested islands ("insular western Europe" in Weppe et al.'s title) with its own peculiar endemic mammals — tapir-like palaeotheres, stocky anoplotheres, small rabbit-sized cainotheres [group examples not checked]. Europe after: cooler and markedly more seasonal, more open woodland, and populated by newcomers that had walked from Asia — early rhinoceroses, hamsters and other rodents, modern-type carnivores and ruminants [examples not checked].
- Antarctica is the other half of the picture: forested coasts with mountain glaciers before; an ice sheet reaching the sea, and a global shoreline about 70 m lower, after (sea-level figure from Hutchinson et al. 2021 [read]; the forested-Antarctica image not checked).
- In the sea the victims are microscopic: hantkeninids were planktonic foraminifera with long hollow spines, like tiny spiked stars [morphology not checked]. On the Gulf Coast, rich warm-water shell beds give way to poorer ones.

## 6. Pliocene marine megafauna extinction (and the fate of *Otodus megalodon*)

**Verdict up front.** This is a recent, single-study proposal, not an established event with a dated horizon. It is better supported than the Aptian case — there is a dedicated paper with numbers — but it describes elevated losses somewhere within the Pliocene (5.33–2.58 Ma), not a pulse at the Pliocene–Pleistocene boundary, and the most famous supposed victim, megalodon, may have died out about a million years before the boundary. The Wikipedia list entry "Pliocene–Pleistocene boundary extinction, 2 Ma" appears to descend from the same sentence in Raup & Sepkoski (1986) as the Aptian entry ("additional apparent extinction events in the Aptian … and Pliocene stages"), plus Stanley's 1980s work on Atlantic molluscs.

**Date.** No single date. Pimiento et al. 2017 compare the Pliocene as a whole with the Pleistocene: 36% of Pliocene marine megafauna genera did not survive into the Pleistocene (abstract via search). The Pliocene–Pleistocene boundary is 2.58 Ma (Gibbard et al. 2010). For megalodon the two published estimates disagree:
- about 2.6 Ma (modal estimate; range to 3.5 Ma), from 42 records judged reliable (Pimiento & Clements 2014 [read]);
- about 3.5 Ma (3.51 Ma), i.e. near the early/late Pliocene boundary at 3.6 Ma, after re-examining the record and rejecting or re-dating several of the youngest occurrences (Boessenecker et al. 2019 [read]; the paper calls the figure a median in one place and a mode in another).
The later study is a direct correction of the earlier one, and I would treat ~3.6 Ma as the current best estimate while noting the disagreement. Regional mollusc extinctions in the western Atlantic began in the late Pliocene and continued into the early Pleistocene (Stanley 1986, abstract via search).

**What died.** From the abstract of Pimiento et al. 2017 (via search): large marine animals — marine mammals, seabirds, sea turtles and sharks — with extinction rates "three times higher" than in the rest of the Cenozoic; 36% of Pliocene genera lost; among the coastal genera (86% of the total), seven "functional entities" (14%) and 17% of functional richness disappeared. Press coverage of the paper gives a breakdown — marine mammals 55%, sea turtles 43%, seabirds 35%, sharks 9% — which I could not confirm against the paper itself: treat as UNVERIFIED until checked in the full text or supplement. Individual victims usually named include megalodon, several dugongs, small baleen whales, odd walrus-faced dolphins and the swimming sloth *Thalassocnus* [list from general knowledge, not checked]. Separately, western Atlantic molluscs: of 361 early Pliocene bivalve species studied, about 65% were lost in the eastern United States after allowing for normal background turnover (Stanley 1986, figure as quoted in a 2019 *Palaios* paper via search); "about 70 percent of tropical western Atlantic mollusk species have become extinct since the Pliocene" (Allmon et al. 1993, abstract read).

**Proposed causes.** Pimiento et al. 2017: loss of shallow coastal habitat as ice-age sea-level swings began — "from the late Pliocene onwards, the global area of the neritic zone significantly diminished and exhibited amplified fluctuations" (abstract via search). For megalodon specifically: cooling and range fragmentation, and competition with the newly evolved great white shark (Boessenecker et al. 2019 [read]). For Atlantic molluscs: cooling, with the extinction acting as "a thermal filter" that removed purely tropical species (Stanley 1986, abstract via search). A nearby supernova irradiating large animals has been proposed (Melott et al. 2019) and is labelled a hypothesis by its own authors; it is not a mainstream explanation. None of these is a settled consensus.

**How disputed is it as an extinction?** I found no paper flatly rejecting the Pimiento et al. 2017 result, but it rests on one database analysis that compares whole epochs, so it cannot show a sudden event, and it has not (as far as I could find) been independently replicated. Boessenecker et al. 2019 undercut the emblematic case by moving megalodon's extinction back to about 3.5 Ma and noting it "may also suggest a globally asynchronous extinction" [read]. For molluscs, Allmon et al. 1993 showed that although most Pliocene species are gone, "regional diversity has not changed since the Pliocene" because new species arose as fast as old ones died (abstract read) — a turnover, not a collapse. Honest label: "a proposed interval of elevated extinction among large marine animals, linked to the onset of the ice ages".

**Key localities.**
- California and Baja California: Purisima, Capistrano, San Diego and Tirabuzón formations (Boessenecker et al. 2019 [read]).
- Lee Creek Mine, North Carolina (Yorktown Formation) — abundant megalodon teeth and whales (Boessenecker et al. 2019 [read]).
- Florida: Pinecrest Beds near Sarasota, enormously rich shell beds (Allmon et al. 1993, abstract).
- Pisco Basin / Sacaco, Peru (whales, seals, swimming sloths, sharks; mentioned in Boessenecker et al. 2019 as late Miocene for megalodon).
- Tuscany, Italy (Dominici et al. 2018, title).

**Facts a painter could use.**
- Setting: warm, shallow, sunlit shelf seas and broad coastal embayments, wider than today's because sea level was higher and there was little northern ice; then, after about 2.6 Ma, shorelines repeatedly retreating far out as ice sheets grew (the mechanism in Pimiento et al. 2017).
- Megalodon is known almost entirely from teeth — serrated triangles as big as a hand — and a few vertebrae; body length estimates of around 15 m or more are reconstructions [sizes not checked this session]. In its last million years it shared the sea with the first true great white sharks (Boessenecker et al. 2019 [read]).
- Before and after: Pliocene baleen whales were mostly small to medium-sized; "after this extinction, baleen whales … reached modern gigantic sizes" (Pimiento & Clements 2014 [read]; they stop short of claiming cause and effect).

## 7. Late Pleistocene (late Quaternary) megafauna extinctions

**Date.** Mostly between about 50,000 and 10,000 years ago, but staggered by continent and continuing on islands into the last thousand years. Barnosky et al. 2004: fifty thousand years ago the continents held "more than 150 genera of megafauna (animals >44 kg)"; "by 10,000 years ago, at least 97 of those genera were gone" [read from the PDF]; they stress that the event "was spread over more than 50,000 years globally". Svenning et al. 2024 frame it as the Late Pleistocene (129,000–11,700 years ago) to Middle Holocene (to 4,200 years ago), concentrated in the last ~50,000 years [read]. Your range (~50,000 to ~10,000 years ago) is the standard one.

More recent species-level figures (Svenning et al. 2024 [read]): only 11 of 57 megaherbivore species (1,000 kg or more) survived to AD 1000 — an 81% loss; 134 known extinctions among land mammals of 100 kg or more; very few extinctions among mammals under 10 kg. In Australia every land animal of 50 kg or more went extinct. The losses were therefore strongly size-selective. The two sets of numbers use different units (genera over 44 kg versus species in other size classes) and should not be mixed.
Timing (Svenning et al. 2024 [read]; Barnosky et al. 2004 [read]): Australia and New Guinea mainly 60,000–40,000 years ago (Roberts et al. 2001 put it at about 46,000); northern Eurasia in pulses across roughly 60,000–5,000 years ago; the Americas from about 20,000–15,000 years ago, with the main North American pulse at 11,500–10,000 radiocarbon years ago (roughly 13,000–11,500 calendar years [my conversion, approximate]); islands later — Madagascar about 1,000 years ago, New Zealand about 600 years ago.
Familiar victims [examples from general knowledge, not checked this session]: Australia — *Diprotodon* (rhino-sized wombat relative), giant kangaroos, the marsupial "lion" *Thylacoleo*, the giant bird *Genyornis*, the giant lizard *Megalania*. North America — mammoths, mastodons, ground sloths, glyptodonts, sabre-toothed cats, dire wolves, native horses and camels. South America — giant ground sloths (*Megatherium*), glyptodonts, *Toxodon*, *Macrauchenia*, gomphotheres. Eurasia — woolly mammoth, woolly rhinoceros, cave bear, cave lion, giant deer, straight-tusked elephant. All proboscideans and ground sloths in the Americas were lost (Svenning et al. 2024 [read]).

**Proposed causes, and the state of the debate.**
- *Humans* (hunting, plus fire and habitat change): the extinctions track the arrival of *Homo sapiens* continent by continent; they are size-selective in the way hunting models predict; similar climate swings in earlier glacial cycles did not produce them; and they are mildest in Africa and southern Asia where animals had long co-existed with hominins (Svenning et al. 2024 [read]; Sandom et al. 2014; Lemoine et al. 2023; Smith et al. 2018).
- *Climate*: rapid warming events correlate with population replacements and extinctions in ancient-DNA records from the northern continents (Cooper et al. 2015); in North America megafauna declines correlate with climate rather than with estimated human population size (Stewart et al. 2021); in Australia–New Guinea some argue the losses were staggered and largely preceded humans (Wroe et al. 2013). Each of these has been contested (Saltré et al. 2016 for Australia; the comment on Cooper et al.; Svenning et al. 2024 generally).
- *Both together*: Barnosky et al. 2004 concluded that human impact combined with climate change in the north, that Australian evidence pointed to humans, and that data for South America and Africa were insufficient [read]. Many researchers still hold a combined view.
- *An extraterrestrial impact about 12,900 years ago* (the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis): rejected by most specialists (Holliday et al. 2023), though its proponents continue to publish replies.
Where consensus lies: there is no full consensus. The weight of recent global analyses favours humans as the main or necessary cause, with climate as a contributing factor in some regions (most plausibly northern Eurasia and Beringia); a substantial minority argues climate was primary in particular regions. A fair wording for the public is "humans were very probably the decisive factor on most continents, with climate change contributing; researchers still argue about the balance".

**How disputed is it as an extinction?** Not disputed at all as a real loss — the animals are gone and many are dated by radiocarbon. What is disputed is the cause, and whether it should be called one event: it unfolded over tens of thousands of years, at different times on different continents, and arguably continues today.

- Rancho La Brea tar pits, Los Angeles; Clovis-age mammoth kill sites in the US Southwest and Plains.
- Permafrost of Siberia, Alaska and Yukon (frozen carcasses, ancient DNA; Cooper et al. 2015); Wrangel Island, the last mammoths.
- Naracoorte Caves and Lake Callabonna, South Australia; Cuddie Springs, New South Wales, a disputed late-survival site (mentioned in Barnosky et al. 2004 [read]).

**Facts a painter could use.** [General knowledge unless marked; check before use.]
- Northern scene: the "mammoth steppe" — a cold, dry, bright, largely treeless grassland with herbs and dwarf shrubs under big skies, stretching from western Europe across Siberia to Yukon, with woolly mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, horses, bison, saiga and reindeer, and lions and hyenas. Afterwards: wet tundra, bog and dark conifer forest, and far fewer large animals.
- Australian scene: about 45,000–50,000 years ago, dry open woodland and shrubland with two-tonne *Diprotodon*, short-faced giant kangaroos, a flightless bird taller than a person, and people newly arrived. No ice, no mammoths.
- The Americas around 13,000 years ago: retreating ice sheets to the north, a mosaic of spruce parkland and grassland, and a mix that looks impossible today — elephants (mammoths and mastodons), car-sized armoured glyptodonts, ground sloths that could rear up to browse trees, camels and horses, alongside the first small bands of human hunters. Within a few thousand years the largest animal left in North America was the bison (72% of large-mammal genera gone — Barnosky et al. 2004 [read]).

1. **Stehlin 1909** (the paper that named the Grande Coupure): exists and is universally cited, but has no DOI; sources disagree on volume number (9 vs 19) and year (1909 vs 1910).
2. **Schlanger & Jenkyns 1976**: no DOI; confirmed only through the journal archive and a university repository as reported by a web search.
3. **Page ranges for four older GSA *Geology* papers** (Simms & Ruffell 1989; Little & Benton 1995; Sageman et al. 2006; Pearson et al. 2008): Crossref stores only the first page. End pages given here are from other papers' reference lists or memory.
4. **Article numbers for several AGU papers** (McArthur et al. 2008; Malinverno et al. 2010; Leckie et al. 2002; Jenkyns 2010): Crossref returns the DOI but not the article number; numbers given are from memory. The DOIs are confirmed.
5. **The 26% global genus-loss figure for the Toarcian**: traced only as far as Dunhill et al. 2024 citing Danise et al. 2013.
6. **All global percentages for the Cenomanian–Turonian event** (26–27% genera; 33–53% species; 79% Western Interior species): found only as quoted by later authors; I did not see them in the original papers.
7. **The per-group percentages for the Pliocene event** (marine mammals 55%, turtles 43%, seabirds 35%, sharks 9%): found only in press coverage of Pimiento et al. 2017.
8. **Prothero 1994's argument** and **Elder 1989's results**: papers confirmed to exist; contents not read.
9. **Any primary source for an "Aptian extinction" at 116–117 Ma or its link to the Rajmahal Traps**: none found.
10. **Breda et al. 2009 (*Geo.Alp*)** and **Erba et al. 2015 (GSA Special Paper 511)**: seen cited by others, not confirmed in Crossref.
11. **Most "painter" details marked [not checked]**: these are standard textbook descriptions that I am fairly confident of but did not confirm against a source in this session. They are flagged individually above.
