# Dossier: two additions for Colorado and the Rocky Mountains

Research notes behind the site's list of mountain-building events, not text written for visitors, except the lines headed "For visitors" and "Caveat for visitors". Compiled 15 November 2026. Companion to `docs/research/tectonic-orogenies.md` and written to the same conventions.

Part 1 proposes a new entry, the Ancestral Rocky Mountains. Part 2 answers whether, and how, the site may show high ground after a mountain-building event has ended, first for the Colorado and Wyoming Rockies and then in general.

# Part 1. The Ancestral Rocky Mountains

## 1. Names

Ancestral Rocky Mountains (often "ARM"); Ancestral Rockies; "greater Ancestral Rocky Mountains" when the Oklahoma and Texas uplifts are counted (Ye et al. 1996) [abstract]. The two main Colorado uplifts are the ancestral Front Range ("Frontrangia" in older writing [memory]) and the Uncompahgre uplift ("Uncompahgria" [memory]). It is an intraplate event: basement blocks pushed up far inside the continent, not a belt along a plate edge (Sweet et al. 2021; Brotherton et al. 2022) [abstract].

## 2. Age: use 320 to 290 Ma

Published estimates of the start run from about 330 to 315 Ma, and of the end from about 299 to about 270 Ma. The spread is mostly about what counts as the end: the last fault movement, or the burial of the highlands.

- **Start.**
  - "beginning in the Chesterian-Morrowan" (latest Mississippian to earliest Pennsylvanian, roughly 330 to 318 Ma) across the whole system (Ye et al. 1996) [abstract; Ma conversion from memory].
  - "basins largely initiated in the late Morrowan to early Atokan (Bashkirian)", calibrated to the 2020 time scale, from subsidence curves in eight basins; the start was "synchronous" (Sweet et al. 2021) [abstract]. The Bashkirian runs from 323.4 to about 315 Ma, so late Morrowan to early Atokan is about 318 to 315 Ma [secondary and memory].
  - In New Mexico the onset was "relatively synchronous, predominantly beginning in early Atokan time" (Brotherton et al. 2022) [abstract].
  - The Anadarko Basin of Oklahoma began subsiding earlier, in the Late Mississippian (Sweet et al. 2021) [abstract].
  - The ancestral Front Range arch "began developing in early Pennsylvanian time"; the Uncompahgre uplift proper formed later, "in late Pennsylvanian and early Permian time" (Kluth 2007, conference abstract) [secondary].
- **Peak.** Middle Pennsylvanian. Deformation "culminating" in Middle Pennsylvanian time (Kluth and Coney 1981) [abstract]; highest subsidence rates "from the latest Atokan through the Desmoinesian" (Sweet et al. 2019, conference abstract) [secondary]; most basins reached peak subsidence in the Missourian (Sweet et al. 2021) [abstract]. Roughly 312 to 304 Ma [memory conversion].
- **End.**
  - "ending in the Wolfcampian (although minor post-Wolfcampian deformation occurs locally)" (Ye et al. 1996) [abstract].
  - Subsidence "shut off across most of the orogen by the end of the Wolfcampian (Asselian to early Artinskian)", that is by about 290 to 288 Ma; movement on most faults had stopped and the highlands were being buried in the early Permian, so "classical ARM tectonics is mostly a Pennsylvanian event" (Sweet et al. 2021) [abstract]. On that strict reading the building ended near 299 Ma.
  - Early Permian strata in New Mexico "onlap preexisting faults and folds" and are post-orogenic (Brotherton et al. 2022) [abstract].
  - At Manitou Springs the ancestral Ute Pass fault stopped moving in "Middle to Late Pennsylvanian time" (Sweet and Soreghan 2010) [abstract].
  - Later figures: the system formed "during Early Pennsylvanian to middle Permian time", with deformation that "slowed in Permian time" (Leary et al. 2020) [abstract]; syntectonic sediment beside the Uncompahgre uplift is "Desmoinesian to Wolfcampian (about 310 to 260 Ma)" on the older time scale that paper used (Barbeau 2003) [abstract]; subsidence renewed in the southern (Texas and New Mexico) basins in the Leonardian, about 288 to 273 Ma (Sweet et al. 2021) [abstract; Ma from secondary].
- **Why 320 and 290.** 320 is the rounded earliest Pennsylvanian, inside every estimate for Colorado. 290 is the rounded end of the Wolfcampian, the end given by Ye et al. and the time by which Sweet et al. find subsidence shut off. Mountains still stood in the earliest Permian even if the faults were quiet: Lower Permian strata "bury as much as 1000 m of relief atop the paleohighlands" (Soreghan et al. 2012) [abstract]. If the entry is meant to mark fault-driven uplift only, use 320 to 299.

## 3. Cause (debated)

No model is accepted. All agree the uplifts were made by squeezing of the continental interior, roughly north-east to south-west (Ye et al. 1996; Sweet and Soreghan 2010; Leary et al. 2017) [abstract]. The dispute is over where the push came from.

- **Collision to the south-east.** North America collided with South America and Africa along the Ouachita-Marathon belt (Arkansas, Oklahoma, Texas) as the supercontinent Pangaea was assembled. Kluth and Coney 1981 proposed that a wedge of the continent's interior "was pushed northwestward by the progressive collision-suturing", and compared it with Asia deforming far inland from India's collision [abstract]. Dickinson and Lawton 2003 tied the timing of each basin to the suture closing "from east to west" [abstract].
- **Objection.** "The nature, timing, and orientation of events along the Ouachita-Marathon belt make it difficult" to drive the uplifts from there; Ye et al. 1996 instead "speculate" on an Andean-type subduction margin along the south-west edge of the continent [abstract].
- **Plate motion along the south-west margin.** A boundary where plates slid obliquely past and against the continent, from Nevada to Sonora, was active "from Mississippian to middle Permian time" (Lawton et al. 2017) [abstract]. Leary et al. 2017 propose that stress from that margin "combined with that from collision along the Ouachita-Marathon and Nevada margins" (three sides at once) drove the uplift, and state that "no existing model has convincingly explained the timing, northeast-southwest contractional kinematics, and amagmatic nature of this system" [abstract]. Sweet et al. 2021 find the synchronous start "coeval to the initiation of highly oblique convergence" on the south-west margin [abstract].
- **Why these blocks.** The uplifts may follow much older faults: Marshak et al. 2000 suggest both the Ancestral Rockies and the Laramide structures formed "by inversion of Proterozoic extensional-fault systems" [abstract]. Soreghan et al. 2012 point to dense rock emplaced during Cambrian rifting beneath the Oklahoma and Colorado highlands [abstract].

## 4. Extent

**The system as a whole.** Paired uplifts and basins across "much of Oklahoma, Texas, New Mexico, Colorado, and Utah"; each basin thickens toward the neighboring uplift, from which faults "with great vertical relief" separate it (Ye et al. 1996) [abstract].

**Uplifts.**

- **Ancestral Front Range.** On the ground of today's Front Range. The debris apron on its east side is the Fountain Formation; at Manitou Springs it was laid down in fan deltas against "the active ancestral Ute Pass fault" (Sweet and Soreghan 2010) [abstract]. A trough through Woodland Park "separated the ancestral Front Range into a northern block (ancestral Front Range block) and a southern block (Ute Pass uplift)" (same) [abstract]. Kluth 2007 describes the northern block as "a broad northwest-southeast arch" whose faulted south-west margin had about 6 km of structural relief, sloping away north-east to end near the Colorado-Wyoming line [secondary]. Older maps carry the highland into southern Wyoming and across the Park Range [memory]; the north and north-west limits are therefore unsettled.
- **Uncompahgre uplift.** "stretched across southwestern Colorado, from central-eastern Utah into northern New Mexico"; sharp uplift in Pennsylvanian time; worn "to a low, nearly featureless plain" by the Triassic; began to subside in the Late Triassic; at the Black Canyon the crest stayed above the sediment until the Late Jurassic, and the first layer laid on the old rock is the Jurassic Entrada Sandstone (Hansen 1965, USGS Bulletin 1191) [full text of that section]. Its south-west front is a south-west-directed thrust (Barbeau 2003) [abstract]; its east side, in today's Sangre de Cristo Range, is the east-directed Sand Creek-Crestone thrust system (Hoy and Ridgway 2002) [abstract]. Kluth 2007 splits it in two: a "San Luis Highland" that rose in the early Pennsylvanian, and the Uncompahgre uplift proper (the present plateau) that rose in the late Pennsylvanian and early Permian [secondary]. Near Grand Junction, at Colorado National Monument, Triassic Chinle beds rest directly on the Precambrian [secondary].
- **Apishapa uplift.** South-east Colorado, east of the Central Colorado trough; "a possible flexural forebulge", that is a gentle bulge, not a fault-bounded range (Hoy and Ridgway 2002) [abstract]. Now buried under the plains [memory].
- **Others.** Wichita and Amarillo uplifts (Oklahoma and the Texas panhandle), where thrusting of basement over the Anadarko Basin is "clearly documented" (Ye et al. 1996) [abstract]; Pedernal uplift, New Mexico [secondary]; Sierra Grande, Zuni-Defiance, Emery, Central Basin Platform, Nemaha [memory]. Most are buried. In the Wichita Mountains of Oklahoma a Permian landscape has been partly uncovered by late Cenozoic erosion (Soreghan et al. 2012) [abstract].

**Basins (ground that must not get the event).**

- **Denver Basin**, east of the Front Range: Denver, and the hogbacks at the mountain front where the Fountain Formation crops out (Leary et al. 2020) [abstract].
- **Central Colorado trough**, with the Eagle Basin at its north end: "a north-trending basin bordered by ... the Uncompahgre uplift on the west and the Ancestral Front Range and Apishapa uplift on the east", filled with the Minturn, Maroon and Sangre de Cristo Formations (Hoy and Ridgway 2002) [abstract; Maroon from memory]. Vail, Minturn, Eagle, Glenwood Springs, Aspen and the Sangre de Cristo Range lie in it [memory for the towns].
- **Paradox Basin**, "190 km by 265 km", along the south-west flank of the Uncompahgre uplift in Utah and Colorado, with about 5 km of debris against the uplift and thick salt farther out (Barbeau 2003) [abstract]. Moab, and Durango on its east edge [memory for the towns]. The basin boundary runs through the Grenadier block near Silverton (Thomas 2007) [abstract].
- **Woodland Park trough**, inside the Front Range (above).
- Taos trough and Orogrande Basin (New Mexico), Anadarko Basin (Oklahoma), Midland and Delaware Basins (Texas) (Sweet et al. 2021; Ye et al. 1996) [abstract].

## 6. Surface today

Nothing of the Ancestral Rockies' shape survives in today's skyline. The uplifts were worn down, sank, and were buried; today's ranges on the same ground were raised by the Laramide event more than 200 million years later (first dossier, entry 7). What a visitor can see is (a) the debris: red sandstone and conglomerate of the Fountain Formation east of the Front Range and the Cutler Formation beside the Uncompahgre Plateau, "cited for nearly a century as documenting the Ancestral Rockies" (Soreghan and Sweet 2013) [abstract]; Red Rocks, the Boulder Flatirons and the Garden of the Gods are Fountain Formation [memory]; and (b) the gap: places where Triassic or Jurassic rock lies straight on Precambrian basement because the highland stood above the sea of sediment (Colorado National Monument [secondary]; Black Canyon [full text]). The basement rock in the uplifts is Precambrian; the event lifted it and did not make it.

## 7. Claims

- May say: blocks of old basement rose as mountains in the Pennsylvanian; their debris filled the basins beside them; they were later worn down and buried; the cause is debated.
- May say with the hedge "possibly": the highlands carried glaciers. Soreghan and Sweet 2013 present "the hypothesis of proglacial deposition" for the Fountain and Cutler, against the long-held alluvial fan reading [abstract]. This is one group's hypothesis.
- Should not state a height. Kluth's 6 to 8 km is offset on faults, not summit height [secondary]. The only relief figure read is "as much as 1000 m" of buried relief on the highland tops (Soreghan et al. 2012) [abstract].
- Should not say today's Rockies are the Ancestral Rockies "risen again" as if one range persisted.

## 8. For visitors

"About 320 to 290 million years ago, long before today's Rockies, blocks of very old rock were pushed up here into a range of mountains. Rivers carried their rubble into the low ground alongside, where it hardened into red sandstone. Geologists still debate what pushed them up so far from the edge of the continent: a collision with South America and Africa to the south-east, plates grinding past the south-west coast, or both at once."

## 9. Caveat for visitors

"These mountains were worn down and buried by younger rock. The mountains here today were raised much later, on some of the same ground."

## 10. What is uncertain

- The cause (section 3).
- The end date, by up to 30 million years, depending on definition (section 2).
- Whether the Front Range and Uncompahgre uplifts rose together. Kluth 2007 has the Uncompahgre proper rising late (late Pennsylvanian to early Permian); Sweet et al. 2021 find basin starts synchronous. A single date pair hides this.
- The outline of the ancestral Front Range on the north and west.
- How the highlands ended. The long-held view is slow wearing down into the Triassic and Jurassic (as in Bulletin 1191); Soreghan et al. 2012 argue the core highlands sank and were buried in the Early Permian. Both may hold in different places.
- Every Ma figure here is a conversion from stage names (see "How to read this").

# Part 2. Mountains after the building stopped

## A. The Southern and Middle Rocky Mountains since the Laramide

### A1. What published work says

**The two readings.** The Rockies and western Great Plains share a history of late Cenozoic river cutting; "both epeirogenic uplift and climate change (with no associated uplift) have been proposed as the cause" (McMillan et al. 2002) [abstract]. "Despite over a century of study, the timing and causes of elevation gain and incision in the region are unclear" (McMillan et al. 2006) [abstract]. "When and how the central Rocky Mountains ... gained modern topography remain controversial questions" (Fan et al. 2014) [abstract, opening lines only].

**Evidence offered for uplift after the Laramide.**

**Evidence against needing late uplift.**

- Fossil leaves at Florissant, Colorado (35 Ma) give a late Eocene elevation of 2.4 to 2.7 km, against 2.5 km today: "Pliocene uplift is thus not required to explain the present elevation" (Gregory and Chase 1992) [abstract]. A fuller treatment gives 2.2 to 3.3 km and argues the deep young canyons could have been cut by a change to a "drier but stormier climate" with no uplift (Gregory and Chase 1994) [abstract].
- Leaf evidence across the western United States gives Eocene and Oligocene elevations "comparable or higher than those of today", casting doubt on late Cenozoic uplift (Wolfe et al. 1998) [secondary].
- Modeling of river cutting on the High Plains: "climate change is at least as plausible an explanation" as tectonic rejuvenation (Wobus et al. 2010) [abstract].
- Of the four erosion episodes of Cather et al. 2012, "only the most recent ... is temporally correlated with climate change"; those authors still read all four as tectonic [abstract].
- The general argument that a cooling, more variable climate can cut deeper valleys and raise peaks by rebound without raising the land's average height (Molnar and England 1990) [memory: abstract not retrieved; UNVERIFIED].

**Common ground.** Both camps accept these points.

1. The Laramide ranges were worn down after the event. "Erosion beveled the Laramide Front Range uplift in Colorado to a surface of low relief by the end of the Eocene" (Gregory and Chase 1992) [abstract]. "Thirty to forty m.y. of post-Laramide degradation of the southern Rocky Mountains likely produced relatively low-relief topography within the crystalline cores of the ranges" (Anderson et al. 2006) [abstract].
2. The basins between and beside the ranges then filled with sediment in Oligocene to Miocene time (McMillan et al. 2006) [abstract].
3. The region nevertheless stood high. No published estimate read here puts the Colorado Front Range below about 2 km at any time since the late Eocene.
4. The deep canyons and the present sharp relief were cut late, mostly since the late Miocene (roughly the last 10 million years, and on the plains mostly since 2.5 Ma), as rivers emptied the basins and glaciers carved the crests (McMillan et al. 2006; Anderson et al. 2006; Duller et al. 2012) [abstract].

What is disputed is only how much the land's surface rose after the Laramide (from nothing to 2 km), when (candidates at about 40, 27 to 15, and 10 to 0 Ma), and why.

### A2. Is there a defensible dated event?

**No.** There is no named event, no agreed date, and no agreed amount. The candidates differ by tens of millions of years and are argued from the same river terraces and gravels. Karlstrom et al. 2012 is a hypothesis paper by its own description. Eaton's "epeirogen" and Cather's episodes are not in general use as event names.

One piece is firmly dated and undisputed: **the Rio Grande rift**, faulting from about 25 Ma to today (Abbey and Niemi 2020) [abstract]. It did raise the range fronts beside it (the Sangre de Cristo Range, the Sawatch Range above the upper Arkansas valley, the Sandia Mountains [memory for the range names]). But it is the continent pulling apart, not mountain building, and it belongs in the list of rifts. The province map already has it as record 569 "Rio Grande Rift" [probe].

### A3. Recommendation for the Rockies

1. **Do not add a "Neogene uplift" or "second Rockies" mountain-building event.** Do not give dates for post-Laramide uplift in visitor text.
2. **Do not stretch the Laramide entry to the present.** Squeezing ended; saying the event "lasted" to today would be false.
3. **It is defensible to show high ground at Laramide places in every chapter after 50 Ma**, as "persistence" (Part B), because all sides agree the region has stood high throughout. The honest picture differs by time:
   - about 50 to 35 Ma: ranges wearing down;
   - about 35 to 10 Ma: a high, rolling upland with the old range cores standing only modestly above sediment-filled basins. A "sharp peaks" picture would be wrong for this stretch;
   - about 10 Ma to today: the present relief being cut.
   If only one "mountains" picture exists, it can be used for the first and last of these with the caveat below; the middle stretch is the weak point.
4. **Caveat for visitors (Rockies, after 50 Ma).** "The squeezing that raised these mountains had ended by about 50 million years ago. The ranges were worn down to a high, rolling upland, and the peaks and canyons seen today were carved mostly in the last 10 million years. Geologists disagree about whether the land was also lifted again during that time, or whether a changing climate did the carving."
5. The first dossier's Laramide claim line ("later regional uplift and erosion shaped the present Rockies") states the uplift as fact. Soften to "later erosion, and possibly renewed uplift, shaped the present Rockies."

## B. How long mountains stay high after the squeezing ends

### B1. What published work says

- **It is a recognized puzzle.** "A perplexing macrogeomorphic problem is the persistence of topography in mountain ranges that were initially formed by orogenic events hundreds of millions of years old" (Pazzaglia and Brandon 1996) [abstract]. "The reason for the survival of mountainous topography in ancient orogenic belts is a long-standing problem" (Baldwin et al. 2003) [abstract].
- **Models: from a few million to hundreds of millions of years.** The simplest river-erosion model predicts decay in "1 to 10 Myr". Adding the crust's buoyant rebound lengthens that by up to 6 times; rivers choked by their own gravel, by 2 to 3 times; and a threshold below which floods do no work, by about 20 times; together these "could account for the presence of residual topography for hundreds of million years" (Baldwin et al. 2003) [abstract]. A model coupling landslides and rivers likewise explains "significant mountain-range relief in old orogenic belts, up to several hundred million years after tectonic activity has effectively ceased" (Egholm et al. 2013) [abstract].
- **Roots.** Old collision belts still have thick crust beneath them, but less height per unit of root than young ones; the root loses buoyancy as it cools, so rebound is "significantly reduced" with age, though it continues "for hundreds of millions of years" (Fischer 2002) [abstract].
- **Measured rates.** The southern Appalachians "eroded during the Mesozoic and Cenozoic at about 30 m/m.y.", against 100 m/m.y. or more while they were being built; slower erosion after the building stopped enabled "the survival of this ancient mountain belt with its deep crustal root as an isostatically maintained feature" (Matmon et al. 2003) [abstract].
- **Decline is not steady.** "Although one might expect a simple, monotonic decline in relief over time, evidence from several inactive mountain ranges shows alternating sequences of deposition and erosion", with the Southern Rockies, Pyrenees, Alps and Atlas as examples (Tucker and van der Beek 2013) [abstract].

**The examples, and why each is a warning.**

- **Appalachians** (building ended about 280 Ma; first dossier, entry 8). High ground of some kind has been shedding sediment to the Atlantic margin since the Triassic (Pazzaglia and Brandon 1996) [abstract]. But much of today's relief is argued to be young: in one North Carolina basin, relief increased by more than 150% since the Miocene (Gallen et al. 2013) [secondary]; in Pennsylvania, 100 to 150 m of renewed cutting began before 3.5 Ma and perhaps as early as 15 Ma, attributed to mantle flow (Miller et al. 2013) [secondary].
- **Urals** (building ended about 250 Ma; entry 13). A cold root in balance survives (Berzin et al. 1996; Puchkov 2009) [abstract]. But the range did not stand continuously: the belt "was eroded and partially inundated by seas in the Late Jurassic-Early Cretaceous times and has been reactivated since the Oligocene" (Puchkov 1997) [abstract], with "a brief resumption of orogenic activity in the Lower Jurassic and Pliocene-Quaternary time" (Puchkov 2009) [abstract]. Today's Urals are largely a young re-lift of an old belt.
- **Caledonides** (ended about 400 Ma; entry 11). The mountains collapsed by stretching in the Devonian, 403 to 380 Ma (Fossen 2010, cited in the first dossier) [abstract]. Whether the present mountains of Norway are a remnant of the old range kept up by rebound and climate, or a Neogene re-lift of a worn-down surface, is an open published dispute (Nielsen et al. 2009, a review of "Neogene uplift versus the ICE (isostasy-climate-erosion) hypothesis"; Japsen and Chalmers 2000, "Neogene uplift and tectonics around the North Atlantic") [title; arguments from memory].
- **Ancestral Rockies** (Part 1). The core highlands sank and were buried "in Early Permian time, directly following the Pennsylvanian tectonic apogee", within perhaps 10 to 20 million years (Soreghan et al. 2012) [abstract]. Yet the Uncompahgre crest at the Black Canyon stayed above the sediment, as a low plain, until the Late Jurassic, about 130 million years on (Hansen 1965) [full text; the interval is arithmetic from stage ages, memory].
- **Colorado Rockies** (Part A). Worn to low relief within 15 to 40 million years, while staying 2 km high.

### B2. What follows

1. **Order of magnitude.** Some high ground can outlast the building by tens to hundreds of millions of years. That much is sourced, from both models and measured rates.
2. **But height and ruggedness fall off much sooner.** In the two cases where the wearing-down is dated (Colorado Front Range, and the Ancestral Rockies cores), rugged relief was gone within about 10 to 40 million years. No source read gives a general figure, and the models span two orders of magnitude, so **there is no published number that could serve as a universal cutoff.**
3. **Mountains today are not evidence of mountains throughout.** For the Appalachians, Urals and Scandinavia, published work argues the present relief is partly or largely a Neogene renewal. Filling every chapter between the event and the present with mountains, because mountains stand there now, would be wrong for at least the Urals (sea in the Late Jurassic and Early Cretaceous).
4. **Persistence can be ended by something other than erosion**: collapse by stretching (Caledonides), sinking and burial (Ancestral Rockies), flooding by the sea (Urals), rifting (Appalachian belt split by the Atlantic).

### B3. Recommendation, general

1. **Yes, the site may say "worn-down mountains still stood here" after an event ends, but as a separate, weaker statement than "mountains were being built", and never by default all the way to the present.**
2. **Default rule.** Carry "mountains wearing down" for the chapter or chapters that fall within a few tens of millions of years after the event's end. The basis is the model range (1 to 10 Myr at the least, far longer with rebound: Baldwin et al. 2003) and the two dated cases above. The exact span is an editorial judgment, not a sourced figure; it should be stated as such wherever it is recorded.
3. **Per-event overrides, where a source dates what happened next.** Ready now:
   - Ancestral Rockies: stop at the end of the event (buried in the Early Permian) for the Front Range boxes; no "still stood" chapters.
   - Caledonian (Scandian): stop by 380 Ma (collapse).
   - Uralian: no persistence through the Late Jurassic and Early Cretaceous.
   - Laramide (Colorado and Wyoming): persistence to the present is defensible, with the caveat in A3.
   - Alleghanian: persistence into the Triassic is supported in outline by the sediment record (Pazzaglia and Brandon 1996); what the range looked like from the Jurassic to the Miocene was not found in a source.
4. **A picture for it.** If a single "mountains" scene must serve, the text should carry the difference. Better is a lower, rounded "old mountains" variant.
5. **Caveat for visitors (general).** "The forces that built these mountains had stopped by this time. The mountains were slowly wearing down. How high they still stood is not known."
6. **Do not** claim any height for a worn-down range; **do not** say a range "has stood ever since"; **do not** present the present-day relief of the Appalachians, Urals or Norwegian mountains as a simple leftover of the original range.

# What I would not write

Claims that could not be supported from what was read, and should not appear in visitor text or in the event list on the strength of this dossier:

1. Any height for the Ancestral Rocky Mountains, or any comparison with a modern range. No estimate was found.
2. That the Ancestral Rockies were glaciated, stated as fact. It is one group's hypothesis.
3. That the cause of the Ancestral Rockies is known, or that the Ouachita-Marathon collision alone explains them.
4. That the ancestral Front Range reached into Wyoming or covered the Park Range, Gore Range or Wet Mountains. Sources disagree or were not found; the boxes leave these out.
5. Exact outlines for any of the six boxes. They are from memory of where younger rock rests on basement and were not traced from a map.
6. That Red Rocks, the Flatirons and the Garden of the Gods are Fountain Formation, as a sourced statement. It is common knowledge but was not confirmed in a source here; the sourced Fountain locality is Manitou Springs.
7. Ma dates for the Ancestral Rockies to better than about 5 million years. All are conversions from stage names, partly from memory of the chart.
8. That the Uncompahgre and Front Range uplifts rose at the same time.
9. A named, dated "Neogene uplift" of the Rocky Mountains, or any figure for how much the Rockies rose after the Laramide. Published figures run from zero to 2,000 m.
10. That the present height of the Colorado Rockies is "widely attributed" to Neogene uplift. It is attributed so by some authors and denied by others; leaf-fossil evidence puts the Front Range near its present height by 35 Ma.
11. That the Rockies were low, or were a plain near sea level, between the Laramide and the Neogene. That older view is contradicted by the leaf evidence.
12. What Molnar and England 1990, Nielsen et al. 2009, and Japsen and Chalmers 2000 argue in detail, and the result of Fan et al. 2014. Their abstracts were not retrieved; they are cited for their titles and standing only.
13. The Appalachian and Pennsylvania rejuvenation figures (more than 150%; 100 to 150 m; before 3.5 Ma) as exact quotations. They come from search summaries of the abstracts.
14. A general number for how long mountains last after building stops. None exists in what was read; the "few tens of millions of years" default in B3 is an editorial judgment.
15. That any range "has stood continuously" since its orogeny. For the Urals the opposite is published; for the Appalachians and the Norwegian mountains it is disputed or unknown.
16. That the Rio Grande rift is a mountain-building event. Its dates are sound, but it is extension.
