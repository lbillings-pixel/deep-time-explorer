## 1. Thelodonts: time range, and which chapters they may be drawn in

**Recommendation.** Silurian: yes. Devonian: yes. Ordovician: no (or only with an explicit "known from scales alone" flag). Early Carboniferous: no.

**Basis sentence for the whitelist.** Whole-bodied thelodonts are known only from the Silurian and the Early Devonian; isolated scales extend the group back to the Late Ordovician and forward to the early or middle Famennian (Late Devonian); none is known from the Carboniferous.

Evidence:

- **Whole fish are Silurian and Early Devonian only.** "Twenty-four thelodont species, represented by well-preserved squamations, and three species having smaller patches of scales ... have been identified in the Silurian of Estonia, Scotland, northern Canada and Norway, and in the Lower Devonian of northern Canada and southern Britain" (Märss 2011, introduction, citing the 2007 Handbook) **[read as text]**. That count is as of 2011; I did not search for later finds.
- **Stated range of the group:** "ranged from the Upper Ordovician" to the Upper Devonian (Ferrón & Botella 2017) **[read via fetch tool]**. Thelodonts "originated during the Middle?-Late Ordovician", and are "longest lasting of the Palaeozoic agnathans" (Ferrón et al. 2018) **[abstract]**.
- **Ordovician: scales only.** The Paleobiology Database (queried this session) lists three Ordovician thelodont genera, *Sandivia*, *Stroinolepis* and *Larolepis*, all Sandbian to Hirnantian (Late Ordovician), with 6, 2 and 1 occurrences. These are scale taxa (*Sandivia* from the Timan-Pechora region and *Stroinolepis* from Severnaya Zemlya, Russia) **[not checked]**. Sansom, Smith & Smith (1996) reported "scales of thelodont and shark-like fishes" from the Ordovician Harding Sandstone of Colorado (title only; content **[not checked]**; I recall the thelodont assignment of the Harding scales has been questioned since, **[not checked]**). Nothing I found suggests an articulated Ordovician thelodont. An Ordovician entry would rest on isolated scales, a fraction of a millimeter across, and on nothing else.
- **Heyday:** Silurian to Early Devonian. Of the 67 genus names the Paleobiology Database files under Thelodonti, nearly all with occurrences are Silurian or Early Devonian; *Thelodus* (293 occurrences), *Paralogania* (81) and *Loganellia* (77) are the commonest (database query this session; the counts reflect collecting effort as much as abundance).
- **Last:** early to middle Famennian. Scales from Iran (Hojedk section) and northwestern Australia (Napier Formation), "dated as early/mid-Famennian", named *Arianalepis*, are "the first evidence of thelodonts surviving the Frasnian-Famennian extinction events" (Hairapetian et al. 2015) **[abstract]**. Before that paper the youngest were Frasnian (*Australolepis*, Gneudna Formation, Western Australia; Turner & Dring 1981, title only). So "Late Devonian, Famennian" is right, but the Late Devonian ones are known from scales only, and only from Gondwana (Iran, Australia).
- **Caution for the Devonian chapter:** a drawing based on Silurian fish is well founded for the Early Devonian (*Turinia pagei* is an articulated Early Devonian fish from Scotland). For the Middle and Late Devonian the body is unknown (scales only), and the survivors are turiniids on Gondwana. If the Devonian chapter is subdivided, weight the entry to the early part.

## 2. What a thelodont looked like

### 2.1 Which fish to use as the model

- **Best model: *Phlebolepis elegans*** from Himmiste Quarry, Saaremaa, Estonia (Ludlow, so close to Gotland in place and time). Wilson & Märss (2012) re-described it from "hundreds of articulated specimens" (more than 450 specimens on 48 slabs) and published an artist's restoration (their fig. 9, painted by Michael Hanson: a school "swimming in a lagoon" over a carbonate sea floor) **[read as text; I did not view the figure]**. The paper is open access (doi:10.3176/earth.2012.4.06) and the painter should look at fig. 9 directly.
- **Honest caveat the editor should know.** *Phlebolepis elegans* is not one of the fishes that took over after the Lau event. It is "typical for faunas found in older strata on Gotland" and is missing from the post-event samples (Jarochowska et al. 2021 preprint) **[read as text]**. The post-event thelodonts (*Thelodus parvidens*, *Paralogania ludlowiensis*; see section 3) are known on Gotland from scales only. And "*Thelodus* is very rare" as a body fossil: only fragments of squamation of two species, plus one partial juvenile squamation of *T. laevis*, were known in 2011, none with head and tail complete (Märss 2011) **[read as text]**. So a drawing labeled "thelodont" modeled on *Phlebolepis* is defensible; a drawing labeled "*Thelodus parvidens*" would be a reconstruction by analogy and should say so.
- **Not the model:** the Furcacaudiformes of northern Canada (deep, laterally compressed body, large forked tail; Wilson & Caldwell 1993, 1998; content **[not checked]** beyond the titles, which say "stomachs and deep bodies"). Ferrón & Botella (2017) list three general thelodont body forms: "dorso-ventrally flattened body, broad head and hypocercal large asymmetrical tails"; fusiform; and the laterally flattened fork-tailed furcacaudiforms **[read via fetch tool]**.

### 2.2 Description (all from Wilson & Märss 2012, **[read as text]**, unless tagged otherwise)

- **Length:** "about 7 cm". Other articulated thelodonts are larger: *Loganellia* "body length 85-228 mm"; *Lanarkia* 33 to 162 mm (Märss 2011, citing Märss & Ritchie) **[read as text, second-hand]**. Body length of *Thelodus parvidens* is unknown; a painter could reasonably show 7 to 20 cm fish (the upper end is my extrapolation from *Loganellia*).
- **Shape:** "generally fusiform" (spindle-shaped). The head-and-gill region is "somewhat depressed" (flattened top to bottom, "broader than it is deep", but only "slightly to moderately") and makes up "about half the total body length". The trunk is roughly cylindrical just behind the gills, then "very laterally compressed" (flattened side to side) toward the tail, and occupies "about one-quarter" of total length; the tail fin takes the rest (the last quarter is my arithmetic). So: a tadpole-like fish, wide low head tapering to a narrow, blade-like tail stalk. Not a flat skate-like animal.
- **Head:** front margin "slightly convex"; the front corners of the head, just ahead of the eyes, are "pronounced". A small snout ("rostrum") overhangs the mouth. No head shield, no plates: the whole animal is covered in minute scales.
- **Eyes:** at the front corners of the head, facing out sideways ("located just posterior to the anterolateral corners of the head"). The opening is "an elongate oval", not round, bordered by two larger crescent scales above and below. Size is not stated; small.
- **Mouth:** "subterminal" (just under the tip of the snout, not at the very front), a horizontal oval "about half as wide as the head". No jaws.
- **Paired fins:** one pair of pectoral fins (the authors also call them suprabranchial fins). Triangular, "extremely thin", starting "close behind the orbits" and ending at the back of the gill region; straight leading edge. They are flaps along the side of the head region, not stalked fins. "Paired pelvic or abdominal fins were absent."
- **Dorsal and anal fins:** one of each, both "small and little elevated", far back on the tail stalk; the dorsal slightly ahead of the anal. Low and stiff ("not very flexible in life").
- **Tail:** "large and clearly hypocercal": yes, the lower lobe is the main one, "noticeably more stout than the dorsal lobe", and slightly longer, with "a prominent but slightly shorter" upper lobe. Between the two lobes a fin web carried on about nine thin scale-covered "rays" (about five from the lower lobe, about four above); rear edge "emarginate (slightly forked)". A small keel runs under the lower lobe. This is larger and more nearly symmetrical than the older, widely copied drawing by Ritchie (1968).
- **Gill openings:** not known. "No definitive branchial openings have been seen." The authors "suggest that branchial openings of *P. elegans* were small, in an oblique row located beneath the pectoral fin", toward the back of the gill region, "covered externally by skin bearing scales". Possibly four pairs of pouches, "uncertain". For comparison, *Turinia pagei* had "eight pairs of gills" (Donoghue & Smith 2001) **[abstract]**; and I recall that *Loganellia scotica* shows about eight small openings in a row on the underside of each side **[not checked]**. Advice: draw no conspicuous gill slits. At most, a faint slanting row of a few small pores low on the side under the pectoral flap.
- **Scales:** minute, covering everything including the fins. In the juvenile *Thelodus laevis* squamation they are 0.17 to 0.37 mm long; adult *Thelodus* scales reach "up to 1.4 mm in *T. laevis* and up to 1.5 mm in *T. carinatus*" (Märss 2011) **[read as text]**. On *Phlebolepis* the body scales carry fine ridges converging backward, and "the predominant orientation of the rows of scales ... is longitudinal"; tail-lobe scales are "diamond-shaped". At painting scale: a matte, fine-grained, sharkskin or sandpaper surface with a faint lengthwise grain. No visible individual plates.
- **Lateral line:** present as rows of pore scales on the head; not visible at painting scale.
- **Color: nothing is known.** The fossil scales vary "from white through light beige to dark coffee-brown", which is preservation, not life color (the interpretation is mine). I found no study of pigment in thelodonts. Any countershading is artistic convention.
- **Feeding and habits (for posture):** *Turinia pagei* "was probably a deposit feeder" (Donoghue & Smith 2001) **[abstract]**. Ferrón & Botella (2017) infer from scale shape that *Phlebolepis elegans* lived near the bottom on hard substrates, while other thelodonts were open-water or schooling forms **[read via fetch tool; this is inference from scale shape by analogy with sharks]**.

### 2.3 Scottish and other articulated thelodonts (context only)

- *Loganellia scotica*, *Shielia taiti*, *Lanarkia horrida*, *L. spinulosa*, *L. lanceolata* and *Turinia pagei* are re-described in Märss & Ritchie (1997). "A paired ventral fin in *Shielia* ... and *Lanarkia lanceolata* ... has been discovered" **[abstract]**. So some thelodonts had a second pair of fins that *Phlebolepis* lacked. The broader, flatter head usually shown for *Loganellia* and *Turinia* is **[not checked]** here.
- The 2007 Handbook (Märss, Turner & Karatajūtė-Talimaa) is the standard review. I could not read it.

## 3. What Eriksson, Nilsson & Jeppsson (2009) report

**I did not read the full text.** From the abstract and from later summaries:

- The Lau event wiped out "two-thirds of the fish taxa" on Gotland, with reorganization over roughly 200,000 years; before the event "jawed acanthodians dominated"; afterward the fauna became diverse with "a brief but marked dominance of the jawless thelodonts"; the stepwise changes resemble those of conodonts (Eriksson et al. 2009) **[abstract, via the Lund University record through the fetch tool]**.
- Summary by a later paper **[read as text: Jarochowska et al. 2021, EarthArXiv preprint of the GFF paper; wording may differ slightly in the journal version]**: Eriksson et al. "described a step-wise disappearance of vertebrate taxa during the Lau Event, starting at the top of the När Formation (Hemse Group) and continuing throughout the Eke Formation"; they "recorded a shift from an acanthodian-dominated fauna before the event to a thelodont-dominated fauna in the Burgen Oolite Formation during the initial recovery, which was also noted by Fredholm (1989)". Fishes show "overall scarcity in the preceding Eke Formation".
- **Names.** The Eriksson et al. samples from the Burgen Oolite (localities Burgen 7 and 9; originally in E. K. Nilsson's 2005 thesis) "collectively contained remains of" the thelodonts ***Thelodus parvidens***, *Thelodus* sp., ***Paralogania ludlowiensis*** and *Paralogania*? sp., with the acanthodians *Nostolepis striata*, *Gomphonchus sandelensis* and *Poracanthodes porosus* (same preprint) **[read as text]**. Fredholm's (1989) earlier samples from the same unit were "dominated by thelodonts *Thelodus parvidens*, *Th. sculptilis*, '*Loganellia cuneata*', *Paralogania ludlowiensis*, and *Lanarkia*? sp." with "a subordinate number of acanthodians". Jarochowska et al. add *Thelodus trilobatus* (possibly a scale variant of *T. parvidens*) and note that "thelodont scales dominate" their samples too, with a minimum of nine fish species.
- **Before the event:** the thelodonts typical of older Gotland strata are *Thelodus carinatus* and *Phlebolepis elegans* (same preprint). Which acanthodian species dominated just before the event, and the percentages, I could not establish (**full text needed**). Bremer & Blom (2015) note "a sudden appearance of several acanthodian taxa in the När Formation", i.e. shortly before the event **[read as text]**.
- **Whole fish or scales?** Scales only. All the Gotland material is microscopic scales from dissolved limestone samples (the papers above describe scale counts per sample). No articulated thelodont is known from Gotland as far as I found **[not checked beyond these papers]**; *Thelodus parvidens* and *Paralogania ludlowiensis* are scale-based species (described by Agassiz 1839 and Gross 1967).
- **Independent support from Britain:** "*Thelodus parvidens* ... and *T. trilobatus* ... dominate in the Upper Ludfordian" of the Welsh Borderland (Märss & Miller 2004) **[abstract]**; a "*Thelodus parvidens* assemblage" spans the Ludlow Bone Bed there (Turner 1973) **[abstract]**.
- **How long the thelodont dominance lasted:** the abstract says "brief". In the overlying Hamra and Sundre formations vertebrate faunas "remain similar" in composition to the Burgsvik units, and in younger East Baltic strata acanthodians gain in importance (Bremer et al. 2020, GFF) **[abstract]**.
- **A caution from the later authors:** on Gotland "the taxonomy of vertebrates ... is in need of revision" (Bremer & Blom 2015) **[read as text]**, and Jarochowska et al. say their own sample is "too small to discuss any further implications" of the thelodont-to-acanthodian ratio.

## 4. Where thelodonts lived, and whether they shoaled

- **Shoaling: yes, with direct evidence.** The Himmiste-Kuigu bed is "a mass-mortality deposit of articulated specimens", "interpreted as a fossilized school (shoal) subjected to a mass-death event (Robertson 1938; Märss et al. 2003)". "The *Phlebolepis* school or shoal perished in a lagoon ... which was connected with the open sea over the reef belt; *Phlebolepis* inhabited waters on both sides of the reef belt" (Wilson & Märss 2012) **[read as text]**. The sediment is "quiet-lagoonal dolomitic mudstone" (Märss 2011) **[read as text]**. The primary taphonomy paper (Märss, Perens & Klaos 2003) is [CR] but **[not checked]**. Ferrón & Botella (2017) infer schooling for other thelodonts (*Shielia*, several fork-tailed forms) from scale shape **[read via fetch tool]**.
- **Environments in general:** mostly marine: "shallow lagoons, near-shore habitats, and open shelf or deep-water basins"; "a few species have been found in sediments interpreted by some authors as brackish or freshwater", e.g. *Turinia pagei* in the Old Red Sandstone of Scotland (Ferrón & Botella 2017) **[read via fetch tool]**. Early Paleozoic vertebrates lived "nearshore marine to restricted marine in the Ordovician, and on the marine epicontinental shelves of the Silurian" (Žigaitė & Blieck 2013) **[abstract]**.
- **The Lau-event species specifically:** *Paralogania ludlowiensis* "has been shown to mainly occur in high-energy, shallow-marine environments often with a clastic input"; *Thelodus parvidens* occurs "in a wide range of lithologies" (Jarochowska et al. 2021 preprint) **[read as text]**. The Burgen Oolite setting was "above the wave base, in a fully marine, well oxygenated water" with "ooidal and bioclastic-oncoidal shoals and patch reefs".

**Mapping to the site's settings:**

| Setting | Silurian | Devonian | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm shallow sea or reef (incl. lagoon behind a reef) | yes | yes | Himmiste lagoon shoal; Burgen Oolite shoals and patch reefs |
| Open shelf sea | yes | yes | Scales widespread on shelves; *Phlebolepis* "both sides of the reef belt" |
| Shoreline | yes | yes | *P. ludlowiensis* in high-energy sandy shallows; fish remains in the Burgsvik Sandstone, which Bremer & Blom (2015) classify as "Shoreface" |

## 6. Microbial structures of the Lau event on Gotland

### 6.1 What is recorded, and where in the succession

From the stratigraphic figure in Calner's published Reply (2005, *Geology* e92) **[read in full, including the figure]**, bottom to top:

| Unit (thickness per that figure) | Anomalous facies listed |
|---|---|
| Top of the Hemse Group | flat-pebble conglomerate |
| Eke Formation (0.4-14 m) | flat-pebble conglomerate; "stromatolites/oncolites" |
| Burgsvik Formation (3.4-32 m) | "wrinkle structures"; "oolites-oncolites" |
| Hamra and Sundre formations | back to a "carbonate platform with major reef complexes" |

- Calner's own summary: "the sudden appearance of normal-marine stromatolites, wrinkle structures, and a mass occurrence of oncoids during the event" (Reply) **[read]**. The 2005 paper's abstract lists "normal-marine stromatolites, mass occurrence of oncoids, and subtidal wrinkle structures (e.g., *Kinneyia* and microripples)" plus flat-pebble conglomerates **[abstract, via search excerpt]**. Riding's Comment repeats the same list **[read]**.
- Duration of the anomaly: "relatively short-lived (~0.5 m.y.)" (Riding's Comment, reading Calner's fig. 2) **[read]**.
- Calner (Reply): the microbial resurgence has a "well-defined stratigraphic range" and occurs "in various environments over that same period"; "oolites of any significant thickness (several meters) on Gotland occur only in association with the Mulde and Lau Events" **[read]**.
- Eriksson & Calner (2008): the isotope excursion is "closely linked to an increase in stromatolites (mats and oncoids) across a wide range of depths and sedimentary environments" **[abstract, via fetch tool]**.

### 6.2 Each structure: size, shape, setting

**Oncoids (the best documented, and the main thing to add).** Source: Qvarnström (2012), a Lund University bachelor's thesis supervised by Calner, **[read as text]**. It is a student thesis, not peer reviewed, and UNVERIFIED in Crossref (no DOI); treat its numbers as good leads.
- The Eke Formation is "very rich in oncoids"; "in situ grown oncoids are (commonly) so abundant that they form oncolites" (beds made of them).
- **Size:** "The oncoids collected from Gotland range from <1 to >10 cm in diameter." At Bodudden "the dominating size is of 2 cm"; at Petsarve two size groups, "1-2 cm" and "approximately 3 cm"; at Ronehamn "2-4 cm", with some "bigger than 5 cm". So: marble to golf-ball size is typical; the largest are fist-sized.
- **Shape:** "spherical to subspherical, discoidal, elongate or 'bananaic'". The big ones grown in place in quiet water are a lobed "cauliflower" type, often with a hollow underside and thicker growth on the upper side; in rougher, shallower water they are smaller, rounder and concentric.
- **What they are:** crusts of the calcified microbes *Rothpletzella* and *Wetheredella* grown around a shell, bryozoan twig, crinoid piece or snail. Some are intergrown with small tube corals (*Syringopora*).
- **Sea floor:** the thesis figures "a slab of a paleoseafloor with in situ grown 'cauliflower' oncoids" from Ronehamn: lime mud ("much fine micrite") with well-preserved brachiopods, crinoids, trilobites and corals, "indicating low water energy". Setting: "shallow subtidal zone" of the carbonate shelf. No depth in meters is given.
- In the overlying Burgen Oolite the oncoids are different: transported and abraded, "large (2-3 cm) mature" ones and many thin-coated "initial" ones, mixed with ooid sand, in a high-energy fully marine setting "in the proximity of reefs" (Jarochowska et al. 2021 preprint) **[read as text]**. They contrast these with "the 'cauliflower' type known from the Eke Formation".

**Stromatolites.** Source: Cherns (1982) **[abstract, as deposited in Crossref]**.
- In the lower Eke Formation of eastern Gotland, an eroded, scalloped limestone surface (hollows on average "1-2 m" wide with "relief of 40-50 cm") was flooded again, and "finely-layered stromatolitic mats developed over the initial infill, in subtidal environments, and grew to form domed mounds within the erosional cavities". "Some emergence is evident from desiccation features in the upper parts of mounds."
- So the recorded stromatolites are **finely layered mats passing up into low domes**, not tall columns. Their size is bounded by the hollows they filled: my inference is domes up to roughly 1-2 m across and a few tens of centimeters high; **the abstract gives no direct measurement of the mounds**. They grew on hard, already-cemented limestone, in very shallow water that sometimes drained.
- Whether Calner's (2005) "normal-marine stromatolites" are these same mounds, or others, and their dimensions: **full text needed**.

**Wrinkle structures.** In the Burgsvik Sandstone, i.e. on silt and fine sand, not on lime mud ("the wrinkle-structures in the Burgsvik Sandstone (Calner, 2005a; Calner 2005b)", Qvarnström 2012 **[read as text]**), and described as "subtidal" in Calner's abstract. Types named: *Kinneyia* and microripples. No sizes found for Gotland. In general, wrinkle structures are millimeter-scale: winding or honeycomb-like flat-topped ridges and pits a few millimeters wide on a bedding surface, thought to record a microbial mat binding the sand (Hagadorn & Bottjer 1997; content **[not checked]**). To a diver this would read as a smooth, slightly leathery, finely crinkled film over the sand in place of clean ripples.

**Flat-pebble conglomerates.** At the top of the Hemse Group and base of the Eke Formation; the clasts come from the underlying Hemse beds (Qvarnström 2012, citing Calner 2005) **[read as text]**. The Burgen-1 core shows "widespread intraclastic conglomerate" with discontinuity surfaces (Calner & Eriksson 2006) **[abstract]**. These are thin plates of early-hardened lime mud torn up and redeposited; clast sizes on Gotland **not found**. Riding (2005) argues they reflect seawater chemistry more than loss of burrowers **[read]**.

**Coated grains: ooids and pisoids.** The Burgsvik (now Burgen) Oolite: well-sorted ooid sand shoals; ooid nuclei include thelodont scales (Jarochowska et al. 2021 preprint) **[read as text]**. Riding mentions "the marine pisoids of the overlying Burgsvik Formation" (pea-sized coated grains) **[read]**. Sand-grade; at painting scale simply pale, clean, rippled sand.

**Calcimicrobes inside reefs and crusts.** "Increased microbial activity (*Rothpletzella* and *Wetheredella*)" in the Burgen-1 core (Calner & Eriksson 2006) **[abstract]**. Not separately visible to a painter.

### 6.3 What a diver would have seen (my synthesis; each element is sourced above, the combination is mine)

Three successive sea floors, all shallow and sunlit; no depth in meters is published in anything I read:

1. **Eke time (the microbial peak; fish scarce).** A quiet, muddy lime-mud floor strewn, in places paved, with knobbly cauliflower-like oncoids, mostly 2-4 cm, some to 10 cm, among brachiopod shells, crinoid stems and small corals; no large reefs. Locally, on bare rock ledges in very shallow water, low finely layered mat-covered domes up to perhaps a meter or two across.
2. **Burgsvik Sandstone time.** A sandy delta front. Fine sand and silt with a crinkled mat film (wrinkle structures) instead of the usual burrowed, churned surface.
3. **Burgen Oolite time (when the thelodonts dominate).** Pale rippled ooid sand shoals with scattered rounded oncoids of 2-3 cm, crinoid debris, and small patch reefs returning.

**Consequence for the painting.** The thelodont dominance and the densest microbial floor are not quite the same moment: fish are scarce in the Eke Formation and the thelodont-rich samples come from the Burgen Oolite. The most defensible single scene is the third: a shoal of thelodonts over pale ooid sand with scattered 2-3 cm oncoids and a small patch reef. Adding a patch of cauliflower oncoids (2-4 cm, a few to 10 cm) and, in the background shallows, one or two low layered domes, compresses perhaps a few hundred thousand years into one view; that is a fair artistic compression if the caption says "during and just after".

### 6.4 The dispute, stated fairly

- Riding (2005, Comment) **[read]**: accepts the observations but argues that a long-lived high in seawater carbonate saturation about 420 million years ago mattered more than the loss of grazers; "microbial carbonates do not always show resurgence in mass extinction aftermaths".
- Calner (2005, Reply) **[read]**: agrees chemistry contributed, but the several million years between the Mulde and Lau events show no such facies despite rising saturation, so the short events are the trigger.
- Jarochowska et al. (2021) **[read as text, preprint]**: in the Burgen Oolite, oncoids and microbial crusts coexist with a diverse fauna and reefs, which is "not compatible with the interpretation of microbial carbonates ... as 'disaster forms'" there; microbes can be part of a healthy reef.

## 7. Stromatolites and mats after the Cambrian: sources for a whitelist note

- **The general idea and its history**, compactly, in Riding's Comment **[read]**: stromatolite decline in the late Proterozoic has long been linked to the rise of animals (Fischer 1965; Awramik 1971); in the Phanerozoic "metazoan reduction at mass extinction events" could permit "recovery of stromatolites as 'disaster biotas' (Schubert and Bottjer, 1992)"; "by this reasoning, even a relatively minor extinction, such as the Lau Event, might give rise to detectable stromatolite resurgence".
- **Best single review to cite:** Mata & Bottjer (2012) **[abstract]**: "Widespread development of microbialites characterizes the ... aftermath of two of the 'big five' mass extinctions" (Late Devonian and end-Permian); a response "has been suggested for the end-Ordovician event"; none yet recognized after the end-Triassic or end-Cretaceous. So the resurgence is real but not universal.
- Sheehan & Harris (2004), "Microbialite resurgence after the Late Ordovician extinction" **[CR; content not checked]**: the nearest earlier precedent to the Lau event.
- Riding (2006) **[CR; content not checked]** and Riding & Liang (2005) **[CR; content not checked beyond what the Comment says of it]**: long-term abundance of microbial carbonates tracks both animal diversity and seawater chemistry; "most abundant when elevated saturation state coincided with low metazoan diversity" (Comment, summarizing Riding 2005) **[read]**.
- Wrinkle structures specifically: Hagadorn & Bottjer (1997) and Pruss, Fraiser & Bottjer (2004) **[CR; content not checked]**: common before animals burrowed deeply, rare afterward in normal subtidal settings, proliferating again after the end-Permian extinction (that is what the titles say; details **[not checked]**).
- That stromatolites persist today and through the Phanerozoic mainly in restricted settings (very salty lagoons, tidal flats, some lakes) is standard textbook material **[not checked this session; no source verified]**.

**Suggested wording for the whitelist note:** "Stromatolites, microbial mats, oncoids and wrinkled sand never disappeared after the Cambrian, but grazing and burrowing animals confined them largely to restricted settings. They spread again for a time after some extinctions, though not all. In a Silurian open-sea scene they are permitted only where the rocks record them: here, the Eke and Burgsvik formations of Gotland during the Lau event (Calner 2005; Cherns 1982). How much of that resurgence was due to the loss of grazers and how much to seawater chemistry is debated (Riding 2005)."
