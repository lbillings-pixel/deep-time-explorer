# Dossier: six Phanerozoic "great events" (10–15)

Research notes behind the site's texts and pictures, not text written for visitors. Compiled 14 November 2026.

## How to read this

- **Citations.** Every citation below was looked up in the Crossref database (authors, year, title, journal, volume, pages, DOI). Anything not found there is marked **UNVERIFIED**. Author lists longer than seven are shortened to the first three plus "et al." with the author count.
- **Facts.** Each fact carries a tag:
  - **[abstract]** I read it in the paper's own abstract.
  - **[full text]** I read it in the open-access full text. For Hutchinson et al. 2021 only, the full text was read through an automated page summary covering roughly the first third of the article, so quote-check those items.
  - **[secondary]** I saw it only in a search-result summary, a citing paper or a press release; check before use.
  - **[memory]** widely repeated, but I did not confirm it in a source during this work; check before use.
- **Time scale.** "ICS 2024/12" means the International Chronostratigraphic Chart, version December 2024, which I read directly (PDF from stratigraphy.org). Its citation is: Cohen, K.M., Finney, S.C., Gibbard, P.L. & Fan, J.-X. 2013 (updated). The ICS International Chronostratigraphic Chart. *Episodes* 36(3): 199–204. doi:10.18814/epiiugs/2013/v36i3/002.
- **Painter notes.** Each item is labelled *evidence* (fossils or rocks show it), *inference* (reasoned from evidence) or *convention* (artists' habit, no evidence).

## 10. Cambrian explosion

### Date
- **Base of the Cambrian: 538.8 ± 0.6 Ma** (ICS 2024/12). This rests on U–Pb zircon dates from Namibia that bracket the boundary at 538.6–538.8 Ma, more than 2 Myr younger than the earlier 541 Ma figure (Linnemann et al. 2019) [abstract].
- **Trilobites appear at c. 521 Ma**, the base of Cambrian Series 2 (Paterson et al. 2019; ICS 2024/12 gives ~521.0 for the base of Stage 3) [abstract].
- **Chengjiang biota: no older than 518.03 ± 0.69 Ma.** This is a maximum age from the youngest detrital zircon in the Maotianshan Shale, not a direct date of the fossils (Yang et al. 2018) [abstract].
- **Burgess Shale: commonly quoted as c. 508 Ma**, Wuliuan Stage (Nanglu et al. 2020 for the stage) [abstract for stage; secondary for "508"]. I found no direct radiometric date. ICS 2024/12 puts the base of the Wuliuan at ~506.5 Ma, so on the current chart a Wuliuan deposit should be about 506 Ma or younger. The "508" figure dates from when the stage base was placed near 509 Ma. Safer wording: "about 508–505 Ma".
- **Where sources disagree on the span.** Wood et al. 2019 use ~540–520 Ma. Paterson et al. 2019 argue it was a ~20 Myr episode that had "largely concluded" by ~521 Ma. Yang et al. 2018 call it a protracted process of more than 22 Myr reaching its "major phase" near 518 Ma. No source I read supports an end date of 515 Ma specifically.

### What is known
- The Ediacaran-to-Cambrian faunal change in Namibia took less than 410 kyr, between 538.99 ± 0.21 and 538.58 ± 0.19 Ma (Linnemann et al. 2019) [abstract].
- Molecular clocks put the origin of the main animal lineages tens of millions of years before their fossils appear (Erwin et al. 2011; the paper's summary line) [abstract].
- The oldest trace fossils of arthropod type are c. 537 Ma, trilobite body fossils c. 521 Ma, and soft-bodied "Burgess Shale-type" faunas start at c. 518 Ma. Ediacaran sites with similar preservation contain algae but no animals, which argues against a long hidden Precambrian history for arthropods (Daley et al. 2018) [abstract].
- The Burgess Shale dataset covers 77,179 specimens and 234 species-level taxa across four sites; communities differed from site to site (Nanglu et al. 2020) [abstract].
- Marble Canyon, 40 km from Walcott's quarry, is arthropod-dominated and includes the chordate *Metaspriggina* (Caron et al. 2014) [abstract].

### What is debated
- **Cause.** No single hypothesis explains both timing and pattern. Ecological triggers such as predator–prey "arms races" explain the pattern but not the timing; environmental triggers such as oxygenation do the reverse. Sperling et al. 2013 link the two: low oxygen suppresses carnivory, so rising oxygen permits complex food webs [abstract]. Linnemann et al. 2019 favour ecological cascades after the evolution of mobility [abstract].
- **Was it an "explosion" at all?** Wood et al. 2019 argue it was one phase among several radiations running from the late Ediacaran (~571 Ma) into the early Palaeozoic [abstract].
- **Duration and end point.** See the date section.
- **Exact boundary age.** Nelson et al. 2022 (*Earth and Planetary Science Letters* 580: 117396, doi:10.1016/j.epsl.2022.117396) argue from South African sections that the boundary may be younger still. Citation checked; I could not read the abstract, so the size of the shift is unconfirmed.

### What a painter could show
- **Sea floor only; land bare of plants.** *Inference*: no land-plant fossils of this age are known.
- **Chengjiang animals on a muddy sea floor**: arthropods of many forms, lobopodians, early chordates. *Evidence* for the animals (Hou et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2018). Water depth and clarity are *inference*; I did not check a source for the depositional setting [memory: shallow, mud-rich shelf].
- **Burgess Shale animals** such as *Metaspriggina* and abundant arthropods. *Evidence* (Caron et al. 2014; Nanglu et al. 2020). The usual setting, deep water at the foot of a submarine cliff, is [memory] and should be checked.
- **Colour.** *Convention.* No pigment evidence was found in the sources read; all colours in published reconstructions are artists' choices.
- **Light.** *Inference/convention.* Dim blue-green if the deep-water Burgess setting is used; brighter if the shallower Chengjiang setting is used.

## 11. First forests

### Date
- **Somerset and Devon, England: Eifelian, c. 390 Ma.** The Hangman Sandstone Formation holds "the oldest known evidence globally for the relative position of standing trees" (Davies et al. 2024) [abstract]. The paper quotes the Eifelian as 393.3–387.7 Ma; ICS 2024/12 now gives 393.47 ± 0.99 to 387.95 ± 1.04 Ma. The "390 Ma" figure is the press-release midpoint [secondary], not a direct date.
- **Cairo, New York: mid-Givetian, c. 385–386 Ma.** Described in 2020 as "the oldest forest … yet identified worldwide" (Stein et al. 2020) [abstract]. The abstract gives the stage, not a number; ICS 2024/12 puts the Givetian at 387.95–382.31 Ma. "386 Ma" comes from press coverage [secondary].
- **Gilboa, New York: late Middle Devonian**, slightly younger than Cairo (Stein et al. 2007, 2012) [abstract]. Usually quoted as c. 385 Ma.
- **Archaeopteris forests** were widespread in the Late Devonian; the anatomically studied Moroccan material is c. 370 Ma (Meyer-Berthaud et al. 1999) [abstract].
- **Note on the Devonian scale.** ICS 2024/12 stage ages differ by up to about 1 Myr from those in papers before 2023. I believe the new values follow the recalibration of Harrigan et al. 2022 [memory; their abstract does not list the numbers].

### What is known
- The Gilboa stumps (*Eospermatopteris*) and the crown *Wattieza* are one plant: a cladoxylopsid at least 8 m tall, tree-fern-like, with a flat base and many small anchoring roots, shedding its branches as litter (Stein et al. 2007) [abstract].
- A 1,200 m² mapped floor at Gilboa shows a mixed-age stand of these trees, with large woody creeping aneurophytalean progymnosperms running between them and tree-sized lycopsids also present. The setting was a wetland coastal plain, periodically disturbed (Stein et al. 2012) [abstract].
- Cairo has *Eospermatopteris* too, in a periodically drier setting, plus very extensive root systems assigned to the *Archaeopteris* group, "essentially comparable to modern seed plants" (Stein et al. 2020) [abstract].
- The Somerset trees are the cladoxylopsid *Calamophyton*, densely spaced on a large river-plain system subject to seasonal disturbance, shedding abundant debris (Davies et al. 2024) [abstract]. Heights of 2–4 m come from the Cambridge press release [secondary].
- Lycopsid forests with trunks 15–20 cm apart grew in the tropics of Svalbard by c. 380 Ma (Berry & Marshall 2015) [abstract].

### What is debated
- **Which site is "first".** Somerset has the oldest standing trees, but they were small and not woody in the modern sense; Cairo has the oldest forest with deep-rooted woody trees. Either can be called first, depending on the definition of "forest".
- **Effect on CO2 and climate.** The "Devonian plant hypothesis" holds that deep-rooted trees sped up rock weathering, thickened soils, fertilised the seas (causing anoxia and extinctions), and drew CO2 down by as much as 90 per cent through the Palaeozoic (Algeo & Scheckler 1998; Berner 1997; Morris et al. 2015) [abstract]. Dahl et al. 2022 dispute the size of the effect: their plant-based estimates give only ~525–715 ppm CO2 at 410–380 Ma, before forests, and their model finds deep-rooted trees "did not dramatically enhance atmospheric CO2 removal"; earlier shallow-rooted plants may have done the work [abstract]. Dahl & Arens 2020 review the question and call the size of each step "still debated" [abstract].

### What a painter could show
- **Gilboa: stands of palm- or tree-fern-like trees 8 m or more tall, with bare trunks and a crown of branch-like fronds, no true leaves.** *Evidence* (Stein et al. 2007).
- **Woody vine-like plants sprawling along the ground and climbing the trunks.** *Evidence* for ground-running rhizomes; climbing is "probably" in the source, so *inference* (Stein et al. 2012).
- **Wet coastal lowland with dark muddy soil and thick leafless litter.** *Evidence* for setting and soil (Stein et al. 2012); litter is *inference* (Stein et al. 2007).
- **Cairo variant: drier ground, conifer-like *Archaeopteris* trees with spreading roots beside the cladoxylopsids.** *Evidence* for roots and setting (Stein et al. 2020). *Archaeopteris* crown shape comes from younger fossils, so *inference* for this date.
- **No flowers, no grass, no birds, no four-legged animals.** *Inference* from the fossil record; small arthropods are the only land animals expected. Green tones throughout are *convention*.

## 12. Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM)

### Date
- **Onset 56.01 ± 0.05 Ma**, from astronomical tuning (Zeebe & Lourens 2019) [abstract]. ICS 2024/12 gives 56.00 Ma for the base of the Eocene, which is defined by the PETM carbon-isotope excursion.
- **Duration 170 ± 30 kyr** from onset to the recovery inflection (Zeebe & Lourens 2019) [abstract]; ~170 kyr (Röhl et al. 2007) [abstract]; ~200 kyr (McInerney & Wing 2011) [abstract].
- **Disagreement.** Older papers give 55.0–55.9 Ma. Charles et al. 2011 obtained 55.728–55.964 Ma from U–Pb dating in Spitsbergen [abstract]. Kennett & Stott 1991 used a now-obsolete "~57.33 Myr" [abstract].

### What is known
- **Size of warming.** Global mean surface temperature rose 5.6 °C (5.4–5.9 °C, 95% CI) (Tierney et al. 2022) [abstract]. The review range is 5–8 °C (McInerney & Wing 2011) [abstract]. Absolute global mean temperature rose from about 26.3 °C to about 31.6 °C (Inglis et al. 2020) [abstract].
- **Carbon.** Thousands of petagrams of carbon were released, mostly within <20 kyr (McInerney & Wing 2011) [abstract]. The onset took at least 4,000 years, with a release rate below 1.1 Pg C per year, about a tenth of the 2014 human rate (Zeebe et al. 2016) [abstract].
- **Arctic.** Sea-surface temperature near the North Pole rose from 18 °C to over 23 °C (Sluijs et al. 2006) [abstract]. **Palms: yes.** Palm and Bombacoideae pollen occur in PETM sediments of the central Arctic Ocean core, implying winter means of 5 °C or more on nearby land (Willard et al. 2019) [abstract]. The better-known palm-pollen record is from the later ETM2 event, 53.5 Ma (Sluijs et al. 2009) [abstract]. **Crocodilians: not shown for the PETM itself.** Alligators, turtles, tapirs, primates and *Coryphodon* are known from Ellesmere Island in early–middle Eocene rocks, c. 53–38 Ma (Eberle & Greenwood 2012) [abstract]. That is the warm early Eocene, at least 3 Myr after the PETM. I found no Arctic vertebrate fossils dated to the PETM.
- **Mammals.** The orders Artiodactyla (even-toed hoofed mammals), Perissodactyla (including horses) and Primates first appear on all three northern continents at the PETM (Gingerich 2006) [abstract]. The primate *Teilhardina* spread from Asia through Europe to North America in about 25,000 years (Smith et al. 2006) [abstract]. In Wyoming the immigrants caused an abrupt rise in species richness (Clyde & Gingerich 1998) [abstract].
- **Dwarfing.** The earliest horses shrank by about 30 per cent over the first ~130,000 years, then grew by about 76 per cent in the recovery (Secord et al. 2012) [abstract]. Dwarfing recurred, less strongly (~14 per cent), at ETM2 (D'Ambrosia et al. 2017) [abstract]. The horse genus in Secord et al. is *Sifrhippus* [memory; the abstract does not name it].
- **Deep sea.** 30–50 per cent of deep-sea benthic foraminifera species went extinct; plankton turned over but had no major extinction (Thomas 2007) [abstract]. Kennett & Stott 1991 called it one of the largest deep-sea benthic extinctions of the past 90 Myr [abstract].
- **Plants.** In Wyoming, floras became a mix of native and immigrant lineages within 10,000 years; climate was dry early in the event and wetter later (Wing et al. 2005) [abstract].

### What is debated
- **Carbon source.** Methane hydrate from the sea floor: 1.1–2.1 × 10^18 g of carbon at −60‰ would explain the isotope shift (Dickens et al. 1995) [abstract]. Volcanic carbon from the North Atlantic Igneous Province: more than 10,000 Pg, isotopically heavier (Gutjahr et al. 2017) [abstract]. Both: volcanism as trigger, then a second reservoir released in response to warming (Kender et al. 2021) [abstract].
- **Trigger.** Zeebe & Lourens 2019 place the onset near an orbital eccentricity maximum, suggesting an orbital trigger; Charles et al. 2011 argued the timing differs from the later orbitally paced hyperthermals [abstracts].
- **Speed of onset.** "<20 kyr" (review) versus "at least 4 kyr" (Zeebe et al. 2016); both are consistent, but exact duration is unresolved.
- **Why benthic foraminifera died.** Thomas 2007 says the cause is "not clear"; warming itself is the only globally uniform candidate [abstract].

### What a painter could show
- **Wyoming floodplain with small early mammals: a cat-sized horse, small primates in trees, early even-toed hoofed mammals.** *Evidence* for the animals and their small size (Gingerich 2006; Secord et al. 2012). "Cat-sized" is [memory]; check against Secord et al.
- **Open, seasonally dry vegetation early in the event rather than lush swamp forest.** *Evidence* from leaf fossils (Wing et al. 2005). Red-banded soils in the Bighorn Basin rocks are [memory].
- **Arctic coast: mixed forest with palms among swamp conifers, ice-free sea.** *Evidence* from pollen (Willard et al. 2019) and sea temperatures (Sluijs et al. 2006). Whole palms in a landscape are *inference* from pollen. Swamp-cypress forests are documented for the PETM recovery phase.
- **Arctic alligators.** *Evidence* only for c. 53 Ma and later (Eberle & Greenwood 2012). Showing them in a PETM scene is *inference by extension*; label it or move the scene to the early Eocene.
- **Polar light.** *Inference*: the Arctic had months of darkness and months of low sun then as now. A low, long-shadowed sun over a warm forest is physically sound. Hazy "greenhouse" skies are *convention*.

## 13. Antarctica freezes over (Eocene–Oligocene transition)

### Date
- **Eocene–Oligocene boundary: 33.9 Ma** (ICS 2024/12; Hutchinson et al. 2021 [full text, part read]).
- **Transition (EOT): 34.44 to 33.65 Ma, about 790 kyr.** It starts at a nannofossil extinction and ends at the peak of the large oxygen-isotope step (Hutchinson et al. 2021) [full text].
- **Main ice-growth step:** the "Earliest Oligocene oxygen Isotope Step" (EOIS), ending c. 33.65 Ma, after the epoch boundary. It is followed by an early Oligocene glacial maximum about 500 kyr long (Hutchinson et al. 2021) [full text]; Klages et al. 2024 date that maximum to ~33.7–33.2 Ma [abstract].
- **"Oi-1".** The name comes from Miller et al. 1991. Hutchinson et al. 2021 deliberately avoid it because authors use it for different things (the step, the peak or the whole glacial interval) [full text]. If the site uses "Oi-1", define it as the earliest Oligocene glaciation at c. 33.65 Ma.
- **Structure.** Two jumps of about 40,000 years each (Coxall et al. 2005) [abstract]. The first step is "mostly attributable to ocean cooling, with subordinate ice sheet growth" (Hutchinson et al. 2021) [full text]; the second is the main ice-growth step.

### What is known
- **Ice.** The new ice sheet was probably 70–110 per cent of the volume of today's Antarctic ice sheet, and sea level fell by the order of 70 m across the transition (Hutchinson et al. 2021) [full text].
- **Cooling.** Sea surfaces cooled by 1–5 °C in both hemispheres, more (4–8 °C) in some subantarctic records (Hutchinson et al. 2021) [full text].
- **CO2.** Ice-sheet models give a threshold near 750 ppm (DeConto & Pollard 2003; Pearson et al. 2009) [abstracts]. A data–model comparison best fits a CO2 fall of about 40 per cent (~325 ppm), which the authors call an upper estimate (Hutchinson et al. 2021) [abstract].
- **The ice sheet was not complete at first.** A stable, continent-scale ice sheet reaching the coast formed only at c. 32.8 Ma, when CO2 fell below ~600 ppm (Galeotti et al. 2016) [abstract]. West Antarctica's Pacific margin stayed ice-free and cool-temperate during the early Oligocene maximum (Klages et al. 2024) [abstract].
- **Before: Seymour Island, Antarctic Peninsula.** Land mammals from the Eocene La Meseta Formation include polydolopid marsupials (Woodburne & Zinsmeister 1982) and South American-type hoofed mammals, astrapotheres and litopterns (Marenssi et al. 1994; Bond et al. 2006) [abstracts]. Giant penguins lived there in the late Eocene; *Palaeeudyptes klekowskii* is estimated to be the largest and most massive penguin described (Acosta Hospitaleche 2014) [abstract]. A height of about 2 m is [secondary].
- **Before: vegetation.** Latest Eocene pollen from the top of the La Meseta Formation records low-diversity forest dominated by southern beech (*Nothofagus*) with some podocarp conifers, "similar to Valdivian-type forest found today in Chile and Argentina". Just below the transition, land pollen declines and sea-ice indicators swamp the samples (Warny et al. 2019) [abstract]. Mountain glaciers already existed on the Peninsula at c. 37–34 Ma (Anderson et al. 2011) [abstract].
- **After.** A mosaic of southern beech and conifer woodland and tundra persisted on the northern Peninsula through the Oligocene; tundra pockets lasted to at least 12.8 Ma (Anderson et al. 2011) [abstract].

### What is debated
- **Cause: CO2 or gateways.** Kennett 1977 proposed that the opening of the Tasmanian and Drake passages isolated Antarctica thermally. DeConto & Pollard 2003 found CO2 decline to be the main driver, with gateways secondary. Hutchinson et al. 2021 agree CO2 is primary, but note that gateway changes could themselves have lowered CO2. Sauermilch et al. 2021 revive a gateway role: high-resolution models show 2–4 °C cooling of Antarctic surface waters once both gateways deepen [abstracts].
- **Gateway timing.** Drake Passage estimates range from 49 to 17 Ma (Scher & Martin 2006) [abstract]. A deep Tasman Gateway is put at 33.5 ± 1.5 Ma (Hutchinson et al. 2021) [full text].
- **How large the first ice sheet was** and whether Northern Hemisphere ice also grew (Coxall et al. 2005 raised this) [abstract].

### Relation to the "Eocene–Oligocene extinction" and the "Grande Coupure"
**Short answer: largely yes, they are the same climatic event seen from different places, but the three terms are not synonyms.**
- **Antarctic glaciation** is the physical event.
- **"Eocene–Oligocene extinction"** is the biological response worldwide. It is not one sharp extinction. Prothero 1994 treats it as a series of extinctions spread over c. 40–30 Ma [abstract]. The sharpest marine pulse is near the boundary, with over 90 per cent turnover of mollusc species on the US Gulf coast, plausibly from colder winters (Ivany et al. 2000) [abstract].
- **"Grande Coupure"** is the European land-mammal part of that response: many endemic European mammals died out and Asian immigrants replaced them. It falls in the earliest Oligocene and coincides with the first big glaciation (Hooker et al. 2004) [abstract]; Spanish data place it within 0.5 Myr of the boundary (Costa et al. 2011) [secondary: repository abstract seen in search results]. Asia shows a matching turnover, the "Mongolian Remodelling", from large odd-toed hoofed mammals in forest to rodents and rabbits in dry steppe (Sun et al. 2014 [abstract]; term per Hutchinson et al. 2021 [full text]).
- **Caveat.** The Grande Coupure has a second cause that the glaciation only partly explains: immigration from Asia, linked to the closing of a seaway east of Europe. Hooker et al. 2004 conclude that "climate change combined with competition" produced it [abstract].
- **Recommendation.** If the site lists these as separate entries, cross-reference them and give them the same date (c. 34–33.5 Ma).

- **Just before (latest Eocene, Peninsula coast): cool rainforest of southern beech and podocarps, like today's Valdivian forest of southern Chile.** *Evidence* from pollen (Warny et al. 2019). The Valdivian comparison is the authors' own.
- **Giant penguins on the shore, taller than emperor penguins.** *Evidence* (Acosta Hospitaleche 2014). Plumage colour is *convention*.
- **Small marsupials and hoofed mammals in the forest.** *Evidence* for presence in the middle–late Eocene (Woodburne & Zinsmeister 1982; Marenssi et al. 1994). Their survival to the very end of the Eocene is *inference*.
- **Glaciers already on distant mountains before the main event.** *Evidence* (Anderson et al. 2011).
- **After: ice sheet over East Antarctica with tundra and scrubby beech woodland surviving on the Peninsula and an ice-free, cool West Antarctic coast.** *Evidence* (Anderson et al. 2011; Klages et al. 2024). A wholly white continent at 33.6 Ma would be wrong.
- **Light.** *Inference*: at 60–70° S, long summer days and dark winters; low-angle light is correct.

## 14. Messinian salinity crisis and Zanclean refilling

### Date
- **Onset 5.971 Ma** (Manzi et al. 2013, refining 5.96 ± 0.02 Ma of Krijgsman et al. 1999) [abstracts].
- **End 5.333 Ma**, the base of the Zanclean Stage and of the Pliocene, defined in Sicily (Van Couvering et al. 2000 [abstract]; ICS 2024/12).
- **Stages** (consensus scheme of Roveri et al. 2014, as summarised by Andreetto et al. 2021 [abstract]): Stage 1, marginal gypsum, 5.97–5.60 Ma. Stage 2, halite on the basin floor, 5.60–5.55 Ma. Stage 3, Upper Gypsum then brackish "Lago-Mare", 5.55–5.33 Ma.
- **Disagreement** is about what happened, not when. One group argues deep-basin halite began earlier and lasted longer (Meilijson et al. 2019, title only; abstract not read).

### What is known
- **Salt volume.** More than 1 million km³, about 5 per cent of the ocean's dissolved salt (Ryan 2009) [abstract]. A seismic survey gives 821–927 thousand km³ of salt, or 1.2 ± 0.1 million km³ with interbedded sediment (Haq et al. 2020) [secondary: abstract as indexed]. Krijgsman et al. 2024 put the extraction at ~7–10 per cent of ocean evaporite ions [abstract].
- **Salt thickness.** More than 2 km of halite on the basin floor (Andreetto et al. 2021) [abstract]; up to about 1 km of Messinian units in the western abyssal basins and up to 325 m at intermediate depths on the Balearic Promontory (Heida et al. 2022) [abstract].
- **Timing of the halite.** The thick deep-basin salt formed in a short interval, c. 5.60–5.55 Ma (Andreetto et al. 2021) [abstract].
- **Drawdown evidence, eastern basin.** Chlorine isotopes in halite indicate two phases: about 35 kyr of salt deposition in a brine-filled basin, then a drawdown in less than 10 kyr of 1.7–2.1 km in the east and about 0.85 km in the west. The eastern basin lost up to 83 per cent of its water, yet its deepest parts kept more than 1 km of brine (Aloisi et al. 2024) [abstract]. A river deposit more than 500 km long lies on top of the deep-basin salt off the Levant (Madof et al. 2019) [abstract].
- **Drawdown evidence, western basin.** Restored shorelines give a fall of about 1,100–1,500 m (Heida et al. 2022) [abstract].
- **Canyons.** The buried Messinian Nile valley reaches up to 2,500 m deep beneath the delta, a figure quoted by Garcia-Castellanos et al. 2009 [secondary] and attributed to Barber 1981, which I could not read. Gvirtzman et al. 2022 restore the Nile canyon for later subsidence and get an original river base level only ~600 m below present sea level [abstract]. For the Rhône, Clauzon 1982 presents the canyon as "decisive proof" of the desiccated-basin model (title); the commonly quoted depth of about 1 km is [memory].
- **Refilling.** An erosion channel crosses the Strait of Gibraltar: more than 250 m deep and 200 km long in the 2009 paper, nearly 390 km long with ~1,000 km³ removed in the 2020 review. Modelled peak discharge is about 10^8 m³/s, a thousand times the Amazon. Ninety per cent of the water arrived within a few months to two years, and sea level rose by more than 10 m per day at peak (Garcia-Castellanos et al. 2009, 2020) [abstracts].
- **Eastern flood evidence.** A buried chaotic sediment body of ~1,600 km³ in the Ionian Sea at the foot of the Malta Escarpment, next to a 6 km wide amphitheatre-shaped canyon (Micallef et al. 2018; Garcia-Castellanos et al. 2020) [abstracts]. On land in south-east Sicily there are more than 300 streamlined ridges aligned with the flow, a breccia layer between Messinian and Zanclean rocks, and a 20 km wide channel leading to the Noto Canyon (Micallef et al. 2024) [abstract].

### What is debated
- **Full desiccation versus deep brine.** The original model has a dry basin floor more than 3 km below sea level with shrinking brine pools (Hsü et al. 1973; Ryan 2009). The opposing model has salt precipitating from deep brine in a basin that stayed full, with canyons cut underwater by dense salty currents (Roveri et al. 2014, *American Journal of Science*; Lugli et al. 2015) [abstract for the first; title for the second]. The 2024 chlorine-isotope study supports both in sequence: deep brine first, then a large but incomplete drawdown [abstract]. The Nile restoration by Gvirtzman et al. 2022 argues for a drawdown of only ~600 m. Andreetto et al. 2021 call the debate "still vibrant" [abstract].
- **Megaflood or gradual reconnection.** The megaflood is described by its own proponents as "one of the competing scenarios" (Garcia-Castellanos et al. 2020) [abstract]. The alternative holds that the basin was already full of brackish "Lago-Mare" water at high level before normal marine water returned (discussed in Andreetto et al. 2021) [abstract]. The megaflood requires a large drawdown immediately beforehand, so the two debates are linked.
- **Cause of isolation.** Mainly tectonic closure of the Atlantic gateways, with the exact timing set by climate cycles (Krijgsman et al. 1999; Manzi et al. 2013) [abstracts].

## 15. Great American Biotic Interchange (GABI)

### Date
- **Land bridge.** The Isthmus of Panama "sensu stricto" formed around 2.8 Ma. This is pinned by the end of surface-water exchange between the oceans at 2.76 Ma and an acceleration of mammal dispersal "sometime just before 2.7 Ma"; brief breaches may have occurred as late as 2.45 Ma (O'Dea et al. 2016) [full text]. Webb 2006 gives "about 2.7 Ma" [abstract]. Marshall et al. 1982 gave "about 3 million years ago" [abstract].
- **First main pulse.** "GABI began in earnest (GABI 1) at about 2.6–2.4 Ma" (Woodburne 2010) [full text]. O'Dea et al. 2016 describe "a surge in successful dispersals in both directions beginning around 2.6 Ma" [full text]. In temperate North America the late Blancan land-mammal age is defined by the arrival of South American animals from about 2.7 Ma (Morgan 2005; Flynn et al. 2005) [abstracts].
- **Later pulses.** GABI 2 at c. 1.8 Ma; GABI 3 at c. 0.8–0.7 Ma; GABI 4 at c. 0.125 Ma (Woodburne 2010) [full text].
- **Heralds.** From c. 9 Ma northward and c. 7.3 Ma southward (below).
- **Disagreement on closure.** Montes et al. 2015 argue from Panamanian zircons in Colombian river sediments that the Central American Seaway "had vanished" by the middle Miocene [abstract]; other papers summarise this as closure at about 15–13 Ma (Coates & Stallard 2013; O'Dea et al. 2016). Bacon et al. 2015 find waves of land dispersal at c. 20 and 6 Ma in molecular and fossil data and infer land "several millions of years earlier than commonly assumed" [abstract]. O'Dea et al. 2016 reject both as inconclusive [abstract]; Jaramillo et al. 2017 reply that they reject the 2.8 Ma conclusion [abstract]. Coates & Stallard 2013 offer a middle position: an Indonesia-like island arc from c. 15 Ma with narrow straits until 4–3 Ma [abstract]. The debate is partly about definitions: a deep seaway closed early; the last shallow straits closed late.

### The question: Pliocene or Pleistocene?
**Answer: the main pulse straddles the boundary, and by the current time scale most of it is Pleistocene.**

- The base of the Pleistocene, and of the Quaternary, is at 2.58 Ma (ICS 2024/12). It was lowered from 1.8 Ma in 2009, when the Gelasian Stage was moved from the Pliocene into the Pleistocene (Gibbard et al. 2010) [abstract].
- The closure of the land bridge (c. 2.8 Ma) and the earliest arrivals of the first pulse (c. 2.7 Ma in the United States) fall in the **latest Pliocene** (Piacenzian, Neogene), by 0.1–0.2 Myr.
- The first pulse as Woodburne defines it (2.6–2.4 Ma) falls at and just after the boundary, in the **earliest Pleistocene** (Gelasian, Quaternary). The later pulses at 1.8, 0.8 and 0.125 Ma are all Pleistocene.
- **The dating is not precise enough to put the start cleanly on one side.** Morgan 2005 can only bin the first late-Blancan arrivals into "2.2–2.7 Ma" sites. An honest label is "around the Pliocene–Pleistocene boundary, about 2.7–2.6 Ma".
- **Trap in older literature.** Papers before 2010, and some after, call the main pulse "late Pliocene" because the boundary then stood at 1.8 Ma. Examples: Morgan 2005 ("late Pliocene and early Pleistocene, from 2.7 to 1.5 Ma"); MacFadden et al. 2007 ("late Pliocene (ca. 2.2–1.8 Ma)" for the Florida terror bird, which is early Pleistocene on today's scale).
- **Suggested wording for the site's table:** "c. 2.7 Ma, latest Pliocene to early Pleistocene (Neogene–Quaternary boundary)". If a single period must be chosen, Quaternary is defensible for the pulse as a whole; Neogene is defensible only for the land bridge and the first arrivals.

**Against the brief's list**
- *Going north.* **Ground sloths** crossed long before 2.58 Ma (c. 9 Ma), except the giant *Eremotherium* (after). **Glyptodonts** reached Mexico before 3 Ma and the US at c. 2.7 Ma (before, narrowly). **Terror birds** reached Texas c. 5 Ma (before); the Florida fossils are 2.2–1.8 Ma.
- *Going south.* **Gomphotheres, sabretooth cats, bears and deer** are securely documented in South America only after 2.58 Ma (c. 1.8 Ma). **Camels**: the llama lineage before (c. 3.3 Ma), *Palaeolama* and *Hemiauchenia* after. **Horses**: *Hippidion* at about the boundary (c. 2.6 Ma); *Equus* long after.

**Cautions on this list**
- The South American dates lean on the Argentine land-mammal ages, which have few radiometric ties. The "c. 1.8 Ma" Ensenadan arrivals are given as c. 2.0 Ma in another scheme (Carrillo et al. 2015) [full text]. Either way they post-date 2.58 Ma.
- A cat of the puma lineage from north-west Argentina may be late Pliocene–early Pleistocene, but its exact level is uncertain (Ercoli et al. 2019) [abstract]. So "cats only after 2.58 Ma" is probable, not certain.
- A late Miocene (c. 9 Ma) gomphothere, peccaries and tapirs claimed from the Acre region of western Amazonia (Campbell et al. 2010) "await further clarifications" and are not accepted in the reviews read (Carrillo et al. 2015) [full text].
- The South American "*Stegomastodon*" is, I believe, now usually called *Notiomastodon* following Mothé & Avilla 2015 [memory; abstract not read]. Check before naming the animal on the site.

### What else is known
- More mammal genera went south (32) than north (17) (Woodburne 2010) [full text]. About half of living South American mammal genera descend from northern immigrants (Webb 2006) [abstract].
- The imbalance arose from higher extinction among native South American mammals, not from more or faster-diversifying immigrants (Carrillo et al. 2020) [abstract].
- The last of South America's native marsupial-relative carnivores (sparassodonts) and its large terror birds disappear at c. 3 Ma, before the main arrival of northern carnivores (Prevosti et al. 2021) [abstract].
- Climate mattered as much as the bridge. The pulses match glacial intervals, when savanna-like habitat replaced rainforest on the isthmus (Woodburne 2010) [full text].

### What is debated
- When the isthmus closed (above).
- Whether early crossers walked or rafted. O'Dea et al. 2016 note that the pre-3 Ma crossers (raccoon family, rodents, monkeys, sloths) belong to groups known to cross water, which "argues in favor of separation" [full text]. Bacon et al. 2015 read the same early dispersals as evidence of early land [abstract].
- Whether northern carnivores drove native South American predators extinct. The timing in Prevosti et al. 2021 and Soibelzon & Schubert 2011 suggests little overlap [abstracts].

### What a painter could show
- **Setting: open savanna or dry woodland on the isthmus during a glacial interval, not rainforest.** *Inference* from the habitats of the animals that crossed and the match to glacial stages (Woodburne 2010).
- **A first-pulse scene, c. 2.6–2.4 Ma.** Going north: glyptodont, pampathere, armadillo, giant ground sloth, porcupine, capybara. Going south: fox-like dogs, grison-like weasels, the short-legged horse *Hippidion*, llama-like camels and peccaries (already present). *Evidence* for each animal near this date (Woodburne 2010).
- **Do not put bears, sabretooth cats, deer, tapirs or mastodon-like gomphotheres into a scene dated 2.7 Ma.** *Evidence*: they are recorded in South America only from c. 1.8 Ma. They belong in a second-pulse scene.
- **Terror bird.** *Evidence* for *Titanis* in Texas c. 5 Ma and in Florida 2.2–1.8 Ma. It never met the northern carnivores in a "first encounter" on the land bridge as far as the fossils show; a face-off on the isthmus is *convention*.
- **Coats, colours and herd behaviour** are *convention* or *inference* from living relatives. Sky and light are a free choice; a dry-season tropical sky suits the inferred habitat.
