## How to read the verification marks

Exactly as in the first dossier:

- **[V]** The reference exists (record checked) **and** the specific claim was read this session in its abstract, open full text, or the institution's page.
- **[R]** The reference exists (record checked), but the full text is paywalled or was **not re-read this session**. The claim attached to it is what the work is generally cited for; the editor should confirm the page before relying on it.
- **UNVERIFIED** I could not confirm the reference or the claim. Do not use without checking.

How things were read this session, so the marks can be weighed:

- Journal records were checked in Crossref; abstracts were read through Europe PMC or OpenAlex. "V abstract" means the abstract only.
- Three papers were read in open full text through Europe PMC: Oktaviana et al. 2024, Oktaviana et al. 2026, Brumm et al. 2021.
- UNESCO World Heritage pages were downloaded and read directly (the "Outstanding Universal Value" text).
- One PDF, the Louisiana Division of Archaeology's Poverty Point booklet, was read by a crude text extraction that loses some passages. Where a passage came out incomplete I say so.
- Wikipedia is cited nowhere. Search-engine summaries are never the basis of a [V].

Dates: "BC" throughout; "cal BP" = calibrated years before present; "ka" = thousand years ago.

## 1. North American earthworks

**Recommended site and date: Poverty Point (West Carroll Parish, north-east Louisiana), painted at about 1300 BC, with Mound A being raised.** The earthworks were built "in the main" 3,700 to 3,100 years ago [V, UNESCO], given by the state and the National Park Service as 1700 to 1100 BC [V]. Mound A is dated to about 3261 cal BP [V, Ortmann & Kidder 2013 abstract], which the state's booklet gives as "around 1350 B.C." on one page and "around 1300 B.C." on another [V].

Why this site: it is the largest earthwork complex of its age in North America, a World Heritage property, and it was built by people who did not farm: "it was built by hunter-fisher-gatherers" [V, UNESCO]; "the people did not grow crops or raise animals for food" [V, booklet]. It sits comfortably inside the picture's date range (it is older than La Venta and roughly contemporary with Anyang).

Why 1300 BC: it fixes what stands. By the state's sequence [V, booklet]: Mound B is the oldest earthwork (about 1700 BC); Mound E and the first ridges follow; then Mound C in the plaza; Mound A about 1350–1300 BC; Mound F about 1200 BC; **Mound D about AD 700**, by a much later people. So at 1300 BC the scene may show Mounds B, E and C, the ridges, the plaza and Mound A under construction or newly finished; **no Mound F and no Mound D.**

**Structures.**
- **The ridges.** Six concentric C-shaped earthen ridges, outer diameter 1.14 km, bordering a flat plaza [V, UNESCO]. "Each ridge is divided by four aisles that extend from the plaza. The highest ridge stands over 6 feet tall. The lowest ridges, to the south, are less than 1 foot tall" [V, booklet: these are today's heights]. The swales between them are 65 to 100 feet wide [V, booklet]. They never formed a full ring: "there were never any ridges" on the open (bayou) side [V, booklet, sentence partly lost in extraction]. Some ridges were built in stages and others all at once [V, booklet]; one, Ridge West 3, is argued to have been built exceptionally fast (Kidder et al. 2021 [R: title read, text not]). **Original height is not known**; nineteenth- and twentieth-century plowing is usually blamed for lowering them [R, Gibson 2000]. Draw them low and broad, not as ramparts.
- **Mound A.** "72 feet tall, 710 feet long and 660 feet wide" (about 22 × 216 × 201 m), needing as many as 15.5 million basket loads [V, booklet]. It has three parts: a cone, a platform and a ramp joining them; it stands on a north–south line between Mounds B and E [V, booklet]. Often called the "Bird Mound"; that reading of its shape is an interpretation [R, Gibson 2000], and the painting should not sharpen it into a bird.
- **How fast.** The abstract of the study says the mound shows "no erosion events, natural episodes of soil formation, or cultural stages" and infers that it "was constructed by a large labor force over a short period of time" [V, Ortmann & Kidder 2013]. The figures "less than 90 days", "perhaps 30 days" and "about 3,000 workers" come from the university press release and news coverage of that paper [seen in search summaries only; the release itself could not be opened: **UNVERIFIED by direct reading**]. The state's own booklet is more careful: Mound A was made "in three stages"; "some archaeologists think that people built [the platform] section in less than 90 days"; and the builders "took a long break before making the ramp" [V, booklet]. Safe caption wording: "raised very quickly, perhaps in a few months"; not "built in 90 days" as flat fact.
- **Soil.** The platform was made "mixing different colored soils found around the site" [V, booklet]. A mound under construction is bare, varicolored earth carried in baskets and hides [V, booklet for baskets and hides]. Whether the finished mound was left bare, capped or grassed I could not establish.
- **The plaza and its post circles.** A 43-acre plaza, built up and leveled by hand [V, booklet]. Beneath it are rings of very large postholes: circles "up to 213 feet in diameter", holes "over 2 feet wide", posts that "may have stood 20 feet tall"; the rings overlap, the posts were reset often and old posts were pulled rather than left to rot [V, booklet; Hargrave et al. 2021 R]. So: one or two circles of tall bare timber posts standing in the plaza is defensible; many circles at once is not; lintels, roofs, carving or paint on the posts are invention. Their purpose is unknown.
- **The bayou and the "dock".** The site stands on Macon Ridge, 7 to 9 m above the lowlands, with Bayou Maçon along its eastern edge [V, UNESCO]. The south-eastern corner of the plaza slopes down to the bayou (the "dock"), and people traveled and hauled stone by dugout canoe [V, booklet]. Surroundings: hardwood forest, swamp and backwater [V, UNESCO].
- **Houses.** Very little is known, and the state says so: "they have not found any house remains" on the ridges; people are thought to have lived there because the ridges hold more artifacts and earth ovens than anywhere else; from posthole patterns "the houses may have been built somewhat haphazardly, with a mix of thin poles and cane forming the framework"; daub (mud wall-covering) has been found; "the roofs may have been hides, thatch or palmetto fronds" [V, booklet]. Small, irregular pole-and-cane huts with mud daub, loosely scattered along the ridge tops, is as far as the evidence goes. Neat rows of identical houses are an artist's convention.
- **No burial mounds.** No prehistoric graves have been found at the site; the only known graves are those of nineteenth-century settlers and enslaved people [V, booklet; Saunders, 64 Parishes].

**Objects and tools.**
- **"Poverty Point objects" (PPOs):** hand-molded lumps of fired local silt, "about the size of modern charcoal briquettes", heated and used in earth ovens (a pit of hot PPOs, covered, to roast or steam food) [V, booklet; V, 64 Parishes]. Shapes: biconical, cylindrical grooved, cross grooved, melon shaped [V, booklet]. The site's commonest artifact, and the single most characteristic thing to show.
- **Plummets:** teardrop-shaped weights ground from heavy iron ore (magnetite, hematite), "possibly net weights" for fishing; the booklet adds they "may have also" served as loom weights [V].
- **Soapstone (steatite) bowls,** carved at quarries in what are now Georgia and Alabama and traded in finished; many have drilled repair holes [V, booklet].
- **Spear and dart points; the spear-thrower (atlatl) with stone weights; gorgets** [V, booklet]. **No bow and arrow**: "the bow and arrow had not been adopted in North America yet" [V, booklet].
- **Microliths** (tiny chipped blades and drills) made from local gravel [V, booklet].
- **Lapidary work:** beads and pendants of clay, stone and copper; red jasper worked into tubular beads and small animal forms; the red jasper **owl pendants** are iconic but "very rare ... fewer than 30 have been found, mostly at other sites" [V, booklet; V, 64 Parishes]. One or two at most.
- **Clay figurines:** small, mostly female torsos "made without arms or legs", most now headless; meaning unknown [V, booklet].
- **A little pottery:** some imported, some made on site, "the earliest ever made in the Lower Mississippi Valley"; scarce beside the soapstone and PPOs [V, booklet]. Cane basketry is attested by impressions on fired earth [V, booklet].
- **Imported stone:** "some seventy-eight tons of exotic stone" [V, 64 Parishes], from "as far north as Iowa and as far east as the Appalachian Mountains" [V, booklet]; galena and copper among it [V, booklet]. Distances are quoted variously as up to 800 miles (the site's web page) and 1,000 miles (National Park Service) [V both].
- No metal tools (copper is ornament only), no wheel, no draft animals: the earth was moved "without ... domesticated animals, or wheeled carts" [V, povertypoint.us].

**Crops.** None. No evidence of domesticated plants, local or foreign [V, 64 Parishes]. Gathered plants: persimmon, pawpaw, muscadine grape, pecan, black walnut and others; nuts gave "meat, flour, and oil" [V, booklet; 64 Parishes]. **No maize, beans, squash gardens, sunflower plots or tobacco.**

**Domestic animals.** None kept for food [V, booklet]. Fish supplied most of the protein (at one Poverty Point-culture village over 70 percent of identifiable food bone was fish) [V, 64 Parishes]; also deer, turtles, frogs, waterfowl and small mammals [V, National Park Service]. Bone survives badly in the site's soil [V, booklet], so the list is thin. **Dog:** domestic dogs lived in the mid-continent from about 10,000 years ago (Perri et al. 2019 [V abstract]), so a dog is defensible for the period, but I found no statement that dog bone has been identified at Poverty Point itself: **not site-attested**. No horses, cattle, pigs, chickens or turkeys.

**People.** Almost nothing is known, and this should be said in the caption rather than painted over. No burials, so no skeletons and no grave goods; no textiles survive ("they have not yet found any at Poverty Point") [V, booklet]. The figurines show a few bands or belts and nothing more [V, booklet]. Beads and pendants may have been "worn like jewelry or sewn onto people's clothing" [V, booklet FAQ, sentence partly lost]. The state's page says "men and women" did the earth-moving [V, povertypoint.us]. Their name for themselves, language and religion are unknown [V, National Park Service].
**Avoid:** Plains imagery of three thousand years later and a thousand miles away (feathered war bonnets, tipis, horses, fringed buckskin); bows; maize fields; the temple-topped, palisaded platform mounds of Cahokia; burial ceremonies; a chief or priest directing the work (the excavators argue there is "no evidence ... that the mound was constructed under the aegis of a ranked socio-political system" [V, Ortmann & Kidder]); the modern mown-lawn look of the park; and any hint that the builders were someone other than Native Americans.

**Cautions.**
- **The older tradition.** Mound-building in Louisiana is two thousand years older than Poverty Point. At Watson Brake, also in north-east Louisiana, earthworks were begun about 3500 BC and substantial mound-raising ran from about 3350 BC until after 3000 BC: eleven mounds joined by ridges, built by foragers living on "nuts, fish, and deer" [V, Saunders et al. 2005 abstract; V, Saunders et al. 1997 abstract]. The 2005 paper calls these "remote antecedents" of Poverty Point; Middle Archaic mound building then ceased after about 2800 BC [V, 64 Parishes]. So Poverty Point is neither the first nor a continuous inheritance.
- **What falls outside the picture.** The better-known earthworks are too late: the Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks of Ohio were "built between 2,000 and 1,600 years ago" [V, UNESCO], that is about AD 1 to 400; Cahokia belongs to the Mississippian culture of AD 800–1350, peaking 1050–1150 [V, UNESCO]. Both are after the picture's end at about 400 BC and must not lend it their look (geometric enclosures; flat-topped temple mounds). The Adena mounds of the Ohio valley are conventionally dated from about 500 BC onward and so straddle the end date [**UNVERIFIED** this session: conventional dating, no source opened].
- **"Mound builders".** In the nineteenth century the mounds were widely credited to a vanished, superior, non-Native (often "white") race said to have been destroyed by the ancestors of living Native Americans; the story "rationalized land-grabs, removals, and genocidal wars", was invoked by President Jackson in 1830, and was dismantled by the Smithsonian's Cyrus Thomas in 1894 [V, JSTOR Daily summary of Howey 2010; Howey 2010 itself R]. The phrase "Mound Builders", capitalized as if it named a people, carries that history. **Better wording:** "the earthworks of Poverty Point"; "Native American earthworks" or "monumental earthworks of ancient North America"; "the people who built Poverty Point"; "ancestors of today's Native Americans". The state site says "American Indians" throughout [V]; UNESCO's criterion text calls the Poverty Point culture "a vanished cultural tradition" [V], a phrase I would not echo because "vanished" is exactly what the myth claimed of the people.
- **Descendants.** The state's FAQ says that links between Poverty Point and particular present-day tribes cannot be made clear after more than 3,000 years [V, booklet FAQ, answer partly lost in extraction]; the booklet does say the people who added Mound D about AD 700 "were probably the descendants of the earlier earthwork builders" [V]. Do not name a tribe as the builders.
- "City", "capital" and "trade center" are interpretations; how many people lived there year-round ("hundreds ... maybe more" [V, booklet]) and how many gathered periodically is debated.
- The name is that of a nineteenth-century plantation [R, Gibson 2000].

## 2. Indonesian cave art

**Recommended site and date: Leang Karampuang (Maros-Pangkep karst, South Sulawesi, Indonesia), "at least 51,200 years ago".** The date is a minimum and must be written as one.

**What the dated panel shows** [all V, Oktaviana et al. 2024, full text]. A panel on the **ceiling** of the cave's southern chamber, found in 2017, in "a poor state of preservation owing to extensive exfoliation of the limestone rock surface", and partly hidden under small knobbly mineral growths (coralloid speleothems, "cave popcorn"). It shows:
- one pig, identified as a Sulawesi warty pig (*Sus celebensis*), much the largest figure, "standing in a static position with its mouth partly open";
- "at least three human-like figures" (H1 to H3), smaller, painted in "the same red-hued pigment". H1 is a large figure with extended arms and no legs depicted, with "a stick-like object in its left hand"; H2 holds a rod-like object with one end near the pig's throat; H3 (35 × 5 cm) is upside down, legs splayed, one hand "seemingly touching the pig's head". Pigment traces suggest a fourth figure may have been lost;
- hand stencils: at least two seem contemporary with the scene, and one darker stencil lies under the pig and is therefore older.
The authors call it a "narrative composition" and decline to say what is happening. "Human-like" is their careful term: these are stick-like figures, not portraits, and at the neighboring site of Leang Bulu' Sipong 4 the comparable figures are read as part-human, part-animal beings [V, Aubert et al. 2019 abstract]. **The painter should copy what is on the rock (a red outline pig and small red figures), not a tidied "hunting scene".**

**The earlier papers, so the numbers are not mixed** [V abstracts unless noted]:
- Aubert et al. 2014: hand stencil at least 39,900 years old; an animal painting at least 35,400 years old, described then as a babirusa ("pig-deer") and in the team's later papers simply as a pig-like animal [V, Brumm et al. 2021 full text].
- Aubert et al. 2019: Leang Bulu' Sipong 4, a 4.5 m panel of human-like figures with pigs and dwarf buffalo (anoa), at least 43,900 years; re-dated in 2024 to at least 48,000 (50.2 ± 2.2 ka).
- Brumm et al. 2021: Leang Tedongnge, a life-size warty pig (136 × 54 cm) with head crest and facial warts, at least 45,500 years.
- Oktaviana et al. 2024: Leang Karampuang, 53.5 ± 2.3 ka, so at least 51,200 years.
- Oktaviana et al. 2026: a faded hand stencil at Liang Metanduno on Muna Island, south-east Sulawesi, "at least 67.8 thousand years ago (71.6 ± 3.8 ka)"; the authors call it the oldest dated cave art attributable to our species [V, full text]. It is a 14 × 10 cm patch of pigment, not a scene, so Karampuang remains the right subject for a vignette; but **the caption may no longer call Karampuang "the oldest cave art"**: it is the oldest known *picture of something* (representational art).

**Structures.** None. A natural limestone cave in the Maros-Pangkep karst, a landscape of steep limestone towers rising from a flat plain [R: the tower-karst description is standard for the area; I did not read a passage stating it]. At Karampuang the art is "on the overhead ceiling and adjoining upper walls" [V]. How the painters reached the ceiling, and whether the chamber was lit by daylight, I could not establish. **Unlike Lascaux, no lamps or hearths are attested here; do not import the stone lamp.**

**Objects and tools.**
- Pigment: red and "dark red/purplish (mulberry) mineral pigments (ochre)", applied "using brushwork and/or fingertips"; animals drawn as a body outline in profile, "infilled with irregular patterns of painted lines and dashes" [V, Brumm et al. 2021]. Hand stencils made by spraying pigment around a hand; some Sulawesi stencils have deliberately narrowed, pointed fingers [V, Oktaviana et al. 2026].
- Black charcoal drawings in the same caves belong to a much later, Austronesian-era tradition [V, Brumm et al. 2021]: **red only.**
- Excavations at nearby rock-art sites (Leang Bulu Bettue, Leang Burung 2) show people living in the caves in the Late Pleistocene [V, Brumm et al. 2021]; the used ochre pieces and small ornaments reported from Leang Bulu Bettue (Brumm et al. 2017, *PNAS* [R: cited in the 2026 paper's reference list; not read]) are tens of thousands of years younger than the Karampuang panel. For 51,200 years ago, no tool kit is attested: stone flakes and lumps of ochre are the defensible minimum.

**Crops.** None. **Domestic animals.** None, and no dog.

**Animals on the wall.** Sulawesi warty pig: by far the commonest animal in this art (81.1 percent of animal images), "a small (40 to 85 kg), short-legged pig with characteristic facial warts" and a head crest [V, Brumm et al. 2021]. Anoa (dwarf buffalo): present at Leang Bulu' Sipong 4 and possibly at Karampuang (head and neck only) [V]. Babirusa: possible but, as above, the best-known example has been downgraded to "pig-like". **Must not appear:** anything from European cave art (horses, aurochs, bison, deer, mammoths, lions, rhinoceroses); and the large mammals of mainland Asia (tigers, elephants, rhinoceroses, orangutans), which are absent from Sulawesi, an island never joined to the Asian mainland [R: standard biogeography, not sourced this session].

**People.** **Who painted it is an inference, not a finding.** No human bones of this age have been found with the art. The team's own statement is the one to follow: "we are unable to definitively conclude that the dated figurative rock art depiction ... is the handiwork of cognitively 'modern' members of our species. However, this seems to be the most likely explanation given the sophistication of this early representational artwork and the fact that figurative depiction has so far only been attributed to [modern humans] everywhere else in the world" [V, Brumm et al. 2021]. An earlier, unidentified archaic human was on Sulawesi 194,000–118,000 years ago, known from stone tools only [V, same]. The 2024 and 2026 papers attribute the art to *Homo sapiens* without new skeletal evidence [V]. Nothing is known of the painters' appearance, hair, dress or ornament at this date.
**Avoid:** copying Lascaux (lamps, antler points, fur clothing, pale skin: this is an equatorial island); modern Indonesian or Papuan dress or ornament projected back 50,000 years; a hunt staged in front of the wall as if the panel were a snapshot; a confident "shaman"; restoring the panel to a crisp, complete state.

**Cautions.**
- **The dating method and its doubts.** Nobody has dated the paint. The method dates thin mineral crusts that grew over it, by uranium-series decay, so every figure is a minimum age. The 2024 paper replaced the earlier approach (drilling out layers of crust and dissolving them) with laser sampling of a polished cross-section, on the argument that the old approach could average older and younger calcite; the same paper notes the laser method "generally provides age estimates with larger errors" [V]. The new method moved one panel's minimum age by at least 4,000 years (from 43,900 to about 48,000) [V], which shows how much rests on technique. Uranium-series dates on crusts over rock art have been challenged elsewhere (notably the "Neanderthal" dates from Spain) on the ground that uranium can leach from thin crusts and inflate ages; I did not open that literature this session: **UNVERIFIED here**, but the editor should know the dispute exists. All the Sulawesi dates come from one research group, though across several papers and two methods.
- "Earliest storytelling" and "narrative" are the authors' interpretations of figures placed together.
- The art is flaking off the walls, and the team links the loss to climate (Huntley et al. 2021 [R: title read]).
- Credit: the work is led jointly by Indonesian (BRIN, formerly ARKENAS; Hasanuddin University) and Australian (Griffith, Southern Cross) researchers; captions should not present it as a foreign discovery.

**My view: beside Lascaux, not instead of it.** Reasons. (1) The two scenes show different things: Lascaux is about *how* (lamps, pigments, a tool kit, all attested); Karampuang is about *when and where* (the oldest known picture, on an island on the way to Australia). (2) Karampuang has almost no supporting objects and no evidence for the painters' appearance, so as a replacement it would be a weaker-evidenced scene sitting in the picture's opening slot. (3) Keeping Lascaux alone, however, leaves Europe holding the "first art" position that the evidence has moved to Indonesia. So: add Karampuang as a small, wall-focused vignette (hands, red pigment, the pig and figures as they survive), place it before Lascaux, and make sure no caption calls Lascaux early or first. If there is room for only one Paleolithic scene, I would still keep Lascaux and state Sulawesi's priority in the text; that is a judgment about paintability, not importance.

## 3. New Guinea farming

**Recommended site and date: Kuk Swamp (upper Wahgi valley, Western Highlands, Papua New Guinea), painted at about 4500 BC (roughly 6,500 years ago), the mounded-cultivation phase.** World Heritage as the Kuk Early Agricultural Site [V].

**The phases** [V, Denham et al. 2003 abstract; UNESCO]:
- Phase 1, 10,220–9,910 cal BP: "plant exploitation and some cultivation" on the wetland margin. UNESCO hedges: "possibly as early as 10,000 BP".
- **Phase 2, by 6,950–6,440 cal BP: mounding cultivation.** This is where the paper puts the independent origin of agriculture ("arose independently in New Guinea by at least 6950 to 6440"), and where UNESCO puts the "technological leap".
- Phase 3, by 4,350–3,980 cal BP (about 2400–2000 BC): ditched cultivation; "drainage of the wetlands through digging of ditches with wooden tools from 4,000 BP to the present" [V, UNESCO].

**Which moment to paint.** Phase 1 is too thin and too disputed to paint as farming. Phase 2 carries the headline claim and the best crop evidence, but what was excavated is a buried surface of low mounds, so the scene is an inference from a ground plan. Phase 3 is the most visually specific (networks of straight ditches, and this is the phase to which wooden digging tools belong) but is the later, less remarkable moment. **My recommendation is Phase 2**, with the mounds kept low and irregular and the caption saying "by about 6,500 years ago". Phase 3 (about 2000 BC) is the fair alternative if the painter needs ditches and spades. Do not blend them: **no drainage ditches in a Phase 2 scene.**

**Structures.** Low earth mounds or raised beds on the wet margin of a swamp, giving better-drained ground for some plants beside wetter ground for others [R, Denham et al. 2003 text; Golson et al. 2017]. Setting: a broad highland valley floor at about 1,500 m [R: altitude not checked this session], a large swamp, and, by this date, grassland on cleared ground with montane rainforest beyond: forest clearance began in the early Holocene "with modification to grassland at 6950 to 6440 cal yr B.P." [V, Denham et al. 2003]. Houses: none are attested for this phase that I could confirm (house sites are reported from the later, ditched phases [R, Golson et al. 2017]). If a shelter is shown, keep it small, distant and generic.

**Objects and tools.** Stone tools carrying starch and other residues from plant processing: the evidence for taro and yam is on these tools [R, Fullagar et al. 2006: the title names "taro (Colocasia esculenta), yam (Dioscorea sp.)"; text not read]. Wooden digging sticks are a fair inference for any phase. **Wooden paddle-shaped spades** preserved in the swamp belong with the ditch-digging phases, from about 4,000 years ago onward [V, UNESCO for "wooden tools" from 4,000 BP; R, Golson et al. 2017 for the spades and their dating]: in a Phase 2 scene, digging sticks only. Ground stone axes for clearing are plausible but I did not verify their date in the highlands [R]. **No pottery** (none in mainland Papua New Guinea before about 3,000 years ago [V, O'Connor et al. 2011 abstract]); no metal.

**Crops.**
- **Banana** (*Musa*): "intensively cultivated by at least 6950 to 6440 cal yr B.P." [V, Denham et al. 2003]; the evidence is microscopic plant silica (phytoliths) in the soil [R, same paper's text].
- **Taro** (*Colocasia esculenta*): "utilized in the early Holocene" [V, Denham et al. 2003]; starch on stone tools [R, Fullagar et al. 2006].
- **Yam** (*Dioscorea*): starch on tools [R, Fullagar et al. 2006]; UNESCO names "bananas, taro and yam" [V].
- Sugarcane, often listed as a New Guinea domesticate: I found no statement of evidence for it at Kuk in anything I read: **UNVERIFIED; leave out.**
- **Trap: no sweet potato.** It is the staple of the modern highlands and its mounded gardens are what every photograph of highland farming shows, but it is an American plant [V, Roullier et al. 2013 abstract], and its arrival in the New Guinea highlands only a few centuries ago is the standard account [R, Golson et al. 2017]. Also no maize, cassava, coffee or tea (Kuk was a twentieth-century tea research station [R, Golson et al. 2017]). The bananas should not look like supermarket fruit: early cultivated bananas were smaller and seedier [R; inference from crop history, not read this session].

**Domestic animals. None.** This is the main trap.
- **No pigs.** Pigs are central to highland life today, but "current evidence shows neither pig nor pottery arrived before 3,000 calBP in mainland PNG" [V, O'Connor et al. 2011 abstract]; in the highlands they are later still [R, Sutton et al. 2009, whose abstract mentions "the introduction of exotic fauna" without dates]. A pig in a scene of 4500 BC, or of 2000 BC, is wrong by thousands of years.
- **No dogs** at this date [R: late-Holocene arrival is the usual view; not verified this session]. **No chickens** [first dossier: Peters et al. 2022].
- Wild animals that were hunted, if any are wanted at the forest edge: cassowary, tree kangaroo, possums [R, Sutton et al. 2009].

**People.** Nothing direct: no burials, figurines or pictures from Kuk at this date. Dress and ornament are unknown.
**Avoid:** modern ceremonial dress presented as ancient (bird-of-paradise plume headdresses, painted faces, human-hair wigs, pearl-shell crescents); pigs and pig feasts; sweet-potato mounds; steel axes and bush knives; neat modern grid-ditched gardens; the words "primitive" and "Stone Age people" (a phrase that has been applied to living Papua New Guineans); any suggestion that farming was brought in from Asia.

**Cautions.**
- How strong the case is for Phase 1 as cultivation, and whether Phase 2 counts as "agriculture" or as a step toward it, are argued; UNESCO's own text hedges the 10,000-year figure [V].
- UNESCO's criterion (iii) text speaks of "the culture of early man in the region" [V]: dated language, not to be copied.
- The land belongs to living customary owners (named as the Kawelka in the site literature [R, Golson et al. 2017]); UNESCO's management text refers to "the local community" and to "modern versions of traditional activities" continuing on the site [V].

## 4. Aboriginal Australia

Two candidates were researched. **My view: Budj Bim**, for the reasons given after (b).

### (a) Recommended: Budj Bim (Gunditjmara Country, south-west Victoria)

**Recommended site and date: the Budj Bim Cultural Landscape, at Tae Rak (Lake Condah); date stated as a span, "built and rebuilt over at least 6,600 years", rather than as a single year.** If a single moment is required, any time between about 4600 BC and 400 BC is equally (and equally weakly) supported; see cautions.

Why: one of "the world's most extensive and oldest aquaculture systems"; "over a period of at least 6,600 years the Gunditjmara created, manipulated and modified these local hydrological regimes" [V, UNESCO]. It is inside the picture's date range, and it shows engineering and food production by people who were not farmers. It is also owned, managed and presented to the public by the Gunditjmara themselves [V, UNESCO; budjbim.com.au], which matters for section 5.

**Structures.**
- A lava flow from the volcano Budj Bim (formerly Mount Eccles) with "a patchwork of wetlands" on it; in the black basalt, "interconnected clusters of constructed and modified water channels, weirs, dams, ponds and sinkholes" that "trap, store and harvest kooyang that migrate seasonally through the system" [V, UNESCO]. "Modified channels diverted water and kooyang ... into holding ponds", where the eels "grew fat"; they were harvested "with woven baskets set in weirs built from volcanic rocks and wood lattice structures" [V, budjbim.com.au].
- **The date.** Radiocarbon dating of charcoal in the sediment that filled an excavated channel at Lake Condah: "a 6,600 year old channel feature" [V, ANSTO, the laboratory's own account; McNiven et al. 2012 R: record verified, abstract not available to me, paper not read]. This is a minimum age for one channel. The paper is generally cited for a second phase of building within the last several hundred years [R]; a search summary gave "a dam wall was added 500 years ago", which I could not confirm.
- **Stone houses: not for this date.** Low curved (C- or U-shaped) basalt wall bases, the footings of dome-shaped huts, are found across the lava flow, and the Budj Bim site shows "reconstructed stone huts" [V, budjbim.com.au, photo caption]. But I found **no dates for them older than the nineteenth century**: the one excavation I could trace reports a mid-nineteenth-century house at Kurtonitj (McNiven et al. 2017, *Archaeology in Oceania* [**UNVERIFIED record**: seen in search results; Crossref lookup failed]). Counts ("about 70", "200 to 300") come from news and tour pages. A cluster of stone huts in a scene set before 400 BC is not defensible. If the picture is given an open, undated "over six thousand years" framing, a hut is defensible as part of the tradition, and the caption must not imply it is 6,600 years old.
- **Smoking trees.** Hollow trees said to have been used to smoke eels for keeping and trade appear in heritage summaries ("may have been used for smoke-curing eels", per a search summary of an Australian government document I could not open). **UNVERIFIED, and undated in any case.** Leave out, or ask the Gunditjmara (section 5).

**Objects and tools.** Long woven fiber eel baskets set in gaps in the weirs: the weaving of them continues today, and UNESCO lists "the construction of stone weirs and weaving of fibre baskets" among continuing practices [V]. Their form before the nineteenth century is not archaeologically attested (fiber does not survive), so the basket in the painting is the historically recorded and living form, projected back. Basalt blocks; digging sticks [inference]. Stone tools generally. **No metal, no pottery** (not made anywhere in Aboriginal Australia), no bow and arrow.

**Crops.** None in the farming sense. UNESCO speaks of supporting the "enhancement of other food resources" [V] without naming them. Do not show tilled plots.

**Domestic animals.** None. **The eel is not a domestic animal**: wild short-finned eels (kooyang, *Anguilla australis*) were diverted, held and fattened [V]. **Dingo:** reached Australia only in the last few thousand years (the usual figure is about 3,500 years ago [R: not verified this session]); a dog is wrong for the early millennia of the system and unverified for the later ones. Leave it out. No sheep or cattle (European introductions of the nineteenth century).

**People.** Gunditjmara. For the nineteenth century there are written and drawn records, and UNESCO cites "documented Gunditjmara cultural traditions" [V]; cloaks of sewn possum skin are the garment usually described for south-eastern Australia [R: not sourced this session]. For thousands of years earlier there is no direct evidence of dress. Who wove the baskets: news and tour pages say women; I did not find this in a source I would cite.
**Avoid:** desert and northern imagery from the other end of the continent (didgeridoos, dot painting, red sand, boomerang-and-spear poses); nakedness as a shorthand for "primitive"; body paint, ceremony, ancestral beings or the creation story of Budj Bim shown as a scene; any identifiable individual; the word "nomadic"; "hunter-gatherers" used as if it settled what the Gunditjmara were (see cautions); the modern drained-and-restored lake with its 2010 weir [V, UNESCO] used as if it were the ancient shoreline.

**Cautions.**
- **One dated channel carries the "6,600 years".** Everything else in the scene (weirs, baskets, huts, smoking) is known from the nineteenth century onward and from living practice. UNESCO asserts the system "retains the form and functionality it had during the last six millennia" [V]; that is nomination language resting on that channel and on Gunditjmara knowledge. An honest caption says "at least 6,600 years ago" for the first channels and makes no claim about what a trap looked like then.
- **Two kinds of deep time.** "For the Gunditjmara, deep time refers to the idea that they have always been there. From an archaeological perspective, deep time refers to a period of at least 32,000 years" of Aboriginal presence [V, UNESCO]. The volcano's eruption is dated to 36.9 ± 3.1 thousand years ago, and if Gunditjmara tradition of the Ancestral Being Budj Bim revealing himself reflects the eruption it would be among the oldest oral traditions known: the authors put this conditionally [V, Matchan et al. 2020 abstract]. The site's own page says "around 30,000 years ago" [V].
- **Words.** "Aquaculture", "eel farming", "engineers", "villages" and "husbandry" are used by UNESCO, the Federal Court (as quoted by UNESCO: "their ancestors farmed eels for food and trade") and the Gunditjmara [V]. Some Australian anthropologists and archaeologists object to describing Aboriginal economies in farming terms at all (the dispute around Bruce Pascoe's *Dark Emu* and the 2021 reply by Peter Sutton and Keryn Walshe [**not sourced this session**]). Following UNESCO's and the Gunditjmara's own wording is the defensible course; the caption should not go beyond it (no "farmers", no "towns").
- The landscape is a living cultural landscape with a history of violent dispossession in the 1840s (the Eumeralla conflict [not sourced this session]); it is not a ruin.

### (b) The alternative: the first peopling of Sahul (Madjedbebe, Mirarr Country, Northern Territory)

**Site and date:** Madjedbebe rock shelter, on the edge of the Arnhem Land escarpment; claimed first occupation "around 65,000 years ago" [V, Clarkson et al. 2017 abstract].

- **What was found:** "a distinctive stone tool assemblage including grinding stones, ground ochres, reflective additives and ground-edge hatchet heads" in the lowest occupation band [V, Clarkson et al. 2017]; charred remains of plant foods, some needing processing, dated 65,000–53,000 years [V, Florin et al. 2020 abstract; the author list includes Mirarr Traditional Owners]. Ground-edge axes of this age would be the oldest in the world.
- **The dispute, which is live.** O'Connell et al. 2018: "an age estimate of >50 ka for this site is unlikely to be valid" [V abstract]. Williams et al. 2021: artifacts may have been moved down through older sand by termites and other processes; "the early dates for human presence at Madjedbebe ... must be rejected" [V abstract]. Smith, Ward & Moffat 2020 defend the site's integrity against the termite argument [R: title and the rebuttal's description of it read]. In 2025 the argument continued in *Archaeology in Oceania* over what human DNA studies imply (Allen & O'Connell, "Recent DNA studies question a 65 kya arrival of humans in Sahul"; Veth et al., "Do recent DNA studies refute a 65 kya arrival ...?") [records verified; neither read]. The 2026 Sulawesi paper takes "about 65 ka" as its working figure for the peopling of Sahul [V]. **Unresolved.** Safe wording: "at least 50,000 years ago, and possibly 65,000".
- For a painter: a sandstone rock shelter; people grinding ochre, hafted ground-edge hatchets, grinding stones. Nothing is known of the people's appearance. The shelter's walls carry rock art [R], which is exactly what should not be copied (section 5). The landing itself (boats, a beach) has no direct evidence anywhere.

**My view: Budj Bim is the better fit, and the peopling of Sahul belongs in the text.** (1) Budj Bim falls inside the picture's span; Madjedbebe is some 40,000 years older than Lascaux and would be an outlier in a picture about the last few thousand years before 400 BC. (2) Budj Bim shows building and managed food production, which is the picture's subject, and, with Poverty Point, makes the point that monuments and engineering did not need farming. (3) Madjedbebe's date is contested in the journals; Budj Bim's 6,600 years is a minimum that nobody disputes, though it rests on one channel. Against: Budj Bim's visible detail is nineteenth-century and living practice projected back, and the dossier above says so. The 65,000-year (or 50,000-year) arrival should be stated in the text beside the Sulawesi vignette, where it belongs geographically.

## 5. Gaps to mention in the text only

One or two safe sentences each, with one verified source. These are not vetted to vignette standard.

- **Nubia (Kerma).** "South of Egypt, at Kerma on the Nile in what is now Sudan, stood the capital of the kingdom of Kush, a city of more than 30 hectares with monumental mudbrick buildings; early excavators wrongly took it for an Egyptian outpost." Source: Swiss–Sudanese archaeological mission, https://kerma.ch/en/research/ [V for the capital, the size, the mudbrick *deffufa* and the corrected "Egyptian trading post" reading]. The conventional dates for the Kerma kingdom, about 2500–1500 BC, are **not on that page and were not verified this session**. Its later successor at Napata (from about 900 BC) is on the World Heritage List: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1073 [V].
- **The Nok culture (central Nigeria).** "In central Nigeria the Nok culture began around the middle of the second millennium BC; its famous terracotta sculptures, and iron working, belong to a main phase from the ninth to the fourth century BC." Source: Franke, G. (2016). A chronology of the central Nigerian Nok culture: 1500 BC to the beginning of the Common Era. *Journal of African Archaeology* 14(3): 257–289. https://doi.org/10.3213/2191-5784-10297 [V abstract]. Inside the date range, and the strongest candidate for a future African vignette beyond Egypt.
- **Göbekli Tepe.** Already sourced in the first dossier (vignette 2, cautions, and its sources: UNESCO https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1572): monuments built by hunter-gatherers, 9600–8200 BC. It pairs naturally with Poverty Point, Budj Bim and Jōmon Japan in one sentence about building without farming.
- **The Lapita expansion.** "About 3,000 years ago people of the Lapita culture sailed into the previously uninhabited islands of the South Pacific, reaching Vanuatu and, by about 2,840 years ago, Tonga: the beginning of the last great human settlement of empty lands." (The usual fuller account, with the Bismarck Archipelago as the starting point and Fiji and Samoa on the route, is not in the two abstracts I read.) Sources: Skoglund, P. et al. (2016). Genomic insights into the peopling of the Southwest Pacific. *Nature* 538: 510–513. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature19844 [V abstract: "around 3,000 years ago ... the last major human dispersal to unpopulated lands"]; Burley, D., Weisler, M.I. & Zhao, J.-x. (2012). High precision U/Th dating of first Polynesian settlement. *PLOS ONE* 7(11): e48769. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0048769 [V abstract: Nukuleka, Tonga, "2838±8 BP"].
  **Does it deserve a vignette more than one of the four? My view: yes, more than the Sulawesi cave scene, if the aim is to show a Pacific diaspora.** Lapita is the diaspora proper (open-ocean voyaging, about 900–800 BC, inside the date range). The Sulawesi scene shows Ice Age art on the route toward Australia; it answers "Europe was not first", not "people settled the Pacific". Kuk and Budj Bim show people who had already been in place for tens of thousands of years. But I have not researched Lapita to vignette standard, and it has its own trap: no Lapita canoe survives, so the boat, the obvious centerpiece, would be reconstruction from later Pacific canoes and from linguistics, while the firm evidence is pottery with toothed-stamp decoration, shell tools and settlement sites. It would need its own dossier.
- **Amazonia.** "People in south-west Amazonia were cultivating squash and manioc more than 10,000 years ago and building thousands of artificial forest islands in the savanna." Source: Lombardo, U. et al. (2020). Early Holocene crop cultivation and landscape modification in Amazonia. *Nature* 581: 190–193. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2162-7 [V abstract]. **The famous earthwork settlements are too late for the picture:** the Casarabe culture of the Bolivian Amazon dates to "around AD 500 to AD 1400" [V, Prümers et al. 2022, *Nature* 606: 325–328, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04780-4], and the platform-and-road centers of Ecuador's Upano valley begin "around 500 BCE", just before the picture ends, and run to AD 300–600 [V, Rostain et al. 2024, *Science* 383: 183–189, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adi6317].
- **Jōmon Japan.** "In Japan, Jōmon communities of hunters, fishers and gatherers lived in settled villages, built stone circles and earthworks, and made some of the world's oldest pottery, from about 13,000 BC to 400 BC, without farming." Source: UNESCO, Jomon Prehistoric Sites in Northern Japan, https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1632 [V: "sedentary hunter-fisher-gatherer society ... from about 13,000 BCE to 400 BCE"]; Craig, O.E. et al. (2013). Earliest evidence for the use of pottery. *Nature* 496: 351–354. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12109 [V abstract: pottery from about 15,000 years ago, used for cooking fish].
- **Other omissions a careful reader would notice first** (my selection):
  - *Stonehenge and the megaliths of Atlantic Europe*: monument building "between circa 3700 and 1600 BC" [V, UNESCO, https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/373]. A British or Irish reader will look for it at once.
  - *Liangzhu*, on the lower Yangtze: "an early regional state with rice-cultivating agriculture", its city built "ca. 3300–2300 BCE" with large dams [V, UNESCO, https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1592]. It is a thousand years older than Anyang and means the picture's "Early China" is neither the earliest nor the rice-growing south.
  - *The early Maya*: a very large ceremonial platform at Aguada Fénix, Tabasco, of about 1000–800 BC (Inomata et al. 2020, *Nature* 582: 530–533, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2343-4 [record verified; dates as generally reported, abstract not read: R]). It bears on the La Venta vignette's "mother culture" caution.
  - *The first peopling of Australia and New Guinea* (vignette 4b): one sentence, "at least 50,000 and possibly 65,000 years ago".
  - Still unsourced from the first dossier's list: the Horn of Africa, Elam and Persia, the Hittites, Bronze Age Crete and Mycenae, the Central Asian oasis cities, the Andes after Caral (Chavín), South-East Asia.
