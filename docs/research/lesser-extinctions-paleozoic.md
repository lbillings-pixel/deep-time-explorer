## 1. Sinsk event (early Cambrian; often loosely called the "end-Botomian extinction")

### Date
- **About 513.5 Ma**, early in Cambrian Stage 4 (Murphy et al. 2025, abstract: "approx. 513.5 million years ago"). Zhuravlev & Wood (2020, Scientific Reports 10:6784) give "ca. 513 Ma" for the onset of the anoxia.
- **Radiometric bracket:** Myrow et al. (2024) place it in the *Pararaia janeae* trilobite Zone and bracket it between 514.45 ± 0.36 Ma (upper Stage 3, Britain) and 511.87 ± 0.14 Ma (tuff in the Billy Creek Formation, Flinders Ranges, South Australia); they note it could be "a million years or more younger" than the older date. So "514 to 512 Ma" is the honest range.
- **Duration:** unknown. Murphy et al. (2025) state that the duration of the anoxic interval is unknown. The wider "Botoman-Toyonian extinction" interval is given as ca. 513-508 Ma (Zhuravlev & Wood 2020).
- **Naming problem.** The Botoman (Botomian) is a Siberian regional stage, not an international one. Zhuravlev & Wood (1996) placed the Sinsk event in the *early* Botomian (their abstract), and a second, separate pulse (the Hawke Bay regression) in the early Toyonian. "End-Botomian" is therefore a loose label; the Sinsk event is not at the end of the Botoman. Base of international Stage 4 is given as about 514.5 Ma on the ICS chart but the boundary is not yet formally defined (this I could confirm only from secondary summaries of the chart, check the chart itself).
- The commonly quoted figure of about 513 Ma is right.

Crossref-verified (Crossref lists first page 311 only; end page 314 from library listings). **The paper that named the event.**

- Supporting, for the volcanic link: Jourdan, F. et al. (2014). High-precision dating of the Kalkarindji large igneous province, Australia, and synchrony with the Early-Middle Cambrian (Stage 4-5) extinction. *Geology* 42(6): 543-546. doi:10.1130/G35434.1. Crossref-verified.

### What died
- **Archaeocyaths** (cup-shaped calcified sponges, the first animal reef builders) were nearly wiped out: "only 8 species remaining by the Toyonian" (Zhuravlev & Wood 2020). They did not finally disappear at the event; Murphy et al. (2025) say archaeocyaths, coralomorphs, halkieriids and hyolithelminths were "decimated" at the Sinsk but lingered to about 510 Ma. Only one group (the tommotiids) is lost in the immediate aftermath.
- Also hit: hyoliths, helcionelloid molluscs, many "small shelly fossil" groups and some trilobites. Brachiopods and trilobites as a whole came through and then radiated (Myrow et al. 2024; Murphy et al. 2025).
- **Numbers (they disagree, and measure different things):**
  - "Over 60% of metazoan species were lost globally" (Murphy et al. 2025, introduction, citing earlier work, not their own data).
  - "Global genus-level reduction in diversity of ~45%" (Myrow et al. 2024).
  - On the Siberian Platform, skeletal species counted fall from a peak of 312 (about 514 Ma) to 127 at the Sinsk (Murphy et al. 2025). The authors say these counts are uncorrected for sampling and may be inflated by over-splitting of species.
  - The 1996 paper's own genus figures: I could not read the full text, so no number is quoted from it.
- Diversity did not regain pre-Sinsk levels until the Early Ordovician (Murphy et al. 2025).

### Proposed causes
- **Broad agreement:** shallow-water anoxia. Black, laminated, unburrowed, pyrite-rich shales and limestones spread across tropical shelves at the event level (Zhuravlev & Wood 1996; the Sinsk Formation and equivalents cover more than 750,000 km2 of the Siberian Platform, Murphy et al. 2025).
- **Debated: what caused the anoxia.**
  - Eutrophication and plankton blooms (Zhuravlev & Wood 1996).
  - Warming from the Kalkarindji large igneous province of northern Australia. The timing is contested inside the same research group: Murphy et al. (2025) say emplacement "probably occurred at approximately 514-513 Ma"; Zhuravlev & Wood (2020) gave 508-498 Ma and said it post-dated the size changes; Jourdan et al. (2014) tie the main phase (about 510-511 Ma) to the later Stage 4-5 extinction, not to the Sinsk. Treat a Kalkarindji-Sinsk link as unproven.
  - Tectonic trigger: mountain building along the Gondwana margin (Ross and Delamerian orogens) drowning carbonate platforms and setting off the chain (Myrow et al. 2024). The authors call this a hypothesis to be tested by better dating.
- A second pulse, the **Hawke Bay regression** (early Toyonian sea-level fall), removed more reef habitat (Zhuravlev & Wood 1996).

### How disputed is it as an extinction event?
- That archaeocyath reefs collapsed is not disputed. What is disputed is whether it was one sharp global event. Sundberg et al. (2020, *Geology* 48(5): 441-445, doi:10.1130/G46913.1, Crossref-verified) show the later trilobite extinctions near the early-middle Cambrian transition were asynchronous between continents, and archaeocyaths persisted locally (for example in Laurentia) after the reef collapse. The species-loss figures come largely from one region (Siberia) and are uncorrected for sampling (Murphy et al. 2025 say so themselves). The label "first Phanerozoic mass extinction" is the Edinburgh group's phrase; the event is not one of the Big Five.

### Key localities
- **Siberian Platform, middle Lena River, Yakutia (Russia):** Sinsk Formation (black organic-rich shales and limestones, about 60 m thick), resting on the Perekhod Formation; the older Pestrotsvet Formation below holds the archaeocyath reef record (Murphy et al. 2025 use all three formations). Note: Sinsk rests on Perekhod, not directly on Pestrotsvet.
- **Central Transantarctic Mountains, Antarctica:** Shackleton Limestone (archaeocyath-microbial reefs up to 38 m thick) capped by a phosphatic hardground, then Holyoake Formation (dark shale and nodular carbonate, 5-10 m), Starshot Formation (siltstone to pebbly sandstone) and Douglas Conglomerate (river and fan gravels with boulders of the reef limestone) (Myrow et al. 2024).
- **South Australia:** Kangaroo Island (White Point Conglomerate, Marsden Sandstone, Emu Bay Shale) and the Flinders Ranges (Billy Creek Formation tuff, 511.87 ± 0.14 Ma) (Myrow et al. 2024).

### Facts a painter could use
- **Before:** a warm, shallow tropical sea floor of low reef mounds built by archaeocyaths (centimetre-scale porous cups and vases, often double-walled) bound together by crusts of calcified microbes; trilobites, hyoliths (small cone-shaped shells), and spiny small shelly animals on the bottom. No fish, no corals in the modern sense, nothing on land.
- **During/after:** the same shelf under dark, still, oxygen-poor water. The rock record is black, paper-thin laminated shale and limestone with no burrows. In Antarctica the dead reef is literally capped by a dark phosphate crust and buried by black mud, then by sand and gravel shed from rising mountains, including boulders of the old reef.
- **Colour contrast on the Lena River:** the older reef-bearing Pestrotsvet Formation is "variegated" (the name means multicoloured; burgundy-red clay-rich limestones are described from it), against the black Sinsk Formation above. (Colour of Pestrotsvet from a single 2023 search result; check before relying on it.)

## 2. Late Cambrian trilobite "biomere" extinctions and the SPICE (candidate only)

### Date
There are three extinctions, not one, and they are spread over roughly 10 million years:

| Extinction (North American name) | Position | Age |
|---|---|---|
| End-Marjumiid (Marjuman-Steptoean), base of Pterocephaliid biomere | at or just below the Guzhangian-Paibian (Miaolingian-Furongian) boundary; coincides with onset of SPICE | 497 Ma in GTS2020 / ICS chart; **494.5 +0.7/-0.6 Ma** in Farrell et al. (2025) |
| End-Pterocephaliid (Steptoean-Sunwaptan) | early Jiangshanian | roughly 2.5-3 Myr after the first (no direct date found) |
| End-Ptychaspid (end-Sunwaptan) | just below the Cambrian-Ordovician boundary | boundary 486.85 ± 1.5 Ma on recent ICS charts (check chart); Furongian ends 487.3 ± 0.08 Ma in Farrell et al. (2025) |

- **SPICE duration:** 2.6 +0.9/-0.8 Myr (Farrell et al. 2025), shorter than older estimates of 3-4 Myr. SPICE is a positive carbon-isotope excursion of about +4 to +5 per mil (Saltzman et al. 2000).
- **Sources disagree on the absolute age by about 2.5 Myr.** The 497 Ma figure (GTS2020, ICS chart) rests on few dates; Cothren et al. (2022) and Farrell et al. (2025) used new U-Pb zircon ages from the western USA and put the base of the Furongian near 494.5 Ma. Your "497-486 Ma" spans the whole set correctly; just do not present it as one event.
- Cuggy & Westrop note trilobite extinctions recurred about every 5 Myr in the Late Cambrian of North America (conference abstract, 1996).

### What died
- **Trilobites, almost exclusively**, on the shallow shelves of Laurentia (ancient North America). Each "biomere" is a package of shelf trilobites that diversified and then was abruptly replaced by a low-diversity fauna that came in from deeper, cooler water off the shelf.
- **Numbers:** the end-Sunwaptan (end-Ptychaspid) extinction "eliminated about half of North American trilobite families" (Westrop & Ludvigsen 1987, abstract). I found **no published percent-of-genera figure** for the end-Marjumiid extinction in anything I could read this session. Do not quote one without checking Palmer (1984) or Westrop & Cuggy (1999, *Journal of Paleontology* 73(2): 337-354, doi:10.1017/S0022336000027815, Crossref-verified, not read).
- The end-Marjumiid extinction came in two steps: first most shallow-water trilobites of the *Crepicephalus* Zone vanish, then a deeper-water *Aphelaspis* Zone fauna moves in (Schiffbauer et al. 2017; Gerhardt & Gill 2016). The onset of SPICE lines up with the second step, not the first (Gerhardt & Gill 2016).
- Brachiopods and conodonts are reported to be affected at some boundaries (from memory, check).

### Proposed causes
- **No consensus.** Candidates:
  - Cold and/or oxygen-poor deep water spreading onto the shelf during sea-level rise (Palmer's original idea was cooling; later versions stress anoxia).
  - Widespread euxinia (anoxic, sulphidic water): Gill et al. (2011) found a sulphur-isotope excursion in parallel with SPICE, implying massive burial of organic carbon and pyrite.
  - Immigration and biogeographic replacement without a shelf-wide physical kill: Westrop & Ludvigsen (1987) argue the end-Sunwaptan extinction "cannot be attributed to a shelfwide physical environmental perturbation" such as cooling or anoxia.
- An iridium search at two biomere boundaries in Utah found no anomaly, so impact is not supported (Orth et al. 1984, *Science* 223(4632): 163-165, doi:10.1126/science.223.4632.163, Crossref-verified; result from memory of the title and abstract, check).

### How disputed is it as an extinction event?
- **Heavily qualified.** These are real, repeated turnovers of shelf trilobites, best documented in North America; they are not a single event and are not established as global mass extinctions. Schiffbauer et al. (2017) argue SPICE is "time-transgressive and facies-dependent" and "decoupled" from a possibly diachronous Marjumiid-Pterocephaliid crisis, so the isotope excursion should not be treated as the cause, or even a precise time marker, of the extinction. Westrop & Ludvigsen (1987) read at least one of them as faunal replacement driven by geography. Keeping this as "candidate only" is the right call.

### Key localities
- **Great Basin, western USA** (House Range and other ranges of western Utah and Nevada, e.g. Shingle Pass, Nevada; Smithfield Canyon, Utah): Palmer's type area; grey shelf limestones.
- **Southern Appalachians:** Conasauga Group, including the Nolichucky Formation (Tennessee, Virginia) (Gerhardt & Gill 2016; Schiffbauer et al. 2017).
- **Deadwood Formation, Black Hills, South Dakota;** central Missouri drill cores (Schiffbauer et al. 2017).
- **Wilberns Formation, central Texas** (Ptychaspid biomere: Longacre 1970, *Journal of Paleontology* 44(S4): 1-61, Crossref-verified) and Oklahoma.
- Global SPICE reference sections include South China (Paibi, Hunan, the stage stratotype), Kazakhstan and Queensland (Mt Whelan) (Saltzman et al. 2000).

### Facts a painter could use
- **Setting:** a vast, shallow, warm carbonate sea covering most of North America (then lying across the equator and rotated), the "Great American Carbonate Bank": pale lime mud and sand shoals, microbial mounds and stromatolites instead of coral reefs, bare land with no plants.
- **Before:** a varied crowd of small shelf trilobites (many endemic families), with inarticulate brachiopods and eocrinoids. **After:** a sparse, monotonous fauna of a few wide-ranging, thin-shelled trilobite types that came up from the dark outer shelf and slope (the *Aphelaspis* fauna after the first extinction; olenid-like forms are the classic low-oxygen specialists).
- **Rock colours:** pale to mid-grey bedded limestone on the shelf; dark grey to black shale and lime mudstone in the deeper-water tongues that carry the replacement faunas. The event is a change of water mass over the same sea floor, not a visible catastrophe. (This bullet and the two above are general knowledge of the setting, from memory; check against Palmer 1965 or Westrop & Cuggy 1999.)

## 3. Lau event / Kozlowskii event (late Silurian, mid-Ludfordian)

### Date
- **About 424 Ma.** Two U-Pb zircon dates from ash beds in Podolia, Ukraine, bracket the Lau carbon-isotope excursion: 424.08 ± 0.20 Ma below and 422.91 ± 0.07 Ma above (Cramer et al. 2015, abstract). The gap between them is about 1.2 Myr (my subtraction, not a figure stated in the abstract), so the excursion lasted no longer than that.
- The extinctions began slightly *before* the isotope excursion, in the latest *Polygnathoides siluricus* conodont zone (Bowman et al. 2019). The fish turnover is put at roughly 200,000 years (attributed to Eriksson et al. 2009 in a search excerpt of the abstract; check).
- **Stage ages differ between chart versions:** Ludfordian Stage is 425.0 ± 1.5 to 422.7 ± 1.6 Ma on the ICS chart v2024/12 (ICS change log, read this session); before that update it was 425.6 ± 0.9 to 423.0 ± 2.3 Ma.
- **Wikipedia's "420" is out of date.** It reflects older time scales (a 2005 comment on Calner's paper also says "around 420 Ma"). A figure of about 424 Ma is right.
- A new paper, Cramer et al. (2026), "Quantifying uncertainty in the structure and duration of Silurian biogeochemical events", *Scientific Reports* 16: 37462, doi:10.1038/s41598-026-63884-3, exists (Crossref-verified) and may revise the duration; I could not read it (publisher page required sign-in). Worth checking.

### What died
- **Conodonts** (eel-like early vertebrates known from tooth-like elements): severely hit; "no platform-equipped taxon survived" (Calner & Eriksson 2006, *Geological Magazine* 143(1): 15-24, doi:10.1017/S001675680500169X, Crossref-verified, citing Jeppsson & Aldridge 2000). Afterwards the fauna is dominated by a single species, *Panderodus equicostatus* (Younes et al. 2017; "up to 95%" is from a 2024 conference abstract only).
- **Graptolites** in deeper water: the Kozlowskii event (Urbanek 1993; Koren' 1993, as cited by Bowman et al. 2019).
- **Fish:** "two-thirds of the fish taxa" lost on Gotland; jawed acanthodians dominated before, jawless thelodonts briefly dominated after (Eriksson et al. 2009, abstract excerpts).
- Also brachiopods and acritarchs (organic-walled plankton) (Bowman et al. 2019, citing Talent et al. 1993 and Stricanne et al. 2006).
- **Overall number:** "~23% loss of genera", "at least the tenth largest extinction event in Earth history" (Bowman et al. 2019, citing Bond & Grasby 2017, *Palaeogeography, Palaeoclimatology, Palaeoecology* 478: 3-29, doi:10.1016/j.palaeo.2016.11.005, Crossref-verified; I did not trace where Bond & Grasby got the figure). A press release for the same paper says "nearly 25% of marine species", which mixes up genera and species; do not use it.
- **Order of losses:** bottom-dwellers and swimmers first, plankton later (Bowman et al. 2019).

### Proposed causes
- **No consensus on the trigger; reasonable agreement on the sequence.** Oxygen-poor water spread first (thallium isotopes, Bowman et al. 2019), the extinctions began, and only then came one of the largest positive carbon-isotope excursions of the last 540 million years (peak 9.01 per mil on Gotland, Younes et al. 2017), with a parallel sulphur-isotope excursion pointing to sulphidic (euxinic) water.
- **Cooling and glaciation:** oxygen isotopes in conodont apatite rise by up to 3.8 per mil and sea level fell on all palaeocontinents; Frýda et al. (2021) name this the "Mid-Ludfordian Glaciation" on polar Gondwana. Debated: no direct glacial deposits are cited in the abstract.
- **Jeppsson's oceanic model** (switching between two ocean circulation states) was the original explanation and is now less used.

### How disputed is it as an extinction event?
- The turnover is real and seen on several continents, but it is modest in overall size. Calner (2005) himself calls it a "minor" biotic event (his quotation marks) whose ecological footprint looks out of proportion to its taxonomic size. Stanley (2016) left Silurian crises out of his magnitude estimates because they "remain to be studied in detail". The 23% figure is second-hand. A published comment on Calner (2005) by R. Riding argued that the flat-pebble conglomerates reflect seawater chemistry more than loss of animals (citation details UNVERIFIED; seen only as a search excerpt). A "Discussion" of Jeppsson & Aldridge by D. K. Loydell appeared in *Journal of the Geological Society* 158(4): 731-732 (2001), doi:10.1144/jgs.158.4.731 (Crossref-verified, not read).

### Key localities
- **Gotland, Sweden** (type area; named after Lau parish): uppermost Hemse Group, Eke Formation, Burgsvik Formation.
- **Prague Basin, Czech Republic:** Kosov section, "one of the most complete and best studied" (search excerpt of a follow-up study); Kopanina Formation (formation name from memory, check).
- **Latvia** (Priekule drill core; core name from memory) and other East Baltic cores; **Podolia, Ukraine** (dated ash beds); **Carnic Alps, Austria** (Cellon section); **southern Laurentia** (Bowman et al. 2020, *Palaeogeography, Palaeoclimatology, Palaeoecology* 553: 109799, doi:10.1016/j.palaeo.2020.109799).

### Facts a painter could use
- **Setting:** a warm, shallow tropical sea on the southern edge of the continent Baltica (Bowman et al. 2019). Land nearby was bare rock with at most ankle-high early plants at the water's edge (general knowledge).
- **Before:** reefs of stromatoporoid sponges and tabulate corals with sea lilies (general knowledge of the Gotland Silurian, check); small spiny jawed fish (acanthodians) dominant; graptolite colonies drifting offshore.
- **After:** the sea floor goes "back in time". Calner's "anachronistic" facies: microbial mats and wrinkled sand surfaces, stromatolites, beds of oncoids (marble- to golf-ball-sized grains coated by microbes), and flat-pebble conglomerates (torn-up plates of lime mud), signs that grazers and burrowers had thinned out. Flat, scale-covered jawless thelodonts replace the acanthodians for a time. Sea level drops and pale quartz sand and oolite (Burgsvik Formation) spread over the limestone platform.

## 4. Serpukhovian extinction (mid-Carboniferous)

### Date
- **Serpukhovian Stage: 330.3 ± 0.4 to 323.4 ± 0.4 Ma** (ICS chart v2024/12, change log read this session; before that update 330.9 ± 0.3 to 323.2 ± 0.4 Ma). Your 330-323 Ma is the stage, correctly.
- **Where in the stage the extinction falls is itself disputed.** One school puts it early in the Serpukhovian, at the onset of major glaciation (Stanley 2016 speaks of the "early Serpukhovian" event; Balseiro & Powell 2020). Another puts it at the end, at the mid-Carboniferous (Mississippian-Pennsylvanian) boundary, 323.4 Ma, with the big sea-level fall (Saunders & Ramsbottom 1986). No source I read gives a duration.

### Key sources
1. Saunders, W. B. & Ramsbottom, W. H. C. (1986). The mid-Carboniferous eustatic event. *Geology* 14(3): 208-212. doi:10.1130/0091-7613(1986)14<208:TMEE>2.0.CO;2. Crossref-verified (first page 208; end page from memory, check). **Early recognition of the boundary event.**
2. Stanley, S. M. & Powell, M. G. (2003). Depressed rates of origination and extinction during the late Paleozoic ice age: A new state for the global marine ecosystem. *Geology* 31(10): 877-880. doi:10.1130/G19654R.1. Crossref-verified (first page 877; end page from memory, check; not read).
3. McGhee, G. R., Sheehan, P. M., Bottjer, D. J. & Droser, M. L. (2012). Carboniferous-earliest Permian marine biodiversification event (CPBE) during the Late Paleozoic Ice Age. *Earth-Science Reviews* 220: 103699. doi:10.1016/j.earscirev.2021.103699. ### What died
- Marine animals of tropical shelf seas: brachiopods (larger-bodied genera selectively, Powell 2008, *Palaios* 23(8): 525-534, doi:10.2110/palo.2007.p07-038r, Crossref-verified, as summarised by Monarrez & Woods), crinoids and rugose corals (restructured rather than wiped out, Monarrez & Woods 2026), ammonoids and conodonts (Saunders & Ramsbottom 1986; from memory, check).
- **Numbers disagree by a factor of three:**
  - 26-28% of marine genera, "sixth-largest of the Phanerozoic" (Sepkoski 1986, 1996 and McGhee et al. 2013, as cited by Monarrez & Woods 2026).
  - 13-15% of genera, "of relatively small magnitude" (Stanley 2016).
  - Higher figures (31%, 39%) circulate online attributed to Bambach (2006) and McGhee et al. (2013). UNVERIFIED: I saw them only in secondary summaries.
  - At the boundary type section (Arrow Canyon, Nevada) 39% of Serpukhovian genera are missing from the overlying Bashkirian, but all but two survive elsewhere, a true extinction of about 6% (Monarrez & Woods 2026).
- **Ecological ranking:** fifth most severe ecologically, above the end-Ordovician (McGhee et al. 2012), revised to sixth (McGhee et al. 2013), per Monarrez & Woods (2026).
- The more durable claim: afterwards, both origination and extinction stayed unusually low for the whole ice age, a "new state" for marine life (Stanley & Powell 2003, title and standard reading; not re-read).

### Proposed causes
- **Broad agreement that it is tied to the start of the main late Paleozoic ice age:** ice growth on Gondwana, cooling of the tropics, and repeated falls of sea level that drained the shallow seas (Saunders & Ramsbottom 1986; Stanley & Powell 2003).
- **Debated mechanisms:** loss of carbonate-platform habitat ("carbonate collapse", Balseiro & Powell 2020); a 2025 conference abstract argues instead for an interglacial warming pulse and anoxia (not peer-reviewed, not cited here).

### How disputed is it as an extinction event?
- **Strongly disputed.** Stanley (2016) halves the size. Shi et al. (2021) see the same interval (mid-Visean to earliest Permian, 41.2 Myr) as a long *diversification* in which species numbers rose 246%, the opposite reading. Monarrez & Woods (2026) find the extinction "is not expressed" at the boundary's own global reference section and conclude support for a Serpukhovian mass extinction "is weaker than previously suggested"; they say sections outside North America are needed. Earlier regional studies (Illinois Basin, Ozarks) also found local disappearance rather than true extinction (Bonelli & Patzkowsky 2008, 2011; Heim 2009, as cited by Monarrez & Woods; not verified individually).

### Key localities
- **Arrow Canyon, Nevada, USA:** global reference section (GSSP) for the base of the Pennsylvanian; Indian Springs Formation below, Bird Spring Formation above; about 75 km north-east of Las Vegas (Monarrez & Woods 2026).
- **Central Appalachian Basin** (Powell 2008); **Illinois Basin and Ozarks** (Chesterian limestones and shales).
- **Moscow Basin, Russia** (type area of the stage, near Serpukhov) and **Naqing section, Guizhou, South China** (deep-water conodont reference section; Qi et al. 2014, *Geological Magazine* 151(2): 254-268, doi:10.1017/S001675681300071X, Crossref-verified).

### Facts a painter could use
- **Before:** broad, warm, shallow limestone seas, the tail end of the "Age of Crinoids": meadows of sea lilies, horn corals, spiny productid brachiopods, lacy and corkscrew bryozoans, coiled goniatite ammonoids (general knowledge, from memory).
- **During:** an ice sheet growing on Gondwana at the far south while the tropics stayed green. Sea level began pumping up and down by tens of metres; shelves were repeatedly left dry. In the rocks this shows as clean grey marine limestone interrupted by silty, sandy beds washed off the land (the Indian Springs to Bird Spring change at Arrow Canyon is exactly this: an influx of terrigenous silt).
- **On land at the same time:** the first great coal-swamp forests of scale trees were spreading. No flowers, no grass; giant arthropods.

## 5. Carboniferous rainforest collapse (Kasimovian)

### Date
- **About 307 to 305 Ma.** Falcon-Lang et al. (2018): first phase "near the Desmoinesian-Missourian boundary (early Kasimovian, ~307 Ma)". Sahney et al. (2010): "earliest Kasimovian time (ca. 305 Ma)". Dunne et al. (2018): Kasimovian "approx. 303-307 Ma".
- Kasimovian Stage: 307.0 ± 0.1 to 303.7 ± 0.1 Ma (ICS chart v2024/12, unchanged from earlier charts).
- **Duration:** not a moment. Falcon-Lang et al. (2018) call it a "stepwise collapse"; Dunne et al. (2018) describe a gradual shift from wetlands to drylands with the tetrapod decline beginning in the Gzhelian (after 303.7 Ma).

Crossref-verified (first page; end page from memory). **These established the vegetation change.**

### What died
- **Plants:** the giant lycopsid "scale trees" (the *Lycospora*-producing lepidodendrids) that had dominated tropical peat swamps for more than nine million years vanish from the western (American) coal basins; tree ferns take over the swamps (Falcon-Lang et al. 2018; DiMichele & Phillips, search excerpt).
- **Numbers:** roughly 67% of species in peat-forming mires eliminated, and at least half the species of clastic (muddy) wetlands (DiMichele & Phillips 1996, abstract as reproduced on a catalogue page). A later paper cites the same source for "87% of peatland tree species". I could not reconcile the two; both need checking against the 1996 paper.
- **Animals (contested):** Sahney et al. (2010) reported that tetrapod extinction rate "peaked", local diversity "imploded" and endemism appeared, with amphibians hit hardest and amniotes (early reptiles and mammal-ancestors) doing better. Dunne et al. (2018) dispute each of these (below).

### Proposed causes
- **Consensus:** climate change in the tropics, a shift to drier and more seasonal conditions, linked to the glacial cycles of the late Paleozoic ice age.
- **Debated:** the exact driver. Falcon-Lang et al. (2018) point to an intensification of glacial-cycle amplitude, with extremes of both warming and cooling. Changes in atmospheric CO2 are also invoked (Montañez et al. 2016, *Nature Geoscience* 9(11): 824-828, doi:10.1038/ngeo2822, Crossref-verified, not read).

### How disputed is it as an extinction event?
- **The plant turnover in Euramerica is well documented; calling it a global "collapse" or a mass extinction is disputed.** It was regional and stepwise: swamps persisted in European mountain basins and parts of central North America, and the coal-forest biome shifted east to China, where it lasted into the Permian (Dunne et al. 2018). Dunne et al. (2018) found that the tetrapod pattern of Sahney et al. was largely a sampling effect: corrected diversity rose into the late Carboniferous and fell later, across the Carboniferous-Permian boundary; their analysis "does not support the hypothesis that the CRC drove endemism". On the other side, Cascales-Miñana & Cleal (2014) count it as one of only two mass extinctions in the plant fossil record (as cited by Dunne et al.; citation not verified individually).

### Key localities
- **Illinois Basin, USA:** coal-ball floras; the silicified dryland forest in Peoria County directly beneath the Exline Limestone and Athensville Coal, the level of the US-wide loss of *Lycospora* (Falcon-Lang et al. 2018).
- **Appalachian Basin** and the Western Interior (Kansas) cyclothems.
- Tetrapod sites on either side (from memory, check): Linton, Ohio and Nýřany, Czech Republic (before); Garnett and Hamilton, Kansas (after).

### Facts a painter could use
- **Before:** an equatorial peat swamp on the supercontinent Pangaea. Pole-like scale trees 30 m or more tall with diamond-patterned bark and small crowns, giant horsetails, seed ferns, standing black water; large amphibians in the pools; giant millipede relatives and dragonfly-like insects (general knowledge, from memory). Rocks: black coal seams between grey shales and pale seat-earths.
- **After:** the swamps shrink to patches dominated by tree ferns (a shaggy trunk of roots with a crown of huge fronds). The lowlands between are seasonally dry and carry open woodland of early conifers and cordaitaleans: Falcon-Lang et al. (2018) describe a forest of silicified coniferopsid stumps that recolonised the lowland after the collapse.
- **Animals:** small, lizard-like early amniotes that lay eggs on land become more prominent beside the big amphibians. Whether this was caused by the collapse is exactly what is disputed.

## 6. Capitanian / end-Guadalupian extinction (Middle Permian)

### Date
There are three "dates" in the literature and they are not the same event level:
- **Marine, South China: mid-Capitanian, roughly 262 Ma.** Bond et al. (2010) place the marine losses "in the middle of the Capitanian Stage", below the stage's top; "most marine extinctions occurred before 261.6 ± 1.6 Ma" (Day & Rubidge 2021).
- **Land, South Africa: 260.26 Ma.** A U-Pb date of 260.259 ± 0.081 Ma from the base of the Teekloof Formation ties the main tetrapod extinction "to around 260.26 Ma" (Day et al. 2015; Day & Rubidge 2021).
- **"End-Guadalupian": 259.51 ± 0.21 Ma**, the Guadalupian-Lopingian boundary on the current ICS chart (set in v2021/07; earlier charts gave 259.8 ± 0.4 Ma in 2013 and 259.1 ± 0.5 Ma in 2016). Rampino & Shen's "259.8 Ma" is the 2013 boundary age.
- Capitanian Stage: 264.28 ± 0.16 to 259.51 ± 0.21 Ma (ICS).
- **Emeishan volcanism:** about 260 Ma at onset (Wignall et al. 2009); a felsic ignimbrite at the top of the lava pile gives 259.1 ± 0.5 Ma for the end (Zhong et al. 2014, figure seen in secondary sources); later dating stretches waning activity to about 257.8 Ma.
- "About 260 Ma" is fine as a round figure. Whether the marine and land crises were one event or two a million or more years apart is unresolved.

Crossref-verified. **Established it as separate from the end-Permian** (with Jin, Zhang & Shang 1994, a conference-volume chapter I could not verify: UNVERIFIED).

### What died
- **Sea:** the large, complex fusulinacean foraminifera (single-celled animals with rice-grain to coin-sized shells) and calcareous algae, at the onset of the eruptions (Wignall et al. 2009); also brachiopods and other reef and shelf invertebrates (Bond et al. 2010).
- **Land animals:** in the Karoo, "an estimated 74-80% loss of tetrapod generic richness"; "the only taxa that became completely extinct were dinocephalians and bradysaurian pareiasaurs" (Day & Rubidge 2021). Dinocephalians were the big-bodied therapsids (mammal relatives) that dominated Middle Permian land faunas.
- **Plants:** 24% of plant species lost in South China; 56% in North China within the Capitanian (Bond et al. 2010, abstract).
- **Marine numbers disagree:**
  - 58% of marine invertebrate genera in the Capitanian (older estimates, the figure Clapham et al. 2009 set out to test).
  - 33-35% of genera (Stanley 2016).
  - No distinct peak at all: a "prolonged but gradual decrease in diversity from the Wordian to the end of the Permian" (Clapham et al. 2009). An earlier conference abstract by the same authors gave 34% of Capitanian genera extinct, not significantly different from the stages before and after.
  - Figures attributed in press coverage to Rampino & Shen (2021) could not be checked. UNVERIFIED, not quoted.
- Note a dating shift: in 2010 the dinocephalian extinction was thought to be older, in the Wordian (Bond et al. 2010, abstract); the 2015 Karoo dates moved it to the late Capitanian.

### Proposed causes
- **Leading suspect, widely accepted: the Emeishan Traps** of south-west China. In the same rock sections the first eruptions, which were explosive because lava met sea water, coincide with the fusulinacean and algal extinctions (Wignall et al. 2009).
- **Debated: the kill mechanism.** Cooling (the "Kamura event"), anoxia, loss of shelf habitat as sea level fell, and changes in provinciality are all proposed (Clapham et al. 2009 list cooling and anoxia). A major negative carbon-isotope excursion comes *after* the extinction, not with it (Wignall et al. 2009).
- **On land the cause is open:** "The causes of the Capitanian mass extinction of vertebrates remain unclear"; a link to Emeishan is by timing only (Day & Rubidge 2021).

### How disputed is it as an extinction event?
- **Seriously disputed in the sea, well supported on land.** Clapham et al. (2009) argue the marine "extinction" is a gradual decline driven by a sharp drop in origination, which "precludes the need to invoke drastic extinction mechanisms". Stanley (2016) and Rampino & Shen (2021) hold that it was a real, large event; Rampino & Shen propose it as a sixth major mass extinction. The Karoo tetrapod extinction is abrupt and radiometrically dated, though Day & Rubidge (2021) caution that part of the dinocephalian gap may be a sampling effect and the extinctions look phased.

### Key localities
- **South China:** Maokou Formation limestones directly beneath and interbedded with Emeishan basalts in Yunnan, Guizhou and Sichuan (Wignall et al. 2009); the Penglaitan and Tieqiao sections at Laibin, Guangxi, the global reference for the Guadalupian-Lopingian boundary (section names from memory, check).
- **Karoo Basin, South Africa:** top of the *Tapinocephalus* Assemblage Zone, Abrahamskraal Formation, to the Poortjie Member at the base of the Teekloof Formation; Puntkraal and Vanvuurenskop sections (Day & Rubidge 2021).
- **Boreal sites:** Spitsbergen and Arctic Canada (Bond and colleagues; not verified this session).

### Facts a painter could use
- **Sea:** a tropical lime-mud platform (pale to dark grey limestone today) crowded with fusulinid shells and green algae, then an eruption through shallow water: steam-and-ash columns, black glassy fragments raining into the lagoon, and afterwards stacked dark basalt lava flows, now the mountains of the Emei Shan region.
- **Land before:** a broad river plain in what is now the Karoo, with herds of heavy, barrel-bodied, thick-skulled plant-eating dinocephalians (cow- to rhino-sized), large predatory dinocephalians, and squat, armoured pareiasaurs (*Bradysaurus*). Vegetation of the *Glossopteris* flora (tongue-shaped leaves; general knowledge).
- **Land after:** the giants are gone. For 20 to 90 m of rock the fauna is sparse, then the small burrowing dicynodont *Diictodon* (dachshund-sized, tusked, beaked) "overwhelmingly dominates", and *Endothiodon* is the first new arrival (Day & Rubidge 2021).

### What died
- The **Ediacara biota**: soft-bodied, mostly immobile organisms, including frond-shaped rangeomorphs and arboreomorphs, quilted mat-like dickinsoniomorphs, sack-like erniettomorphs, and the first animals with mineral skeletons (the stacked-cone tube *Cloudina* and the goblet-shaped *Namacalathus*).
- **Numbers:** about 80% of genera lost between the White Sea and Nama assemblages (Evans et al. 2022; figure from memory of the abstract and press coverage, check). I know of no agreed percentage for the second pulse at the boundary.
- At the boundary in Oman, *Cloudina* and *Namacalathus* disappear together with a sharp negative carbon-isotope excursion (Amthor et al. 2003; from memory).

### Proposed causes (none is consensus)
- **Environmental catastrophe:** loss of oxygen. Evans et al. (2022) argue the first pulse was not a sampling artefact and that survivors tended to have high surface-area-to-volume ratios, as expected if oxygen fell (from memory, check). For the boundary pulse, the carbon-isotope excursion is taken as the sign of a disturbance.
- **Biotic replacement:** newly evolved burrowing, grazing and predatory animals re-engineered the sea floor and displaced the Ediacara biota. Darroch et al. (2015) found the latest Ediacaran communities in Namibia much poorer than older ones, which they read as a drawn-out biotic squeeze (from memory, check).
- **"Cheshire Cat":** the organisms did not die out so abruptly; rather the special conditions that preserved soft bodies (microbial mats sealing the sand) disappeared, so the fossils fade out (Laflamme et al. 2013 set out all three).

### How disputed is it as an extinction event?
- **Very.** It is uncertain whether there was one event, two, or a long decline; the boundary itself is still being re-dated; and some Ediacara-type forms and cloudinids are reported from Cambrian rocks, which blurs any sharp line (reports from memory; specific citations not verified this session). The 80% figure for the first pulse rests on a small number of fossil sites of very different environments and ages. Keeping it as "candidate only" is right. A named dissenting paper I can vouch for from this session: none; Darroch et al. (2023) is the place to find the current list of objections.

### Key localities
- **Nama Group, southern Namibia and north-west South Africa** (Farm Swartpunt and neighbours): the youngest Ediacaran communities and the dated ash beds (Linnemann et al. 2019; Nelson et al. 2022; Darroch et al. 2015).
- **White Sea coast, Russia, and Flinders Ranges (Ediacara Member), South Australia:** the richer, older "White Sea" assemblage.
- **Mistaken Point, Newfoundland:** the oldest, deep-water "Avalon" assemblage (from memory).
- **Ara Group, Oman** (subsurface): the *Cloudina*-*Namacalathus* extinction level (Amthor et al. 2003). **Southern Nevada / Death Valley region** (Wood Canyon Formation; from memory).

### Facts a painter could use
- **Before:** a shallow sandy sea floor carpeted by a leathery, wrinkled microbial mat ("elephant-skin" texture in the rock). On it lie flat, ribbed, quilted discs and ovals up to dinner-plate size and more (*Dickinsonia*), and upright fronds held by discs. Nothing swims; nothing has eyes or legs; nothing burrows deeper than a few millimetres. No life on land.
- **Last communities (Nama):** fewer kinds; sack- or canoe-shaped erniettomorphs half-buried in sand; thickets and small reefs of finger-sized limestone tubes (*Cloudina*) on microbial mounds.
- **After:** the mats are broken up by burrows; the first complex, branching trace fossils (*Treptichnus pedum*) define the base of the Cambrian. In Namibia the rocks are tan to reddish sandstones and blue-grey limestones in desert outcrop (colour from memory, check photographs).

## Cross-cutting notes for the editor

- **Things that surprised me**
  - "End-Botomian" is a misnomer: the Sinsk event was placed in the *early* Botomian by the paper that named it.
  - A 2025 dating study moves the base of the Furongian (and the first biomere extinction and SPICE) from 497 to about 494.5 Ma; the ICS chart has not adopted it.
  - The Serpukhovian extinction is under direct attack in a paper published two weeks before this dossier (Monarrez & Woods, 27 October 2026): at the boundary's own reference section only about 6% of genera truly go extinct.
  - The Lau event's own describer (Calner 2005) calls it a "minor" biotic event with outsized ecological effects.
  - The dinocephalian extinction was thought to be Wordian until 2015; marine and land Capitanian crises may be over a million years apart.
  - ICS chart v2024/12 changed 44 numerical ages, including the Serpukhovian, Ludfordian and base-Ordovician ages used here.
